# Nest sentence formulas inside story formulas

**Her formula list already is a kernel system, so the decision in front of her is not whether to adopt kernels but how much of that structure students should see, and when.** The first part of every code (S1, S2, S3, S4, S5) names the same small set of basic English sentence types that Zellig Harris listed as kernels in 1957 (N V, N V N, N is A, N is N, N is P N), and the dotted letters after it (A, J, B, PRP, C) name the expansions. By my count of her 129-entry list, 71 entries are S1 family members, 14 are S2, and the negatives, questions, commands and exclamations reuse the same numbers (Sn3, Q1, C2, E3), which is exactly how Chomsky's 1957 grammar treated them: as changes made to a kernel. The evidence for teaching sentences as kernels plus expansions is indirect (sentence combining has a meta-analytic effect of 0.50 in grades 4 to 12; no study tests kernel routines in kindergarten), and usage-based language research backs her choice to start a kindergartner on four specific statement formulas: children learn particular patterns first and generalize later. I recommend a hybrid by level (Option C below), with specific anchored formulas at Pre-A and A, and kernel plus named moves with choice at B and C, but the choice is hers. The same idea scales up. Her Hallmark plot, her five-sentence quote paragraph and the five-paragraph essay are each a "kernel plus moves" at a bigger size, so sentence formulas can sit inside paragraph formulas, which sit inside composition formulas, each with a predictable visual template. The route from these explicit recipes to artistic writing is a taught ladder (notice, imitate, rebuild, combine, innovate, change one rule on purpose, invent, publish), and the research on creative examples shows why the last rungs matter: models pull new work toward their surface features unless students abstract the structure and then bend it. **No full-text source was opened for this report**; the session's network blocked journal, repository and social media hosts, so every claim comes from search summaries and is labeled that way.

## How to read the evidence in this report

Every research claim carries two labels, as in the earlier reports in this project. **The tier.** Tier 1 is a meta-analysis, systematic review or randomized trial. Tier 2 is quasi-experimental or single-case. Tier 3 is correlational, descriptive or theory. Tier 4 is practitioner voice: teacher blogs, program sites, vendors, journalism, author communities and screenwriting guides. **Tier 4 shows what is common and usable. It is never evidence that something works.** Vendor and program-authored sources are also labeled "vendor" or "program site." **The verification label** "search summary, not opened" means the claim comes from a search engine's summary, so wording and figures must be checked against the original before they go into curriculum text. Facebook groups, TikTok, Reddit and most YouTube channels could not be reached at all, so nothing is claimed about what is said there. "Data" means her own hand-coded corpus of 2,039 sentences from reading passages at grades 1, 3, 5, Intermediate and 10. Codes and example sentences marked "her list" come from her_formula_list.json. Example sentences and codes marked "my coding" are my own reading of her notation and should be run through the engine before use.

## Part 1. Kernel sentences, explained: her codes already carry them

### 1.1 "Kernel" began as linguistics and survived as a classroom word

The term has two lives. In linguistics, Zellig Harris (1957, *Language* 33) reduced texts to sequences of kernel sentences and reported that English kernels are a handful of structures, "chiefly NV and NVPN, NVN and NVNPN, N is N, N is A, N is PN," with transformations relating every other sentence to them ([Wikipedia: Zellig Harris](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Zellig_Harris); [New World Encyclopedia](https://www.newworldencyclopedia.org/entry/Zellig_Harris); Tier 3; search summary, not opened). Chomsky's *Syntactic Structures* (1957) built a grammar on a kernel of simple sentences from which others are derived by transformations such as the passive ([Syntactic Structures, Wikipedia](https://Www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntactic_Structures); [Britannica](https://britannica.com/topic/Syntactic-Structures); Tier 3; search summary, not opened). Chomsky's own definition could not be checked because the PDF host was blocked. By *Aspects of the Theory of Syntax* (1965) he had "quietly abandon[ed] the notion of 'kernel sentence'" in favor of deep structure ([Chronicle, Lingua Franca, 2015](https://www.chronicle.com/blogs/linguafranca/2015/06/21/revolutionary-methodological-preliminaries/); search summary, not opened).

Teachers kept the word. Paul Roberts's *Patterns of English* (1956), written for high school teachers, presented a few basic sentence patterns that combine and change in many ways ([HathiTrust record](https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/001902944); Tier 3; search summary, not opened). Martha Kolln's *Understanding English Grammar* lists ten basic patterns, grouped by verb type (be verbs, linking verbs, action verbs), and claims they cover most English statements but not questions or interjections ([Towson OWS: Sentence patterns](https://webapps.towson.edu/ows/sentpatt.htm); [Pearson listing](https://www.pearson.com/store/en-ca/p/understanding-english-grammar/P200000002253); [arXiv 2401.09448 citing Kolln & Funk](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.09448); Tier 3; search summary, not opened). Her *Rhetorical Grammar* is reported to use seven, so the count depends on the book ([UHV sentence patterns](https://www.uhv.edu/curriculum-student-achievement/student-success/student-resources/q-z/sentence-patterns); search summary, not opened). Kellogg Hunt coined the T-unit in 1965 (one main clause plus everything attached to it) and found T-units grow longer as children age ([T-unit, Wikipedia](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/T-unit); Tier 3; search summary, not opened). Sentence-combining researchers then used "a basic, no frills SVO sentence" as the raw material for combining drills, and O'Hare (1973) dropped "transformational" from the name, reporting syntactic growth without teaching formal grammar ([Universidad de Chile, Lenguas Modernas](https://lenguasmodernas.uchile.cl/index.php/LM/article/download/45910/47933/162212); [Yale-New Haven Teachers Institute unit](https://teachersinstitute.yale.edu/curriculum/units/1981/4/81.04.06/2); Tier 3; search summary, not opened).

Today's classroom kernel is narrower still. A district guide for The Writing Revolution (TWR) defines it as "a simple, active, declarative sentence containing no modifiers or connectives," which students expand by answering who, what, when, where, why and how ([Baltimore County TWR sentence expansion](https://sites.google.com/bcps.k12.md.us/ewhs-pl-and-literacy/writing-revolution/sentence-expansion); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). Reading Universe calls it "like a popcorn kernel" not yet expanded ([Reading Universe](https://readinguniverse.org/skill-explainer/sentence-writing/sentence-expansion-skill-explainer/explicitly-teaching-sentence-expansion); Tier 4, program site; search summary, not opened). Keys to Literacy expands a noun plus verb kernel in a fixed order: articles and adjectives on the subject, then adverbs, then a phrase ([Nebraska DOE Syntactic Awareness handout](https://www.education.ne.gov/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/syntactic-awareness.pdf); Tier 4, vendor; search summary, not opened).

**One thing she should know before committing: "kernel" does not mean the same thing in every source.** Harris counts a sentence with a prepositional phrase (N V P N) as a kernel. TWR counts only a bare subject and verb. Kolln sorts by verb type. Her own system already sits between them: in S5 ("The cat is under the bed") the prepositional phrase is part of the kernel, while in S1.A.PRP ("The dog ran to the park") the same kind of phrase is an expansion. If she adopts the word, the curriculum should define it in her terms: **a kernel is the clause type named by the first part of her code (S1 to S6), and everything after the first dot is a move.**

### 1.3 What the evidence shows, and what it does not

The strongest evidence is for sentence combining, which takes kernels and joins them. **Graham and Perin's 2007 meta-analysis found a weighted effect of 0.50 on writing quality in grades 4 to 12** ([AdLit: Writing Next summary](https://www.adlit.org/topics/writing/summary-writing-next); [Oregon DOE synopsis](https://www.oregon.gov/ode/educator-resources/essentialskills/Documents/essentialskillwriting_hs_level2_writing_next_synopsis.pdf); Tier 1; search summary, not opened), and the earlier report in this project notes that the comparison conditions were mostly traditional grammar, which itself scored -0.32. Saddler and Graham (2005) found peer-assisted sentence combining raised the syntactic complexity of both stronger and weaker young writers ([WWC study record](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Study/74410); Tier 1; search summary, not opened). A 2023 Turkish study with grades 2 to 4 reported better sentence fluency, quality and length ([Educational Psychology Review 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-023-09806-5); search summary, not opened). Gains can be uneven: one thesis found more words per T-unit and per clause but no more clauses per T-unit ([Montana State thesis](https://scholarworks.montana.edu/bitstreams/ab67093e-0345-4503-951a-bd6f4f4fd57e/download); Tier 2; search summary, not opened).

The named kernel programs have weak independent evidence. TWR's own evaluation by NORC was quasi-experimental, and TWR's page says it cannot claim causality; a Mathematica study measured teacher practice, not student writing ([NORC](https://www.norc.org/research/projects/writing-revolution-evaluation.html); [TWR research page, vendor](https://www.thewritingrevolution.org/research-impact/); search summary, not opened). TWR's own evidence document opens by noting there has been "so little high-quality research on writing instruction" ([TWR evidence PDF, vendor](https://www.thewritingrevolution.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/EVIDENCE-SUPPORTING-THE-WRITING-REVOLUTION-METHOD.pdf); search summary, not opened). **No study was found that teaches kernels to kindergartners, to students with intellectual disability, or to AAC users, and none compares teaching exact formulas with teaching pattern families.** The meta-analytic evidence starts at grade 4, and the best classroom video of kernel expansion is grade 2 ([Reading Universe video](https://readinguniverse.org/resources/video/sentence-writing/sentence-expansion-skill-explainer/using-function-questions-to-write-expanded-sentences); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). Her corpus supplies the most direct argument: kernel shares stay steady from grade 1 to grade 10 while phrases per sentence rise from 0.57 to 2.41, and only 26 of her listed formulas appear, in 3% of sentences (Data). Real texts are kernels plus moves almost every time, and an exact listed formula almost never.

### 1.4 The criticisms, and which ones apply to her

Three criticisms come up. **"The linguistics is outdated."** True of kernel as a theory of the mind: Chomsky dropped it, and a Chronicle essayist notes no linguist today defends the *Aspects* design (Chronicle, above). It does not touch kernel as a classroom label for basic sentence types, which is how Kolln, Keys to Literacy and Reading Universe use it, and how her codes use it. **"Knowing the system does not make students write better."** Mark Lester argued that teaching transformational grammar does not by itself improve writing ([Yale-New Haven unit](https://teachersinstitute.yale.edu/curriculum/units/1981/4/81.04.06/2); search summary, not opened), which matches the negative findings for labeling-only grammar in the earlier report. This applies to her only if students name kernels instead of building with them. **"It makes writing formulaic."** Critics told EdWeek TWR is "too constricting" and stifles creativity, and Hochman replied by asking whether a cook bakes a cake without a recipe ([EdWeek Q&A, 2017](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/q-a-the-writing-revolution-encourages-focus-on-crafting-good-sentences/2017/06); Tier 4, program author voice; search summary, not opened); Keys to Literacy acknowledges "we don't want to teach children that writing is primarily formulaic" ([Keys to Literacy blog, vendor](https://keystoliteracy.com/?p=10654); search summary, not opened). This criticism is mostly argued in interviews, not tested. Her protection against it is the same one the usage-based research suggests (next section): vary the words inside each pattern, then move students to joining and choosing.

### 1.5 Why four statement formulas first was the right call

Her decision to teach four specific statement formulas to a kindergartner fits usage-based research on how children learn grammar. Tomasello's "verb island" account holds that early grammar is organized one verb at a time and only later merges into general patterns like the transitive ([Tomasello 2000, Trends in Cognitive Sciences](https://archive.illc.uva.nl/LaCo/CLAS/clc13/papers/tomasello00tics.pdf); [Lieven 2009](https://www.eva.mpg.de/documents/deGruyter/Lieven_Developing_CogLing_2009_1552984.pdf); Tier 3; search summary, not opened). The strong version is disputed (Pine et al. 1998; Yang 2010), and Lieven and Goldberg argue both item-specific and general knowledge build up together ([Pine et al. 1998](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_2345703_1/component/file_2345702/Pine_etal_1998.pdf); [Yang 2010](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/courses/readings/Yang2010Manu_Zipf.pdf); search summary, not opened). Either way, **specific patterns come first and abstraction grows from them.**

Two lab findings shape what happens next. **Repetition first:** learners picked up a new construction better when one example was very frequent, or when the same examples came first, apparently because a shared concrete element makes the pattern easier to spot (Goldberg et al. 2004, 2007; Casenhiser & Goldberg 2005, reported in [Pulido 2024, SSLA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/89BEAAAE58A0D8DD11FCEC7D5837BF18/S0272263124000081a.pdf/optimizing_the_input_for_learning_of_l2specific_constructions_the_roles_of_zipfian_and_balanced_input_explicit_rules_and_working_memory.pdf); Tier 2; search summary, not opened). Later second-language studies were mixed, and one found **learners with low working memory gained from the repeated, low-variety input while high-working-memory learners gained from balanced input** (Pulido 2024, above), which matters for her students. **Then variety:** varied input helps children generalize, including in a study with children with language-learning disability ([Torkildsen et al., PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3973537); Tier 2; search summary, not opened). Caregivers naturally speak in "variation sets," neighboring utterances that repeat part and change part, and items in those sets show up earlier in children's speech ([PMC6174075](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6174075); Tier 3; search summary, not opened). All of this comes from lab and speech studies, not writing classrooms, so the transfer to her kindergartner is an inference.

The practical sequence that follows is: **one anchor sentence repeated** ("The frog jumps."), then **the same formula with many different words** (The owl hoots. The cat runs. An otter floats.: her own S1.A set is already a variation set), then **one new move at a time** in her code order, then **showing that different codes share a kernel**, then **joining kernels**. Her four-formula start is step one of that sequence. The kernel idea only changes what comes after it.

**My recommendation is C**, because it is the only option that matches both the usage-based research (start specific) and her corpus (real writing is kernels plus moves). It also requires no change to her codes or to her current kindergarten teaching. The engine already writes the first segment of every code, so all three options cost the same in software. The decision, and the hand-off point between A and B, are hers.

## Part 2. Formulas intertwine at three sizes

### 2.1 The nested model: a kernel and moves at every level

Her Hallmark observation is the key to the whole design: "This type of predictable format is what I think about with the story plot charts and paragraph formulas. There's got to be predictability in formatting 1-paragraph responses, 5-paragraph essays." The research and practitioner frameworks agree that each level has a small, stable core with optional additions, which is the kernel idea at a larger size. That gives Symbolic Grammar one rule students can carry everywhere: **every level has a kernel, moves and families, and the slots of each level are filled by formulas from the level below.** This nesting is my design synthesis; no program was found that assigns grammatical formulas to paragraph or story slots by rule (earlier report), so it fills a gap.

| Level | Kernel (smallest complete version) | Moves (expansions) | Families (variants of the same skeleton) | Predictable visual |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sentence | S1 to S6 | J, B, PRP, openers, joins, because-parts, Q/C/E changes | Her formula families | A strip of symbol cards, one per word, phrase as one card |
| Paragraph | Topic, one detail, ending (3 sentences) | Add evidence, add explain, add a transition, add a second detail, add a counterpoint | Story paragraph, informational (TIDE), opinion (TREE), response (RACE), her quote paragraph (five slots) | A slot strip with a slot icon on each box, each box holding a formula strip |
| Composition | Story grammar's core: character and setting, problem, attempt, outcome | More episodes, a falling out, a twist, dialogue scenes | Story Spine (8 lines), Hallmark plot (8 beats), Save the Cat (15 beats), Hero's Journey (12 stages), Propp (31 functions); essay as a paragraph zoomed out | A beat strip, plus a drawn rise and fall line for the story's shape |

The frameworks differ mostly in grain size: story grammar has 6 categories, Vogler's Hero's Journey 12 stages, Save the Cat 15 beats and Propp 31 functions ([Wikipedia: Hero's journey](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Monomyth); [Reedsy Save the Cat guide](https://blog.reedsy.com/guide/story-structure/save-the-cat-beat-sheet); [Wikipedia: Vladimir Propp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Propp); Tier 3 and 4; search summary, not opened). So the curriculum can teach the smallest core first and present the others as "zoomed in" versions, the same way S1.A.J.B.PRP is S1 zoomed in. For paragraph roles, Step Up to Writing already color-codes topic, detail and explanation ([Albany OR outline](https://albany.k12.or.us/media/2016/04/Step-Up-to-Writing-Outline.pdf); Tier 4, program; search summary, not opened), but its green and yellow would collide with her verb and pronoun colors, so **paragraph slots should use borders or slot icons and keep fill colors for word jobs.**

### 2.2 Story formulas: her Hallmark plot is story grammar in a holiday sweater

Journalists and Hallmark writers describe the genre in beat-like terms. Backstage attributes to a New York Times analysis the archetype of "a recently dumped, high-powered female executive from the city" who finds love "in a small town with the help of a handsome local fellow" ([Backstage](https://www.backstage.com/magazine/article/best-hallmark-christmas-movies-78065/); [AOL syndication of the NYT analysis](https://www.aol.com/entertainment/whats-recipe-hallmark-lifetime-holiday-174955468.html); Tier 4, journalism; search summary, not opened; the NYT original was not found). Commentators name a town-comes-together festival scene and note you know within 15 minutes who will fall for whom ([Slashfilm](https://www.slashfilm.com/1728759/things-happen-every-hallmark-christmas-movie); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). A Hallmark writer says the beats recur "in countless Hallmark movies" and "work more often than not," while other writers say "there's no real checklist," only practices like no real villains and a happy ending ([AP via ClickOrlando](https://www.clickorlando.com/entertainment/2024/12/13/how-to-write-a-holiday-rom-com-for-tv-according-to-the-experts/); [ABC News / AP](https://abcnews.go.com/Entertainment/wireStory/tips-writing-christmas-tv-movie-including-villains-keeping-116771604); Tier 4, practitioner voice; search summary, not opened). Save the Cat's site says Hallmark scripts run in a "9-act" format of commercial-break blocks, each ending on a small cliffhanger, mapped first onto standard three-act beats ([Save the Cat, Hallmark](https://savethecat.com/tips-and-tactics/how-to-write-and-sell-a-script-to-the-hallmark-channel); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). Researchers quoted by NBC Boston say the predictability is part of the appeal ([NBC Boston](https://www.nbcboston.com/news/national-international/why-it-feels-so-good-to-watch-hallmark-holiday-movies/2004790/?amp=1); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). No peer-reviewed analysis of Hallmark plots was found.

The table maps her eight beats onto four established frameworks. Stein and Glenn's story grammar has six categories (setting, initiating event, internal response, attempt, consequence, reaction) ([Illinois IDEALS report](https://ideals.illinois.edu/items/17757/bitstreams/63742/data.pdf); Tier 3; search summary, not opened). The Story Spine is Kenn Adams's chain "Once upon a time... Every day... But one day... Because of that... Because of that... Until finally... And ever since then..." ([NPR via WUNC, 2026](https://www.wunc.org/2026-06-23/meet-the-creator-of-the-story-spine-an-8-sentence-tool-to-create-and-analyze-stories); [SessionLab](https://www.sessionlab.com/methods/story-spine); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). Save the Cat places the Catalyst near 10%, the Midpoint near 50% and All Is Lost near 75% ([Kindlepreneur](https://kindlepreneur.com/save-the-cat-beat-sheet/); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). Propp's functions keep a fixed order, end with "31. Wedding," and are sometimes grouped by commentators into six stages (preparation, complication, transference, struggle, return, recognition) ([U. Michigan, Rabkin](https://websites.umich.edu/~esrabkin/Propp.htm); [Columbia Literary AI](https://literaryai.library.columbia.edu/litai/i10/); Tier 3; search summary, not opened). Vonnegut's "Boy Meets Girl" shape starts in the middle, rises, falls and rises again ([The Marginalian](https://www.themarginalian.org/2012/11/26/kurt-vonnegut-on-the-shapes-of-stories/); Tier 4; search summary, not opened).

| 8 | Happily ever after | Reaction | And ever since then... | The closing image | Recognition; function 31, Wedding | Highest point |

Two honest notes on the mapping. Story grammar has no "falling out" category; the falling out starts a second episode, which is how story grammar handles longer stories, and it is the beat that turns a one-episode story into a Hallmark movie. And the Save the Cat names in the table are only those captured in the notes; the full 15-beat list should be checked before it appears in materials.

**One design caution: romance is not the right costume for young students.** The skeleton is what transfers. The same eight beats work as a friendship story (a new kid moves to a farm, meets a kid she thinks is bossy, they are paired for the fair, become friends, fight, make up), which keeps her insight and suits Pre-A to B. The Hallmark version itself is a strong Level C and adult example because students already know the genre.

For students who need a lower language load, the Story Spine and a drawn Vonnegut shape come first: a line that goes up and down is a nonverbal plot formula, and the Story Spine is a fixed set of sentence starters, each of which already implies a sentence family ("But one day," is an opener; "Because of that," leads to a cause and result). Explicit story grammar teaching has some of the better evidence in this report: a 2021 meta-analysis of 26 narrative intervention studies found d = 0.51 and 0.54 in group designs and 1.24 in single-case designs, and children with developmental language disorder differ most from peers on story grammar ([ASHA LSHSS 2021](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2021_LSHSS-20-00160); [ASHA JSLHR 2022](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2022_JSLHR-22-00017); Tier 1; search summary, not opened). The best-known commercial tool, Story Grammar Marker, has only vendor evidence ([MindWing ASHA poster, vendor](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/0853/7592/files/mindWing_ASHAposterFinal.pdf); search summary, not opened). Whether oral narrative gains carry into written stories is not settled (earlier report).

### 2.3 The one-paragraph response: RACE, with a built-in formula move

RACE (Restate the prompt, Answer, Cite evidence, Explain), and RACES with a final Summary, are teaching guides with **no empirical study found** ([Purdue K-12 RACES](https://purdue.edu/science/K12/races.html); [Edutopia RACE video](https://www.edutopia.org/video/building-better-responses-with-the-race-writing-strategy); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). TIDE and TREE are the SRSD paragraph mnemonics with research behind the delivery method (see 2.4). Symbolic Grammar can make one step of RACE predictable in a way no other program can: **restating a question is turning a Q formula back into the S formula with the same number.** Her Q3 "Is the cat big?" becomes S3 "The cat is big." Her Q1 "Did the dog bark?" becomes S1 "The dog barked." Students already know these pairs from the turn-the-kernel lessons.

| Slot | Job | Formula move | Example (my coding) |
|---|---|---|---|
| R | Restate the question | Q to S with the same number | Why did the turtle win? → The turtle won the race. |
| A | Answer | Add a because-part: (S)C[S] | The turtle won the race because it never stopped. |
| C | Cite evidence | Opener PRP, plus the text's words: PRP,S2... | According to the story, the turtle kept walking. (her list: According to the book, frogs swallow food whole. PRP,S2.B) |
| E | Explain | B, opener or "This shows that" | In other words, slow and steady wins. |

### 2.4 Her five-sentence quote paragraph, slot by slot

Her Level C research paragraph (introduction sentence, a sentence introducing the quote, the quote, a sentence restating or supporting it, a conclusion) is **the same move sequence that the most widely taught evidence frameworks share.** University writing centers teach the "quote sandwich": a top slice that introduces the quote with a signal phrase, the quote with its citation, and a bottom slice that explains it, "because readers will not make that connection on their own" ([St. Louis CC Quote Sandwich](https://stlcc.edu/student-support/academic-success-and-tutoring/writing-center/writing-resources/quote-sandwich.aspx); [CMU direct quotations handout](https://www.cmu.edu/student-success/other-resources/handouts/comm-supp-pdfs/direct-quotations-using-source-material.pdf); [UW Odegaard](https://depts.washington.edu/owrc/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/Effective-Quote-Integration.pdf); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). PEEL and TEEL (Point or Topic, Evidence, Explain, Link) are standard in Australia and England ([Deakin paragraph guide](https://www.deakin.edu.au/students/study-support1/study-resources/study-support-guides/paragraph-structure); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). ICE (Introduce, Cite, Explain) is a common classroom name for the sandwich, but no source on ICE was retrieved, so it should be checked before it is named in materials. CER (Claim, Evidence, Reasoning) is the one with experimental evidence: **McNeill, Lizotte, Krajcik and Marx (2006) found that middle schoolers given scaffolds that faded wrote stronger reasoning on later unscaffolded items than students given constant scaffolds, and reasoning was the hardest part** ([University of Arizona record](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/supporting-students-construction-of-scientific-explanations-by-fa/); [Deep Blue full text](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/handle/2027.42/57509/20201_ftp.pdf?sequence=1); Tier 2; search summary, not opened; sample and effect sizes not verified). Her model can say it matches the most common college and secondary structure without adopting any one acronym.

| Her slot | Sandwich | PEEL / TEEL | CER / RACE | Formula families that fit (her list models) | Symbols students will see | Example on one topic (my coding) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Introduction sentence | Your point | Point / Topic | Claim / Answer | S3 or S4 claim: Penguins are amazing birds. (S4.J); opinion: I think that dogs are smart. ((S2.P)C[S3]) | red hand, green arrows, light blue circle | Frogs are surprising eaters. (S4 family) |
| 2. Sentence introducing the quote | Top bread, signal phrase | Evidence lead-in | Cite (lead-in) | PRP opener or a name plus a telling verb: According to the book, ... (PRP,S2.B); On Saturday, my family went to the lake. (PRP,S1.P.PRP) | brown box, red crown, green arrows | In her book about frogs, Dr. Lee studies how frogs eat. (PRP opener + S family) |
| 3. The quote | Filling | Evidence | Evidence / Cite | Her quoted-speech pattern: Mia said, "Let's go!" (S2.PN,"C1.V'P"); the quote inside is coded on its own | red crown or yellow arrows, green arrows, then the quote's own symbols | Dr. Lee writes, "Frogs swallow food whole." (S2.PN,"..." family) |
| 4. Restate or support | Bottom bread, explain | Explain | Reasoning / Explain | B or PRP opener: However, recess helps learning. (B,S2); For example, he chases the ball daily. (PRP,S2.A.P.B); a because-part: We stayed in because it rained. ((S1.P.B)C[S1.P]) | dark blue gear or brown box at the front, purple arrow for because | In other words, frogs do not chew their food. (PRP opener + Sn family) |
| 5. Conclusion sentence | Close | Link | Summary (RACES) | B opener: First, we ate. (B,S1.P) pattern with Therefore or So; semicolon join: It rained; therefore, the game was canceled. ((S1.P);(B,S1.A.V)) | dark blue gear, comma, the kernel | Therefore, frogs eat in a surprising way. (B,S1 family) |

Two observations make this paragraph especially teachable in her system. **Slot 3 uses machinery she already has:** a signal phrase plus a quote is her quoted-speech code (S2.PN,"...") with an author instead of a character, so dialogue and research quoting are one lesson taught twice. **Slot 4 is where students will struggle and where the payoff is largest.** Every framework has an "explain" step, CER research found reasoning hardest, and summarization had one of the two largest effects in Writing Next (ES 0.82), while writing about texts raised reading scores by about 0.40 SD in Writing to Read ([Writing Next PDF, Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org/sites/default/files/guide/writing-next-2007_0.pdf); [LINCS: Writing to Read](https://lincs.ed.gov/professional-development/resource-collections/profile-432); Tier 1; search summary, not opened). Slot 4 deserves the most modeling and the richest formula menu. A paraphrase can be taught as a formula change: keep the meaning, change the formula (turn the quoted simple sentence into a because-sentence or a negative), which is my design suggestion.

**Deliver it through SRSD.** Self-Regulated Strategy Development is the best-supported way to teach paragraph-part mnemonics to students with or at risk for disabilities. The WWC rates it "potentially positive" for students with learning disabilities, with 15 of 17 single-case writing experiments positive ([WWC SRSD report 2017](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/InterventionReports/wwc_srsd_111417.pdf); Tier 2; search summary, not opened), and POW + TREE improved persuasive writing for autistic students in grades 4 and 6 in a multiple-baseline study ([Scilit record](https://www.scilit.net/publications/bfa59331f9da1f8e70ffa6fbfd54e85a); Tier 2; search summary, not opened). The WWC secondary writing guide recommends explicit strategy teaching in a Model-Practice-Reflect cycle ([WWC summary](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_secwrit_summary_053117.pdf); Tier 1 panel guide; search summary, not opened). For her adult band, the federal LINCS TEAL guide recommends writing frames as confidence-building scaffolds ([LINCS TEAL, Make Use of Frames](https://lincs.ed.gov/state-resources/federal-initiatives/teal/guide/makeuse); search summary, not opened).

### 2.5 The five-paragraph essay as a paragraph zoomed out, with a fading path

The five-paragraph essay grew out of older theme-writing traditions ([Nunes 2013, Rhetoric Review](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/07350198.2013.797877); Tier 3; search summary, not opened). Mark Wiley (2000) concluded the harm comes less from the structure than from the dependency teachers build on it ([Scribd document citing Wiley](https://www.scribd.com/document/487851414/wp2final); search summary of a secondary document, not opened), and John Warner (2018) blames testing for its spread ([Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/book/61976); search summary, not opened). Defenders call it a useful beginner and timed-writing scaffold ([NCTE blog 2021](https://ncte.org/blog/2021/11/unmasking-mythical-narrative-surrounding-five-paragraph-essay/); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). English educator Leon Furze says TEEL "creates the illusion of success," like a cloze exercise, but is "perfectly acceptable" as a toehold for struggling writers if the scaffold comes off ([Furze blog](https://leonfurze.com/2021/09/11/teel-is-a-four-letter-word/); Tier 4, practitioner voice; search summary, not opened). The most concrete fading path found is TWR's: a Single Paragraph Outline, then (in the 2025 2.0 edition) Pre-Transition and Transition Outlines, then Multiple Paragraph Outlines in 3-, 4- and 5-paragraph versions ([TWR method page](https://thewritingrevolution.org/?p=507); [MPO 5 template](https://www.thewritingrevolution.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/MPO-5.pdf); [Wiley listing, TWR 2.0](https://www.wiley-vch.de/en/areas-interest/humanities-social-sciences/the-writing-revolution-2-0-978-1-394-35990-5); Tier 4, program; search summary, not opened). No study comparing a fixed five-paragraph form with a faded one was found.

The nesting gives a predictable picture: **the essay is her paragraph zoomed out.** The introduction paragraph grows from slot 1 (the claim becomes a thesis sentence), each body paragraph is one complete five-slot paragraph (slots 2 to 4 at essay size), and the conclusion paragraph grows from slot 5. Students who can build one paragraph already know the shape of the essay, which is the same "same skeleton, bigger size" lesson as S1 to S1.A.J.B.PRP.

| Step | What students do | Frame left on | Source for the step |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | One five-slot paragraph, every slot framed with a formula and slot icon | All slots | Her design; quote sandwich |
| 2 | Plan the same paragraph on an outline in key words, abbreviations and her symbols, then write it | Slots 2 and 4 only | TWR Single Paragraph Outline; McNeill fading |
| 3 | Choose each slot's formula from a menu of two or three | A menu, no fixed frame | Choice scaffolding (earlier report: Myhill's choice effect skipped weaker writers, so keep choices few) |
| 4 | Two paragraphs joined by a transition sentence | Transition slot only | TWR Transition Outline |
| 5 | Three to five body paragraphs, the number chosen by the writer | Checklist of moves only | TWR Multiple Paragraph Outline |
| 6 | Choose a structure (compare and contrast, cause and effect, problem and solution), so five paragraphs is one option, not the rule | None; self-check against the moves | Wiley and Furze critiques; CER fading result |

The critiques argue for building the "frame off" step into every template from the start, not as optional enrichment.

### 2.6 Dialogue with speech tags (her D-27)

Her D-27 decision notes speech tags the way prepositional phrases are noted: a tag rides on a sentence the way a phrase does. Her list already has the coding: "Mia said, "Let's go!"" is S2.PN,"C1.V'P", with the quote coded on its own. That gives a predictable dialogue formula with three positions for the tag, and each position is a move students can see:

| Tag position | Example (my examples) | What moves | Punctuation students must place |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tag first | Mia said, "Let's go!" (her list) | The tag is an opener, like PRP, | comma after the tag, quotation marks, end mark inside |
| Tag last | "Let's go!" said Mia. | The tag moves to the end, like a phrase at the end | end mark inside the quote, tag starts lowercase, period after the tag |
| Tag in the middle | "Wait," said Mia, "the bus is here." | The tag splits the quote, like a phrase set off by commas | commas on both sides of the tag, two sets of quotation marks |

The quote inside is usually a Q, C or E kernel, which is why dialogue is the natural home for those sentence types that are rare in reading texts (questions 1 to 6%, commands 2 to 4%, exclamations 0 to 5% of corpus sentences; Data). The punctuation in the right-hand column follows standard US convention (punctuation inside the closing quotation mark, a new paragraph for each new speaker), but **no source for dialogue punctuation rules was retrieved in this round**, so these should be checked against a style guide before they go into student materials. The progymnasmata, the classical graded exercises, recommended turning indirect speech into direct speech as an early exercise ([BYU Silva Rhetoricae: Fable](https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Pedagogy/Progymnasmata/Fable.htm); Tier 3; search summary, not opened), which is a ready-made D-27 practice: "Mia told Sam to hurry" becomes "Hurry!" said Mia.

## Part 3. A ladder from explicit to artistic

### 3.1 Every major tradition runs the same three-step arc

Four teaching traditions independently arrived at the same arc: study a model, rebuild it together, then write your own. The Sydney School's Teaching and Learning Cycle runs deconstruction, joint construction, independent construction ([ACU, Fenwick 2022](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/download/2e42fb5e661dd0958f254af65a86f5cf9db923bc387a734fe03627c4b8212e98/316773/AM_Fenwick_2022_Teacher_use_of_genre_pedagogy_engaging.pdf); [Victoria DET](https://www.education.vic.gov.au/school/teachers/teachingresources/discipline/english/literacy/writing/Pages/the-teaching-learning-cycle.aspx); Tier 3; search summary, not opened). Pie Corbett's Talk for Writing runs imitation, innovation, invention, and Corbett calls innovation "structured play" and invention "free play" ([Talk4Writing Inventing Stories PDF](https://www.talk4writing.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/08/Inventing-Stories.pdf); Tier 4, program; search summary, not opened). The progymnasmata had students retell, expand, condense and then write a new fable in close imitation before moving on to argument ([Wikipedia: Progymnasmata](https://en.Wikipedia.com/wiki/Progymnasmata); [Kennedy translations, SBL](https://www.sbl-site.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/KennedyProgymnasmata.pdf); search summary, not opened). Benjamin Franklin describes rewriting *Spectator* essays from his own notes, comparing his version with the original, and correcting his faults, which taught him "method in the arrangement of thoughts" ([Project Gutenberg, Autobiography](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/148/148-h/148-h.htm); Tier 4, writer's account; search summary, not opened; the fuller steps often retold, such as jumbling his notes and reordering them, were not verified).

The evidence base for the arc is thinner than its pedigree. The EEF's Talk for Writing pilot "was not able to securely estimate the impact" ([EEF](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/projects/talk-for-writing); Tier 2 pilot; search summary, not opened), and no effect sizes were found for the Sydney School cycle. Within the arc, the parts have different strength: **sentence combining (about 0.46 to 0.50) has more support than studying whole models (reported as 0.25, 0.17 or 0.22 in different summaries)** ([Oregon Writing Next synopsis](https://www.oregon.gov/ode/educator-resources/essentialskills/Documents/essentialskillwriting_hs_level2_writing_next_synopsis.pdf); [ERIC ED659639](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED659639.pdf); Tier 1; search summary, not opened). So formula-based building and combining should be the core, and model imitation a supplement.

### 3.2 The last rungs matter because models pull writing toward them

Creativity research explains why the arc must end in invention, not imitation. People who saw examples before a design task produced work that copied the examples' features, even when told not to, and the effect weakened when an unrelated task came between seeing and making ([Smith, Ward & Schumacher 1993, Memory & Cognition](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/bf03202751.pdf); Tier 2; search summary, not opened). That conformity effect is robust even for arbitrary features ([Memory & Cognition](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758%2FBF03211419.pdf); search summary, not opened). Yet copying abstract artworks increased the creativity of people's own drawings, so it depends on what kind of example is used ([Okada & Ishibashi 2017, eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt9n14h1bp/qt9n14h1bp.pdf?t=mtfdje); Tier 2; search summary, not opened; link attribution not fully confirmed). Constraint can help: rhymes written under constraints were rated more creative, with carryover to later free writing, in small adult experiments ([Haught-Tromp 2017, scite report](https://scite.ai/reports/the-green-eggs-and-ham-KYz9VM); Tier 2; search summary, not opened). Oulipo writers treat constraints as "creative calisthenics" that make the invisible rules of literature visible so they can be overcome ([Ploughshares](https://pshares.org/blog/the-formal-imagination-of-oulipo/); [Poetry Foundation](https://poetryfoundation.org/articles/162152/grave-unseriousness-experimenting-with-oulipo-constraints); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). And grammar taught as a choice with effects on the reader improved writing in an RCT of 744 Year 8 students (ES 0.21), though mainly for stronger writers ([Exeter ORE record](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10036/4479); Tier 1; search summary, not opened).

Three design rules follow, and her symbols already satisfy the first. **Abstract the model**: reduce it to symbols, codes and beats rather than copying its words, because the symbol strip is an abstract example. **Put a gap between studying and inventing**: a different task, or the next day. **Make bending a taught step**: "change one rule on purpose" turns the formula into an artistic choice. **No controlled study was found on whether explicit plot or genre teaching raises or lowers the originality of children's stories**, so these are reasoned extrapolations from adult and design studies.

## Part 4. Mnemonics and visual rules for Symbolic Grammar

### 4.1 What research says about mnemonics, and the rule that follows

Mnemonic instruction has strong support for students with learning disabilities, but mostly for remembering facts and vocabulary, not grammar rules. Scruggs and Mastropieri (1990) describe it as producing some of the largest gains in LD intervention research (a narrative review claim, not a pooled effect) ([KU-hosted PDF](https://simvilledev.ku.edu/sites/default/files/researc/SC32Scruggs.Mnemonic%20instruction%20for%20students.1990.pdf); Tier 3; search summary, not opened), and a systematic review of 20 studies with 669 youth with LD, emotional and behavioral disorders or mild developmental disabilities supported mnemonic interventions across ages and settings ([Wolgemuth, Cobb & Alwell 2008](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/esf_facpub/188); Tier 1; search summary, not opened). Dual coding theory (Clark & Paivio 1991) supports pairing words with visuals that add meaning, not decoration ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF01320076); Tier 3; search summary, not opened). Songs helped grammar transfer in one 2018 classroom study, but a 2024 review could not show songs beat other methods ([Frontiers in Psychology 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02386/full); [Oxford Education Deanery](https://www.education.ox.ac.uk/oxford-education-deanery/digest/can-children-in-classrooms-learn-new-languages-through-songs-a-look-at-what-research-on-this-topic-can-tell-us/); search summary, not opened). **No study of mnemonics for grammar or punctuation rules with LD students was found.** The rule for her catalog: **pair every list mnemonic with a structural test that follows from the grammar**, because a list helps memory but only a test predicts the right answer in a new sentence. Her own definitions ("Identifies time, place, or direction"; "Something you shout") and her engine conventions are the place to anchor those tests.

### 4.3 Punctuation and capital letters

The most predictive punctuation aids are physical or pictorial. **Kung Fu Punctuation** (Ros Wilson, Big Writing) gives each mark a move and a sound, taught in belts from 4 marks at white belt to 9 at black belt; moves vary by school, so a curriculum should fix one set ([Weis, Kung Fu Punctuation](https://weis.squarespace.com/blog/kung-fu-punctuation); [TES](https://www.tes.com/news/english-kung-fu-commas); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). The belt ladder fits her one-mark-at-a-time design, and no outcome research on it was found. **The semicolon as a period sitting on a comma** is accurate and predictive: it shows the mark is stronger than a comma and weaker than a period ([UHCL Writing Center](https://www.UHCL.EDU/writing-center/documents/tip-sheets/thesecretlifeofsemicolons.pdf); [TED-Ed](https://www.ted.com/talks/emma_bryce_how_to_use_a_semicolon/transcript); search summary, not opened), and her code (S1.A);(S3) already draws it as a join between two kernels. **The comma before a joining word** joins two complete kernels, and a comma alone between them is a splice ([NPS Graduate Writing Center](https://library.nps.edu/web/gwc/commas-fanboys); search summary, not opened); one corpus study questions how firm the rule is for short clauses ([Nordic Journal of English Studies](https://publicera.kb.se/njes/article/download/29464/23707/67774); search summary, not opened). **The comma after a fronted because-part** is the AAAWWUBBIS rule, and her code shows it literally: C[S1.P],(S1.P.B). **The apostrophe substitution test** (say "it is" or "it has"; if it fits, use it's) is one rule that covers it's, they're, you're and who's ([Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/when-to-use-its-vs-its); search summary, not opened), and it matches her engine rule that a contraction gets two symbols.

Her code notation is itself a predictive punctuation map, which no other program has: **every comma, semicolon and quotation mark in a code is in the place it goes in the sentence.** B,S1.A puts the comma after the opener; (S1.P);(B,S1.A.V) shows a semicolon and then an opener comma; S2.PN,"..." shows the comma before the quote. Teaching students to read the punctuation in the code is a visual rule that predicts the sentence. Capitalization, quotation-mark and appositive rules were not sourced this round. Per her standing rule, materials should always say "capital letter" and "punctuation."

### 4.4 Usage

Substitution tests beat letter-trivia hooks because they follow from the grammar. **There, their, they're**: "there" contains "here" (place), "they're" can be replaced by "they are," "their" owns ([Paul Brians, WSU](https://brians.wsu.edu/2016/05/25/theyre-their-there/); search summary, not opened). **Then and than**: than compares, then answers "when?" ([Ellii](https://ellii.com/blog/teaching-tips-on-than-and-then); Tier 4). **A lot** is two words; "alot" is not a word ([LibreTexts, Conventions 101](https://human.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Composition/Grammar/Conventions_101%3A_A_Functional_Approach_to_Teaching_(and_Assessing)_Grammar_and_Punctuation_(Ramsey)/01%3A_Chapters/1.13%3A_A_Lot_There_They're_Their_To_Two_Too); search summary, not opened). **Affect and effect (RAVEN: Remember Affect Verb, Effect Noun)** is widely used ([Grammar Girl](https://quickanddirtytips.com/?p=41683); [CDC clear writing](https://www.cdc.gov/nceh/clearwriting/writing-tips/2024/writing-tip-wed-07-31-2024.html); search summary, not opened) but **flagged**: effect can be a verb and affect a noun, and an educator notes RAVEN fails for students who cannot yet tell nouns from verbs ([WeAreTeachers](https://www.weareteachers.com/affect-or-effect/); Tier 4). In her system, RAVEN becomes "green arrows or red hand?", so it belongs only after those symbols are secure. **Who and whom**: "him" and "whom" both end in m; substitute he or him ([Grammar Girl via Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/1453502-who-vs-whom-which-is-correct); Tier 4). **"I before e except after c"** is **flagged**: it holds mainly for words where ie or ei says a clear long e, and has many common exceptions ([Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/i-before-e-isnt-always-correct); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_before_E_except_after_C); search summary, not opened); teach only the narrowed form with a visible "rule breaker" set, or leave it out. **"Never start a sentence with and or but"** is a belief, not a rule ([Grammarly](https://www.grammarly.com/blog/coordinating-conjunctions/); Tier 4). Subject-verb agreement, plural and to/too/two cues were not sourced this round.

### 4.5 Tense

Practitioners teach tense with a timeline and one anchor chart per tense, and textbooks model one verb across times ("keeps, kept, will keep") ([Teacher Thrive](https://teacherthrive.com/verb-tenses-timelines/); [Saylor](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-english-for-success/s05-03-verb-tense.html); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). No standard tense symbol set was found. Shape Coding uses arrows for tense, and its past-tense trial found large gains on taught items but none on standardized grammar measures (earlier report; Tier 1). Her design fits this: **the tense symbol sits on the green arrows, a timeline strip runs past (left), present (middle), future (right), and the word ending is marked on the verb card itself** (-ed, or will plus the verb), because the evidence points to marking the ending, not only the time. Irregular past forms are best shown as visual families (sing, sang, sung; ring, rang, rung) rather than one rule ([UNM Core Writing](https://pressbooks.pub/unmcorewriting/?p=1966); search summary, not opened). Her rule to say past, present and future, never yesterday, now and tomorrow, matches timeline practice.

### 4.6 Her colors clash with Montessori or Colourful Semantics in at least six places

Montessori grammar symbols label word class: noun a large black triangle, verb a large red circle at the center "because the other words depend on it," article a small light-blue triangle, adverb a small orange circle ([Montessori Mom](https://montessorimom.com/grammar-symbols-lesson-62/); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). The other Montessori symbols are usually given as adjective dark blue triangle, pronoun purple triangle, preposition green crescent, conjunction pink bar and interjection gold keyhole, but **these come from general knowledge and were not confirmed** in this round. Colourful Semantics labels sentence role: orange who, yellow what doing, green what, blue where, brown when, with keys varying by provider ([Bradford info sheet](https://bso.bradford.gov.uk/userfiles/file/!Learning%20Support/Colourful%20Semantics%20-%20Info%20and%20Resources.pdf); [Leicestershire NHS](https://www.leicspart.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Colourful-Semantics.pdf); Tier 4, clinical; search summary, not opened); its main group study had only six children ([Structural Learning guide](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/colourful-semantics-a-teachers-guide); search summary, not opened).

Shape Coding (Ebbels) uses shapes for structures, colors for word classes and arrows for tense, and its shapes can nest to show phrases inside sentences ([Moor House School](https://moorhouseschool.co.uk/shape-coding); [Ebbels, UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1516142/1/Ebbels_Ebbels%20Shape%20Coding%20therapy%20(accepted%20version)_segment.pdf); Tier 3; search summary, not opened); it is the closest comparator to study, both to borrow its nesting idea for the brown box and to avoid duplicating it.

## Conclusion

The kernel question turns out to be smaller than it looked and the integration question larger. Her notation already encodes kernels, expansions and transformations more cleanly than the programs that use the word, so committing to "kernels" is mostly a decision about when students are told what the first part of the code means. Her kindergarten start is not in tension with that; it is the first step of the sequence the usage-based research describes. The bigger finding is that the same structure repeats at every size. A sentence, a paragraph, an essay and a Hallmark movie each have a minimum version, a set of moves and a family of variants, and her symbols and codes can make each one visible and predictable in the same way. That consistency is the most distinctive thing Symbolic Grammar could offer, because no program found links the grammar of a sentence to the slots of a paragraph and the beats of a story with one notation.

The open risk is the one her Hallmark example names without saying it: predictable formats are comforting precisely because they repeat, and repetition is what makes writing formulaic. The research on examples says the way out is not to avoid the formula but to abstract it, label it and then bend it on purpose. A student who can say "this is the falling-out beat, and I am going to skip it" has crossed from following the recipe to writing.
