# One fade path for every formula: a proposal

Prepared by Beth, 2026-11-21, in answer to her request of the same day. This is a proposal, not a literature review. Every research claim comes from the three earlier reports in this folder (*Explicit grammar before creative writing*, *Formula families and sentence expansion*, *Formula integration and genre templates*). No full-text source was opened for any of them, because the network blocked journal and repository hosts, so every source keeps the label **search summary, not opened**, and its tier: Tier 1 is a meta-analysis, systematic review or RCT; Tier 2 is quasi-experimental or single-case; Tier 3 is correlational, descriptive or theory; Tier 4 is practitioner voice, which shows what is usable and never shows that something works. "Data" means her hand-coded corpus of 2,039 reading-passage sentences. Example sentences marked "her list" come from her_formula_list.json; codes marked "my coding" are mine and should be run through the Symbolic Grammar Engine before use.

## 1. Summary

**The proposal is one fade path, the same six stages for every formula, every paragraph formula and every story formula, in which each stage removes exactly one support and a student moves on only by meeting a fluency rule and passing an untrained probe.** The supports come off in a fixed order: the given words first, then the symbol strip, then the code, then the job prompt. A student who has learned the path once knows what will happen with every new formula, so the procedure itself becomes explicit and predictable, which is what her sound study already does for letters. The scaffold is the grammar itself, shown with her symbols, so a student always knows why a slot exists; and the symbols never disappear, they change jobs from a building guide to a self-check tool. Two supporting changes make it stronger. **Supporting option 1** keeps the path identical for every learner but sets each student's entry stage and pace with a placement probe, separates the response mode (cubes, symbol cards, pointing, dictation, typing, handwriting) from the stage, and lets students who show mastery on a cold probe skip ahead. **Supporting option 2** runs the path backward alongside the forward path: students code mentor sentences and their own free writing with the symbols, rebuild a model from its code the next day (Franklin's method), and then "change one rule on purpose." The parts are borrowed from methods with evidence (Direct Instruction with precision teaching, CRA fading, faded scaffolds, SRSD, sentence combining). The whole package has never been tested, so it needs a pilot.

## 2. The main option: the six-stage fade path

### 2.1 Why this is the strongest choice

Her students need to know what is coming, and they need the scaffold to come off in a way they can see. Current tools fade unpredictably or not at all: a graphic organizer or sentence starter is handed out, used, and then simply not handed out next time. The fade path makes the fade itself the routine. It is strongest for four reasons.

- **It reuses the best-supported sentence method for students with disabilities.** Datchuk's studies teach one simple sentence pattern with Direct Instruction, then build rate and accuracy with timed practice (precision teaching) before anything else is added (Datchuk 2016, 2017; Datchuk & Kubina 2017; Tier 2 single-case; search summary, not opened). The WWC elementary guide rates fluent sentence construction as Moderate evidence (Tier 1 panel guide; search summary, not opened; one excerpt says Strong, unresolved).
- **It fades in a known order, as CRA does.** CRA is an evidence-based practice for math with students with learning disabilities, and its core move is to fade objects and drawings until students work with symbols (Bouck, Satsangi & Park 2018; Flores, Hinton & Schweck 2014; Tier 1 synthesis; search summary, not opened). **Nothing in the CRA research tests grammar or writing.**
- **Faded scaffolds beat constant scaffolds in the one writing-structure study found.** McNeill, Lizotte, Krajcik and Marx (2006) found middle schoolers given fading scaffolds wrote stronger reasoning on later unscaffolded items than students given constant scaffolds (Tier 2; search summary, not opened; sample and effect sizes not verified).
- **It ends in building and choosing, not labeling.** Grammar taught as naming and parsing has a negative effect on writing (Graham & Perin 2007, ES -0.32; Tier 1; search summary, not opened), while sentence combining is positive (ES 0.50; same source). Every stage of the path ends with the student producing a sentence.

### 2.2 The six stages, defined

Each stage removes one support. The order never changes.

| Stage | Name | What is fixed | What the student chooses | Symbol and visual support that stays | Response mode (default) | Mastery rule to move on |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Rebuild** | The anchor sentence, its words, its code and its symbol strip | Nothing: the student rebuilds the model after the teacher's I do | Full symbol strip with word cards on it; the code printed underneath | Build with cubes or symbol cards, say it, then write or point | Rebuilds and reads the anchor with no prompt, on 2 separate days |
| 2 | **Swap** | The code, the symbol strip, all words but one slot | One word, from a closed bank of 2 or 3 picture word cards, for one slot at a time (the noun first, then the verb, then the phrase) | Full symbol strip; the open slot shows only its symbol | Pick a card, place it, read the new sentence | Fluency aim met for correct swaps per minute, on 2 of 3 sessions |
| 3 | **Fill** | The code and the symbol strip | Every word, from an open bank or the student's own words | Full symbol strip, empty | Build, then write (or the student's mode, Option 1) | Fluency aim met for correct formula sentences per minute, on 2 of 3 sessions, **plus an untrained probe** passed |
| 4 | **Code** | The code only (for example S1.A.PRP) | Every word | No strip during writing; after writing, the student lays or draws the symbols over the sentence to check it | Write from the code, then self-check with symbols | Fluency aim met with self-check accuracy, on 2 of 3 sessions |
| 5 | **Choose** | A job, not a formula ("Tell where the cat hid." or a picture with a question) | The formula family and the words; the student names the family before or after writing | No code given; a family menu of 2 or 3 choices with symbols is available on request | Write, then code their own sentence | The chosen formula does the job and matches the student's own code, on 2 of 3 sessions |
| 6 | **Free** | Nothing about the sentence; the writing purpose comes from the student's story, answer or report | Everything | Symbols as a self-check tool only: the student codes chosen sentences after writing and checks for green arrows, a closed brown box, a capital letter and punctuation | Free writing in the student's mode | Untrained probe passed in free writing (the formula family appears, correct, without being asked for) |

**The fixed language.** Each stage opens with the same teacher words, so the routine is predictable even when the formula is new: "Today the words are given" (1), "Today you change one word" (2), "Today you choose the words" (3), "Today you get the code" (4), "Today you get the job" (5), "Today you write; then we check" (6). These words are my draft and are hers to change.

**The mastery rule.** Two parts, both required at the stages that name them.

1. **Fluency.** Accuracy alone does not move a student on; accuracy at a rate does. Datchuk's studies score complete simple sentences per minute, correct word sequences (CWS), incorrect word sequences (IWS) and CWS minus IWS (Tier 2; search summary, not opened). The path uses "correct formula sentences per minute": a sentence counts when every slot holds a word of the right job, with a capital letter and punctuation. **No fluency aim for sentence formulas was found in any source**, so the aim must be set locally, from her own baseline data, and recorded as a curriculum decision, not a research figure. "2 of 3 sessions" is also a local starting rule.
2. **An untrained probe.** In the past-tense Shape Coding trial, taught items gained a great deal but standardized grammar measures did not move at all (Calder et al., JSLHR; Tier 1, small crossover RCT; search summary, not opened; the d is reported as 3.03 in one summary and 1.22 in another, unreconciled), and researcher-made measures show larger effects than general ones (2025 meta-analysis, UCC repository; Tier 1; search summary, not opened). So at Stage 3 and Stage 6 the student meets a picture and words never used in lessons. Passing means the formula appears, correct, with new content.

**Step back rule.** If a student falls below the aim on 2 sessions in a row, the teacher returns one stage, not to the start. This keeps the fade honest in both directions. It is a design rule, not a research figure.

**Where prompting fits.** Stages say what support is built into the task; her existing Prompt Ladder and Response Codes tab says how much the adult helps within a stage. Mastery counts only responses at the independent level of that ladder, so "independent versus prompted" is recorded the same way it is now.

### 2.3 End to end on one formula: S1.A.PRP, "The cat hid under the bed." (her list)

The engine breakdown is article, noun, verb, preposition, article, noun. Her symbols: **pink triangle** (The), **red pointing hand** (cat), **green four-way arrows** with the **past tense symbol** on them (hid), and a **brown box** holding the whole phrase "under the bed" (inside it, the preposition, a pink triangle and a red pointing hand). The brown box is one card or one snapped cube stick, which applies D-25 (prepositions taught standing alone and in a phrase). If the student writes "under the bunk bed," bunk bed takes one red pointing hand (D-28, one thing). A word-group preposition such as "out of" takes one brown symbol (D-29).

| Stage | What the student sees and does |
|---|---|
| 1 Rebuild | The teacher models: hides a toy cat under a toy bed, says the sentence, builds it with five cards (triangle, hand, arrows, brown box, with the box holding three words). The student rebuilds it, touches each card while reading, writes it. Before Stage 1 on any PRP formula, P1 and P2 from the prepositional phrase family (the preposition alone, then the phrase as one piece) are already in place. |
| 2 Swap | Same strip. The red hand slot is open: dog, fox, mouse. "The dog hid under the bed." Next session the arrows slot is open (hid, slept, sat; all past). Then the brown box is open (under the bed, behind the door, in the box). This is the variation set her S1.A list already models. |
| 3 Fill | Empty strip, student's own words: "The frog sat on the log." Timed rounds. Untrained probe: a new picture (a bird in a nest) produces "The bird sat in the nest." |
| 4 Code | Card reads only S1.A.PRP. Student writes "A boy ran to the park," then lays symbols over it to check; the check shows the brown box closes with a naming word. |
| 5 Choose | Picture of a dog behind a couch, prompt "Tell where the dog is hiding." Menu if requested: S1 with a brown box, or S5 (be plus a phrase, "The dog is behind the couch," my coding). Student picks, writes, codes it. |
| 6 Free | Inside the student's own story the family shows up unasked: "My cat slept on my homework." The student codes one chosen sentence afterward. |

Tense rides along at every stage: from Stage 3 on, the student can be asked to rebuild the same sentence in past, present and future ("The cat hides under the bed." "The cat will hide under the bed."), with the tense symbol on the green arrows and the ending marked on the verb card, as her Tense Builder procedure already does.

### 2.4 The same path at paragraph level: her five-sentence quote paragraph

At paragraph level, the layers are the same in kind. The "words" are the sentences; the "symbol strip" is a slot strip with each slot's formula code and symbols; the "code" is the slot strip with slot icons only; the "job" is the paragraph's purpose. Her slots: (1) introduction sentence, (2) sentence introducing the quote, (3) the quote, (4) a sentence restating or supporting it, (5) a conclusion. The quote sandwich, PEEL/TEEL and CER share this sequence (Tier 4 writing-center and program guides; search summary, not opened), and her slot 3 reuses her quoted-speech code, "Mia said, "Let's go!"" (S2.PN,"C1.V'P", her list), with the speech tag noted like a prepositional phrase (D-27).

| Stage | Paragraph version |
|---|---|
| 1 Rebuild | A model paragraph on one slot strip; the student rebuilds it from five sentence strips in order and names each slot's job |
| 2 Swap | Four sentences given; the student writes one slot, starting with slot 1 (claim, an S4 family sentence such as "Frogs are surprising eaters." my coding) and ending with slot 4 |
| 3 Fill | Slot strip shows a recommended formula family per slot (for example slot 2: a brown box opener, "In her book about frogs, Dr. Lee studies how frogs eat." my coding); the student writes all five. Untrained probe: a new article |
| 4 Code | Slot icons only; the student chooses each slot's formula, then codes the paragraph to check every slot is filled |
| 5 Choose | A purpose ("Use one quote to prove your point."); the student picks the paragraph formula (quote paragraph, RACE, or a story paragraph) |
| 6 Free | A real research response; the student checks slots afterward |

Slot 4 should get the most time at every stage, because every framework flags "explain" as the hardest part and McNeill et al. found reasoning hardest (Tier 2; search summary, not opened). Deliver the paragraph stages inside SRSD's stages (background, discuss, model, memorize, support, independent), which has the best support for students with or at risk for disabilities (WWC SRSD report, 15 of 17 single-case writing experiments positive; Tier 2; search summary, not opened; POW + TREE with autistic students in grades 4 and 6, Tier 2; search summary, not opened).

### 2.5 The same path at story level: her Hallmark-style beat plot

Here the "symbol strip" is a beat strip with an icon and a recommended formula family for each of her eight beats; the "code" is the beat strip with icons only; the "job" is the genre. Her beats, shortened: (1) the protagonist in her life, (2) an event moves her somewhere new, (3) she meets someone she thinks is a wrong fit, (4) they are thrown together by a festival or charity, (5) they grow closer, (6) a falling out, (7) something brings them back together, (8) happily ever after. For Pre-A to B, the same beats wear a friendship costume (a new kid at a farm, a kid who seems bossy, paired for the fair, a fight, making up).

| Stage | Story version |
|---|---|
| 1 Rebuild | Retell a model friendship story beat by beat, placing each beat sentence on the strip (oral, cards or writing) |
| 2 Swap | Change one beat only, for example the festival (the fair becomes a science night) |
| 3 Fill | Write one sentence per beat using the beat's family (beat 6, the falling out: an Sn sentence, "Jack did not call." my coding; beat 8: S6, "Holly felt happy." my coding) |
| 4 Code | Icons only; the student picks each beat's formula |
| 5 Choose | A genre job ("Write a story where two people who disagree become friends."); the student chooses the plot formula (eight beats or the Story Spine) |
| 6 Free | The student's own story, then labels the beats as a self-check |

Story grammar instruction has some of the better evidence here: a 2021 meta-analysis of 26 narrative intervention studies found d = 0.51 and 0.54 in group designs and 1.24 in single-case designs (ASHA LSHSS; Tier 1; search summary, not opened). Whether oral narrative gains carry into written stories is not settled (Davison SRCLD 2020 poster; Tier 2; search summary, not opened), which is why each story stage ends in at least one written sentence.

### 2.6 How this differs from graphic organizers, writing prompts and sentence starters

| | Graphic organizers | Writing prompts | Sentence starters and frames | The fade path |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| What the scaffold holds | Ideas and their order | A topic | The first words of a sentence | The grammar itself: the job of every word, shown with her symbols |
| Does the student know why each part exists? | Usually the box's purpose, not the sentence's structure | No | Rarely; the starter is copied | Yes: each slot is a word job with a name, a symbol and a test |
| What is fixed | The boxes | Nothing about form | The opening words | Named per stage, one support at a time |
| How it fades | Usually not planned; handed out, then not | Not applicable | Usually not planned | Six stages in a fixed order, the same for every formula |
| How the fade is decided | Teacher judgment | Not applicable | Teacher judgment | Fluency rule plus an untrained probe |
| What stays when the scaffold is gone | Nothing | Nothing | Nothing | The symbols, as a self-check tool |
| Same procedure at sentence, paragraph and story level? | Different organizer for each | No | No | Yes |
| Evidence | Listed in autism syntheses, almost always inside multi-component packages (Accardo and colleagues; Tier 1 review of mostly Tier 2; search summary, not opened) | None found for prompts as instruction | LINCS TEAL recommends frames for adults as confidence builders (search summary, not opened); critics say frames create "the illusion of success" if never removed (Furze; Tier 4) | Parts have evidence (above); the package is untested |

The fair summary: the fade path does not replace these tools so much as absorb them. Her beat strip and slot strip are graphic organizers; Stage 2 is a sentence frame. What is different is that the frame is built from word jobs instead of words, it comes off on a measured schedule, and something (the symbols) stays behind that the student can still use.

### 2.7 What is new

No program was found that applies one fixed fade sequence to sentence patterns or assigns grammatical formulas to paragraph and story slots by rule, and no study tests a fluency criterion for formula scaffolds or symbols kept as a self-check. **These are her design gaps to pilot, not findings.**

### 2.8 Risks and how to manage them

| Risk | Management |
|---|---|
| The path becomes labeling drills, the category with negative evidence | Every stage ends with a produced sentence; coding alone never counts as a session |
| Students stall at Stage 3 and never leave the strip (the "illusion of success") | The untrained probe is required; Stage 4 starts when the rule is met, not when the teacher feels ready |
| Six stages times 91 codes is too slow | Stages run per **family**, not per code: once a student clears Stage 3 for the S1 family with a brown box, new S1 members enter at Stage 3 or 4 (see the cold probe in Option 1) |
| Writing becomes formulaic | Creative writing is never graded on formula use; Stage 6 counts only whether the student can check, not whether every sentence is a formula. Option 2 adds bending on purpose |
| The symbols get credit they have not earned | In the key Shape Coding trial a semantic therapy without shapes did about as well (Ebbels, van der Lely & Dockrell 2007; Tier 1 small RCT; search summary, not opened). Describe the symbols as a vehicle for explicit structure |

## 3. Supporting option 1: same path, different entry and pace

### 3.1 The change

The path stays identical for every learner. Three dials change.

| Dial | How it is set |
|---|---|
| **Entry stage** | A short placement probe per formula family. Give Stage 4 first (code only). Passing at the fluency aim starts the student at Stage 5; partly correct starts at Stage 3; no correct sentence starts at Stage 2 or 1. Her existing Writing Check and placement, and the Sentence Points probe between benchmarks, are the homes for this. |
| **Pace** | Sessions per stage are set by the mastery rule, not the calendar. A general education student may clear Stages 1 to 3 in one lesson; a student with high support needs may spend weeks at Stage 2 |
| **Response mode** | Separate from the stage. The stage says what is fixed; the mode says how the student answers. Modes: real objects and acting it out; snap cubes; symbol cards; pointing to or selecting a card; digital drag tiles in Literacy Manipulatives; dictation to a scribe or speech-to-text; typing; handwriting. Mastery is recorded per mode, so a student can be at Stage 5 when dictating and Stage 3 when handwriting, and both are true |

**Acceleration rules.** (1) A cold probe before each new family member: meeting the aim on first try skips the stage. (2) Students who clear Stage 3 for two families in a row enter the next family at Stage 3. (3) Level C and general education students may enter paragraph and story paths at Stage 3, using the sentence families they already have. **Deceleration rules** are the step back rule, plus more Stage 2 swaps with low variety for students who need it.

### 3.2 Why this is the strongest support for the main option

It keeps explicitness the same for everyone and changes only where a learner starts and how fast she moves, which is the UDL position the curriculum already takes. It also answers the specific evidence about her students:

- **Low working memory benefits from repeated, low-variety input; high working memory from balanced input** (Pulido 2024, SSLA, reporting Goldberg and colleagues; Tier 2; search summary, not opened). That is a direct argument for letting Stage 2 run longer for some students and shorter for others, inside the same stage.
- **Removing transcription lets students with learning disabilities compose longer and better texts**: dictation ES 0.55 (Gillespie & Graham 2014; Tier 1; search summary, not opened); dictated stories longer, better and with fewer grammar errors (MacArthur & Graham 1987, n = 11; Tier 2; search summary, not opened). Separating mode from stage keeps a student with dysgraphia from being held at Stage 2 by her pencil.
- **Transcription load crowds out ideas** (McCutchen 1996; Berninger's Simple View of Writing; Tier 3; search summary, not opened). Handwriting and spelling still get taught (handwriting instruction ES 0.84 on quality, Santangelo & Graham 2016; Tier 1; search summary, not opened); the handwriting mode simply advances on its own track.
- **Choice helped able writers and was neutral for below-average writers** (Jones, Myhill & Bailey 2013, 744 Year 8 students, ES 0.21; Tier 1; search summary, not opened). Stage 5's menu of 2 or 3 options is the extra scaffold weaker writers need, and stronger writers can skip the menu.
- **Explicit, modeled instruction has the largest effects for learners with LD** (strategy instruction 1.09, Gillespie & Graham 2014; Tier 1; search summary, not opened) and points the same way for autistic learners (Pennington & Delano 2012; Accardo and colleagues; Tier 1 reviews of mostly Tier 2; search summary, not opened). For ADHD the writing evidence is one single-case SRSD study with 2 students (Tier 2; search summary, not opened), so the ADHD supports here (short timed rounds, visible progress, quick feedback) are reasoned design, not tested practice.

### 3.3 Proven methods reused

Precision teaching (Datchuk line, Tier 2), CRA fading (Tier 1, math only), guided invented spelling (Sénéchal et al., small RCT with 56 kindergartners; Tier 1; search summary, not opened) and dictation (Tier 1), all cited above.

### 3.4 What is new

A single fade path with response mode as a separate dial, and mastery tracked per mode. No study was found of this structure.

### 3.5 Risks and management

| Risk | Management |
|---|---|
| **AAC is outside the curriculum's current scope.** The standing scope boundary says no ASL, AAC or other-language directions, supports or materials until consultations happen | "Pointing to or selecting a symbol card" is written as a general response mode, not as AAC instruction. Whether the path names AAC systems is a decision for her (Section 7). Story Champs adapted for AAC users worked in a proof-of-concept study (KU Life Span Institute; Tier 3; search summary, not opened), logged as landscape only |
| Mastery per mode multiplies record keeping | Literacy Manipulatives logs stage and mode automatically for digital builds; paper probes use one sheet with a mode column |
| Choking hazard with cubes for students who mouth objects | Cubes wider than 1.25 inches (CPSC small-parts rule; search summary, not opened); OT to confirm |
| Placement probe misplaces students | The step back rule corrects it within two sessions |

## 4. Supporting option 2: run the path backward, then bend it

### 4.1 The change

The main option runs from rigid to free: synthesis. Option 2 adds the reverse direction, analysis, on a weekly loop that starts once a student has cleared Stage 3 for a family. The loop has three steps.

1. **Code it.** Code one mentor sentence from a reading passage, and one sentence from the student's own free writing, with her symbols and codes.
2. **Rebuild it (Franklin).** Put the original away. The next day, write a new sentence from the code alone, then compare it with the mentor sentence card by card and correct it.
3. **Change one rule on purpose.** Take the rebuilt sentence and change exactly one thing, then say what it does to the reader. The menu of rules is her own grammar: change the tense on the green arrows; turn the kernel into its transformation using her numbering (S3 "The turtle is slow." to Q3 "Is the turtle slow?" to Sn3 "The turtle is not slow." to E3 "The turtle is so slow!", my coding for the last three); slide the brown box to the front with a comma (PRP,S1); join two kernels with a purple two-way arrow or a because-part in a bracket; take every light blue circle out; at story level, skip or move one beat.

The same loop runs at paragraph level (reduce a model paragraph to a slot strip of codes, rebuild it, change the order of one slot) and story level (reduce a picture book to its beats, rebuild a new story on them, bend one beat, as in a "fall, then rise" plot instead of the usual rise).

### 4.2 Why this is the strongest bridge

The research on creative examples says models pull new work toward their surface features (Smith, Ward & Schumacher 1993; Tier 2; search summary, not opened) and that the pull weakens with a gap between seeing and making, while abstract examples can raise creativity (Okada & Ishibashi 2017; Tier 2; search summary, not opened; link attribution not fully confirmed). **Coding a sentence is abstraction**: the student keeps the structure and throws away the words. **Rebuilding the next day is the gap.** **Changing one rule is choice taught explicitly**, which is what the Myhill trial found helps writing (Tier 1; search summary, not opened), and constraint play has small adult evidence of raising creativity with carryover to free writing (Haught-Tromp 2017; Tier 2; search summary, not opened). Sentence combining, which step 3's joining moves are, has more support (ES 0.50) than studying whole models (0.25, 0.17 or 0.22 in different summaries; Tier 1; search summary, not opened), so the loop keeps building and combining at its core and uses models only as raw material.

I considered two other bridges. **The next-sentence decision tree** is her own design and a good later tool for Levels B and C, but no study of a "pick the next sentence" tool was found for any population, so it is weaker as the main bridge; it fits best as the delivery tool for Stage 5 once this loop is running. **Studying models alone** (Talk for Writing's imitation, innovation, invention) has only a pilot that the EEF could not use to estimate impact (Tier 2 pilot; search summary, not opened). The code, rebuild and bend loop uses parts of both and adds the abstraction step neither has.

### 4.3 Proven methods reused

Franklin's account of rewriting *Spectator* essays from his notes and comparing (Project Gutenberg; Tier 4, writer's account; search summary, not opened; the often-retold jumbling step was not verified); Killgallon's unscramble, imitate, combine and expand (Tier 4); sentence combining (Tier 1); SRSD's self-check (Tier 2). The progymnasmata's exercise of turning indirect speech into direct speech (BYU Silva Rhetoricae; Tier 3; search summary, not opened) is a ready-made D-27 rule change: "Mia told Sam to hurry." becomes ""Hurry!" said Mia."

### 4.4 What is new

A notation in which the code is the abstracted model, the transformations are numbered with their kernel (S3, Q3, Sn3, E3), and punctuation sits in the code where it goes in the sentence (B,S1.A; C[S1.P],(S1.P.B)). No program was found that does this, so "change one rule" can be precise in her system in a way it cannot be elsewhere.

### 4.5 Risks and management

| Risk | Management |
|---|---|
| Coding becomes the goal (labeling) | The loop always ends with a sentence written and a rule changed; coding alone is not a session |
| Mentor sentences are too complex to code (her corpus has 1,811 different formulas in 2,039 sentences, and only 3% match her list; Data) | Teachers pick mentor sentences from the student's cleared families only; the engine flags any sentence outside them |
| Weaker writers cannot judge "what it does to the reader" | Show two versions side by side with symbols and ask which fits a named job; offer 2 choices, not open questions |
| D-25 is still open, so "We stayed in" has no settled code | Leave sentences with a stand-alone preposition at the end of a clause out of the mentor set until she decides |

## 5. What changes if she adopts it

**In the curriculum (brief).**
- One new procedure in Procedures, Strategies and Activities: the six-stage fade path with its fixed teacher language, used by every formula, paragraph and story lesson. Procedure A (Introducing a New Sentence Formula) becomes Stage 1 of it.
- One mastery rule in the Assessment Guide: fluency aim plus untrained probe, the step back rule, and the cold-probe acceleration rule; every skill page points to it instead of defining its own.
- Each skill page lists the entry stage by level (Pre-A, A, B, C) and the response modes.
- Paragraph and story pages get a slot strip and a beat strip with a recommended family per slot.
- The code, rebuild and bend loop goes into the Practice Activities Bank as a weekly routine from Level B (Level A in oral form).
- New terms (the six stage names, "untrained probe," "change one rule") go into the Glossary first.

**In Literacy Manipulatives (brief).** The builder gets a stage setting per formula that locks given words (Stage 1), opens one slot with a closed card bank (2), opens all slots (3), hides the symbol strip and shows only the code (4), hides the code and shows a job card (5), or shows nothing and offers "check my sentence," which lays her symbols over typed text (6). It logs correct formula sentences per minute, stage, mode and prompt level, and runs the timed probe. Codes and symbols come from the Symbolic Grammar Engine, the only shared piece; no code moves between Literacy Manipulatives and the dashboard.

## 6. How to pilot and measure it (brief)

**Design.** A single-case multiple baseline across formula families, matching the Datchuk designs: start the fade path on one family (S1 with a brown box) while two others stay at baseline, then start the second, then the third. Run it with her kindergarten student, two or three Level A and B students with high support needs, and one small general education group to test acceleration. If possible, teach one family with the fade path and one with her usual sentence starters, to see whether the measured fade matters.

**Measures.**

| Measure | Shows |
|---|---|
| Correct formula sentences per minute, by stage and mode | Fluency and the stage decisions |
| CWS, IWS and CWS minus IWS on a 3-minute picture prompt | Sentence accuracy, scored the Datchuk way (Tier 2) |
| Untrained probe, every two weeks | Generalization beyond taught items |
| Families used and brown boxes per sentence in free writing | Transfer to Stage 6; compare with the corpus (0.57 phrases per sentence at G1; Data) |
| Self-check accuracy (did the student's code match the sentence?) | Whether the symbols work as a tool after the template is gone |
| Sessions to criterion per stage | Whether later families go faster (the predictability claim) |
| Slot 4 score in the quote paragraph, 0 to 2 | The hardest paragraph slot |

Take the baseline before anything changes. Expect taught-item gains to be larger than general gains; that is the pattern in every close trial.

## 7. Decisions for her

1. **Adopt the six-stage path** (Rebuild, Swap, Fill, Code, Choose, Free) as the one procedure for every formula, paragraph and story, with its fixed teacher language? If yes, are the stage names right?
