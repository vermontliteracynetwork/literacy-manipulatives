# Teach sentence formulas the way she teaches sounds

Her brainstorm turns into seven strands, and they need to be built in a set order. **First, define formula families: a small set of kernel sentences plus named expansion moves, with prepositional phrases as the first and biggest expansion family.** Then wrap them in one fixed daily routine, "sentence study," which copies her sound study and UFLI step for step. Snap cubes carry the families from concrete to abstract inside that routine. Oral storytelling runs alongside from the first day. Choice-based formulas with a decision-tree tool come next for Levels B and C, and paragraph formulas come last. Measurement starts first, so there is a baseline. The curriculum's own corpus makes the case for families over lists. Kernel shares stay steady from grade 1 to grade 10. Prepositional phrases per sentence rise from 0.57 to 2.41. The 2,039 sentences hold 1,811 different exact formulas, and only 26 of her listed formulas appear, in 3% of sentences. So students need the kernel and the moves, much as a word family gives a reader a rime that unlocks many words. The research supports the parts of this design: sentence combining (a meta-analytic effect of 0.50), explicit sentence construction for students with disabilities (single-case studies), explicit structure with visual coding (Shape Coding trials), guided invented spelling, and strategy instruction for paragraphs. **No study tests the whole design.** Nobody has packaged a fixed UFLI-style routine for sentence patterns, tested cubes for grammar, or tested a "pick the next sentence" tool. Those are her theory-grounded design choices, and they need a pilot. ## How to read the evidence in this report

Every research claim below carries two labels. **The tier.** Tier 1 means a meta-analysis, systematic review or RCT. Tier 2 means a quasi-experimental or single-case design. Tier 3 means correlational or descriptive. Tier 4 means practitioner voice. **The verification label** "search summary, not opened" means the finding comes from a search engine's summary of the source, so wording and figures must be checked against the original before they go into curriculum text. **Practitioner voice (Tier 4) shows what is usable and common in classrooms. It is never evidence that something works.** That includes The Writing Revolution, Keys to Literacy, IEW, Step Up to Writing, Patterns of Power, Killgallon, PEEL and product listings. "Data" means the curriculum's own hand-coded corpus: 2,039 complete sentences from reading passages at G1, G3, G5, Intermediate and G10. The corpus describes the texts students read. It does not describe what students write.

## Seven strands, built in a dependency order

The brainstorm lists her ideas in the order they came to her. Here they are reordered by what each one depends on. Formula families come first. They are the content, and every other strand acts on them: the routine teaches them, the cubes show them, oral work rehearses them, the decision tree offers them as choices, and paragraph formulas assign them to slots. The routine and the cubes are built together, because the cubes are the materials for the routine's "build it" steps. Oral storytelling does not wait for anything. It starts on day one as its own track, which the earlier research supports: dictation and oral composing let students compose ahead of their transcription skills. Choice-based work needs families that are already fluent. Paragraph formulas need both families and choice. Measurement comes before the first pilot lesson, so there is a baseline.

## Strand 1: formula families are the curriculum's word families

### 1.1 Why families instead of a formula list

**What she said.** "Maybe we need to have a system for formulas and their relationships to one another ... formula families, where groups of sentences, groups of formulas are taught explicitly, but within an applied sense," similar to word families.

**What the research says.** Two traditions back the idea of grouping sentences by a shared core. School grammar has a small set of basic sentence patterns, defined by verb type (Kolln): S-V, S-V-O, S-LV-PN (linking verb plus naming word) and S-LV-PA (linking verb plus describing word). Modifiers are layered on top. Guides disagree on whether Kolln lists 7 or 10 patterns ([U. Arkansas Pressbooks](https://uark.pressbooks.pub/journalismgsp/chapter/sentence-patterns/); [TCC sentence patterns](https://www.tcc.edu/wp-content/uploads/archive/writing-center-handouts/grammar-sentence-patterns.pdf); Tier 3, descriptive; search summary, not opened). Construction grammar treats sentence-level constructions as form-meaning pairs ([Goldberg 2003](https://legacy.cs.indiana.edu/~port/teach/sem05/Goldberg.constrctns.TrCgSci.03.pdf); Tier 3, theory; search summary, not opened). Croft recommends studying "a single construction or family of constructions" ([LLAS Good Practice Guide 141](https://web-archive.southampton.ac.uk/www.llas.ac.uk/resources/gpg/141.html); Tier 3; search summary, not opened). Her S1 to S4 line up with Kolln's first four patterns. S5 and S6 are her own additions. Neither tradition gives a teaching sequence. **No study was found showing that teaching sentences grouped by pattern improves school-age writing in a child's first language.** The family system is a theory-grounded design choice that fills a gap in the research.

The family idea also steers clear of the one clearly negative finding. Grammar taught as naming and parsing does not help writing. Three meta-analyses found negative effects on text quality, from -0.29 to -0.41 ([Koster et al., JoWR](https://jowr.org/jowr/article/download/661/627/557); Tier 1; search summary, not opened). Sentence combining was positive, ES = 0.50 across 5 studies ([Writing Next synopsis](https://www.oregon.gov/ode/educator-resources/essentialskills/Documents/essentialskillwriting_hs_level2_writing_next_synopsis.pdf); Tier 1; search summary, not opened). Families work because students act on them: they build, expand, move and join. If they only label, the family system lands in the category with negative evidence.

**What the corpus says.** This is the strongest argument in the report. The kernels are steady and the surface varies almost endlessly. With 1,811 different formulas in 2,039 sentences, students will rarely meet a listed formula in a real text. They will meet a kernel family member almost every time: S1 in 20 to 22% of sentences and S2 in 45 to 57%. Seen this way, the phonics analogy fits the structure exactly. A kernel is the rime (-at). An expansion move is a change at the edges (c-, h-, fl-). A family is the set of sentences one kernel and its moves can produce.

**Draft design.** A family is one kernel plus its expansion slots. Her listed formulas stay, but each one is filed as a member of a family ("S1.A.PRP is the S1 family with the where-move"), not as a formula to memorize. Every family has a "meaning name" for students, which is the form-meaning point from construction grammar: a doing sentence, a doing-to sentence, a being sentence.

**Open questions.** Does she accept "kernel plus moves" as the official frame, with her list as members of families? Should S6 (feel) be its own family or a member of the S3 describing family? It behaves like S3, with feel in place of be.

### 1.3 Prepositional phrases as the priority family

**What she said.** "Prepositional phrases are going to need to be a very big, important thing to teach based on the frequency of these passages."

**What the research says.** Teaching methods converge on four moves. Define prepositions explicitly. Teach the phrase as the piece that answers where or when. Act it out with objects or bodies. Move the phrase to test where it can sit ([Victoria DET example lesson](https://www.education.vic.gov.au/school/teachers/teachingresources/discipline/english/literacy/writing/Pages/exampleteachgrammar.aspx); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). California's ELD standards include expanding sentences with prepositional phrases at kindergarten and grade 1 ([CDE ELD.PII.K.5](https://www2.cde.ca.gov/cacs/id/web/7048); Tier 4, standards; search summary, not opened). The question-word expansion in The Writing Revolution, Keys to Literacy and Reading Universe is the main delivery route, because most where and when answers are prepositional phrases ([TWR Rosa Parks expansion template](https://www.thewritingrevolution.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/Rosa-Parks-Sentence-Expansion.pdf); [Reading Universe lesson plan](https://readinguniverse.org/resources/pdf/sentence-writing/sentence-expansion-skill-explainer/lesson-plan-for-sentence-expansion); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). Research on learning prepositions comes from speech-language work with children with developmental language disorder (DLD). Children with DLD struggle with locative prepositions ([Language Learning and Development 2024](https://www.informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/15475441.2024.2313226); Tier 3; search summary, not opened). An errorless-learning program has been built for above/under and before/after ([Hsu & Bishop 2014, PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/656); search summary, not opened). High variability in both objects and labels did not help preschoolers learn prepositions ([U. Arizona record](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/variability-of-input-in-preposition-learning-by-preschoolers-with/); Tier 2; search summary, not opened). A six-week kindergarten classroom program found that just saying spatial words does not improve spatial tasks. Children must use the words in ways that matter to the task ([CoDAS 2022, PMC9886288](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9886288/); Tier 2; search summary, not opened). **No controlled study of explicit prepositional phrase teaching improving written sentences was found.** This family is her design, built from speech-language and practitioner parts.

**What the corpus says.** At G1, 47% of sentences already have a prepositional phrase. At G3, the average is about one phrase per sentence (1.09). At G10, it is 2.41. Openers before the subject are only 3 to 7%, so most phrases sit after the verb or after the object. Many commas between G5 and G10 belong to phrase openers and joined clauses. This family therefore carries students from G1 texts to G10 texts more than any other single skill.

**Draft design: the prepositional phrase family in teaching order.** This is the sentence version of "lowercase first, because it appears most in print": teach the most frequent form first.

| Step | Move | Example | Why this order |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | Preposition alone, acted out with objects (in, on, under), one at a time (D-25: taught standing alone) | Put the bear in the box. Where is the bear? In. | Speech-language locative work; keep objects few at first (the input-variability finding) |
| P2 | Prepositional phrase as one piece: preposition plus its naming word (D-25: taught in a phrase) | in the box | The phrase becomes one snapped cube stick (Strand 3) |
| P3 | S5: be plus a phrase | The bear is in the box. | The phrase is the whole job of the sentence, which makes it the easiest first sentence |
| P4 | Where-phrase at the end of S1 | The dog ran to the park. (S1.A.PRP) | The most common position in print |
| P5 | Where-phrase after the object in S2 | The cat ate the fish in the kitchen. | The most common kernel (45 to 57%) |
| P6 | When-phrases (after lunch, on Monday) | We swim on Monday. | Before/after is hard for children with DLD, so teach it after locatives; days, months and holidays are PRP (her 2026-11-17 rule) |
| P7 | Word-group prepositions as one piece (D-29: because of, instead of, according to, out of) | The cat ran out of the room. | One symbol and one cube for the group |
| P8 | Two phrases in one sentence | The cat slept on the rug by the fire. | The G3 to Intermediate average is about one phrase per sentence; G10 is 2.41 |
| P9 | Phrase as an opener, with a comma (PRP,S1.A) | After lunch, the dog ran. | Rare in print (3 to 7%), so it has to be taught on purpose, like uppercase after lowercase |
| P10 | Phrase that describes a naming word | The dog with spots ran. | Teaches that a phrase can describe a noun as well as tell where |
| P11 | Speech tags with dialogue (D-27: noted like prepositional phrases) | "Run!" said Mia. | A tag rides on a sentence the way a phrase does, and it is taught together with dialogue in Strand 4 |

**Open questions.** Is P3 (S5) the right first sentence, before P4? It is the easiest, but it is a less common kernel. At what level does each step belong? Should P10 count as PRP or get its own code? D-25 also asks whether a preposition alone at the end of a clause ("We stayed in") is coded as an adverb or a preposition. The engine is waiting on that answer.

### 1.4 A family sequence that works like SATPIN

**What she said.** Sound study runs in an order "like SATPIN," then moves to words, "similar to how UFLI does it."

**What the research says.** SATPIN is ordered so that a few letters make many words quickly. Keys to Literacy gives a fixed order for building out a noun-plus-verb kernel: (1) add articles and adjectives to the subject, (2) add adverbs to the predicate, (3) add a phrase, (4) combine sentences ("turtle dives" becomes "The small, green turtle dives quickly") ([Nebraska DOE, Syntactic Awareness](https://www.education.ne.gov/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/syntactic-awareness.pdf); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). Shape Coding's strongest results are on argument structure, the "who did what to whom" skeleton ([Balthazar, Ebbels & Zwitserlood review](https://researchgate.net/profile/Rob-Zwitserlood/publication/340512173_Explicit_Grammatical_Intervention_for_Developmental_Language_Disorder_Three_Approaches/links/63bc0f8203aad5368e78baca/Explicit-Grammatical-Intervention-for-Developmental-Language-Disorder-Three-Approaches.pdf); Tier 1, small RCTs; search summary, not opened). That supports teaching the kernels before the expansions. Datchuk's work teaches the simple sentence to fluency before anything else ([Datchuk 2017, Iowa Research Online](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-Direct-Instruction-and-Precision-Teaching/9983993324302771); Tier 2; search summary, not opened).

**Draft design: "SATPIN for sentences."** As with SATPIN, the goal is to generate many sentences from very few cards as early as possible.

| Order | Family or move | New cards needed | Sentences students can now make |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | S1 with names (Maya runs.) | names, 3 to 4 verbs | every name times every verb |
| 2 | Article plus noun subject (S1.A) | a, the, 4 nouns | the number doubles |
| 3 | PRP-where at the end (P1 to P4) | in, on, under, 3 place nouns | each sentence times each phrase |
| 4 | S2 (doing to) | 3 object nouns | the most common kernel in print |
| 5 | J (describing word) | 4 adjectives | |
| 6 | Tense on the verb (past, present, future clock cards) | tense symbols | every sentence in three times |
| 7 | S3, S4, S5 (being families) | is, are, was | |
| 8 | B (how word) | 3 adverbs | |
| 9 | PRP-when, then two phrases | after, before, on (days) | |
| 10 | Q, C (command), E changes | punctuation cards | |
| 11 | Joining with and, but, so | conjunction cards | |
| 12 | Openers with a comma (PRP, and B,) | the comma card | |
| 13 | Because and when parts (C[ ]) | because, when | |

**Open questions.** Should tense come at step 6 or with the first kernel? Her kindergarten session used clock cards from the start. Is S6 part of step 7? Where does she want pronouns (P) to enter?

## Strand 2: sentence study runs the same steps every day

### 2.1 Mapping her sound study and UFLI onto sentence study

**What she said.** Her preschool "sound study," used instead of letter of the week, runs the same way every time. Explore how the sound feels in the mouth. Talk about words with that sound, especially students' names. Make a word list with drawn pictures. Look at flashcards and real objects. Then see the grapheme for the first time (lowercase first, then uppercase). Write and practice it. Do reinforcing activities. Review prior letters every day. Then apply to words. "I want writing to feel like that. I want symbolic grammar to feel like that, where it is procedurally similar every time."

**What the research says.** UFLI Foundations runs an 8-step lesson. Steps 1 to 4 are warm-up and review. Step 5 introduces a new concept explicitly. Steps 6 to 8 apply it to words and connected text, and Step 8 (about 15 minutes) includes reading sentences and a dictated sentence, such as "Tom fed the big pet." ([UFLI Implementation Support](https://ufli.education.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/UFLIFoundations_Implementation_Support.pdf); [UFLI pocket reference](https://ufli.education.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/UFLI-foundations-pocket-reference.pdf); Tier 4, program description; search summary, not opened). Structured literacy treats brief cumulative review as essential ([Lexia](https://www.lexialearning.com/blog/how-to-design-a-structured-literacy-block); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). Explicit instruction includes review, modeling, examples and non-examples, guided practice with frequent responses, and feedback ([Kentucky DOE](https://www.education.ky.gov/curriculum/EarlyLiteracy/Documents/Structured_Literacy_Grounded_in_the_Science_of_Reading.pdf); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). The closest research-based sentence routine is Datchuk's explicit sentence-writing block of 10 to 15 minutes, which fits any ELA core. It comes with an eight-step fluency routine that uses picture-word prompts and opens with a review of prior correct writing sequences ([EdWeek](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/young-writers-need-structure-to-learn-the-craft-how-much-is-enough/2023/01); Tier 2 line of work, reported in the press; search summary, not opened). Patterns of Power runs a fixed weekly sequence: notice, compare, imitate, then apply and edit ([Zaner-Bloser](https://zaner-bloser.com/writing/patterns-of-power/how-it-works); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). Killgallon's four operations are unscramble, imitate, combine and expand ([Killgallon, Definition](https://userpages.umbc.edu/~killgall/definition.php); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). A cueing procedure that has the child produce the correct form after an error beat recasting in a trial with 31 five-year-olds with language impairment ([LSHSS 2015](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2015_LSHSS-14-0041); Tier 1, small trial; search summary, not opened). **No program was found that applies a fixed, UFLI-like daily routine to sentence patterns.** Sentence study fills that gap. Every step is borrowed from a documented routine. The whole package is new.

**Draft design: sentence study, step by step.**

| Step | Her sound study | UFLI | Sentence study (example: P4, a where-phrase at the end of S1) | Source of the step |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Review | Every day, review prior letters | Steps 1 to 4, warm-up and review | Rebuild yesterday's sentences with cubes or cards; quick-read the symbol cards; say each family's meaning name | Cumulative review; Datchuk's review start |
| 2. Feel it | How the sound feels in the mouth | (none) | Act out the move with the body and real objects: put the bear in the box, walk to the door. "Where did it go?" | Embodied work (Moved by Reading); speech-language spatial terms |
| 3. Talk it | Words with the sound, students' names | (none) | Make oral sentences about the class, using names: "Maya walks to the door." Everyone says the full sentence | Oral rehearsal; the cueing result (the child says it) |
| 4. Collect it | Word list with drawn pictures | (none) | Make a class sentence chart, with a drawing for each sentence and the phrase circled | Language Experience-style chart |
| 5. Look at it | Flashcards and real objects | (none) | Picture cards and objects; sort examples and non-examples ("in the box" is a where-phrase; "the box" alone is not) | Explicit instruction: examples and non-examples |
| 6. Meet the symbol | See the grapheme for the first time, lowercase first | Step 5, new concept | Meet the preposition symbol and the PRP code. Start with the most common form (phrase at the end); the opener form comes later | Explicit introduction; frequency-first order from the corpus |
| 7. Build and write it | Write and practice the letter | Step 6, word work | Cubes, then symbol cards, then code, then write the sentence with supported spelling (Strand 3) | CRA; guided invented spelling |
| 8. Play with it | Reinforcing activities | Step 7 | Unscramble it, imitate a mentor sentence from a passage, expand a kernel with a where-question, move the phrase | Killgallon; TWR question-word expansion; Victoria's move-the-phrase |
| 9. Apply it in text | Apply to words | Step 8, connected text and dictation | Read a short passage and find the family; take one dictated sentence in the family; say and build it once more to close | UFLI Step 8; cueing |

The fixed part is the order of the steps and the language used at each one. The new family or move is what changes. Following UFLI's pacing, steps 1 and 9 never drop out. Steps 2 to 5 can shrink to a few minutes once students know the routine. A new family takes several days, as a sound does in sound study. One family per one- or two-week cycle is a reasonable starting guess, to be piloted.

**Open questions.** How many minutes does she want: a 10 to 15 minute block like Datchuk's, or longer for Pre-A? Does a new move get one day or a week? What should the routine's fixed words be at each step (for example, "Feel it. Say it. See it. Build it. Write it.")?

### 2.2 What her kindergarten session already shows

**What she said.** Today a kindergarten student played with a worksheet of four statement-sentence formulas, using word flashcards and the clock cards for past, present and future. Spelling was supported about 75% of the time.

**What the research says.** Three findings bear on this. First, in a study of 245 children aged 3 to 5, those with stronger transcription skills wrote sentences closer to the oral sentence they planned ([search result citing Quinn and colleagues, Springer 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10643-026-02207-7); Tier 3, correlational; search summary, not opened; author and year unconfirmed). Second, guided invented spelling helps. The child tries, and the adult gives feedback a step above the attempt. In an RCT with 56 kindergartners who had weak phoneme awareness, children trained this way learned to read more words than children in phoneme segmentation or storybook reading groups ([Sénéchal et al., Reading and Writing](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-011-9310-2/fulltext.html); Tier 1, small RCT; search summary, not opened). Third, for tense, the best evidence is Shape Coding plus a cueing hierarchy for past tense with 5- and 6-year-olds with DLD. It found a large effect on taught past tense that lasted 5 weeks, **but no gain on standardized grammar measures.** The reported d is 3.03 in one summary and 1.22 in another, unreconciled. The developer's employer sells the method ([Calder et al., JSLHR 2020](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2020_JSLHR-20-00132); Tier 1, crossover RCT; search summary, not opened). **No study tested clock cards or timelines alone for tense in kindergarten.** Researchers say sentence construction in the early elementary grades is still a research gap ([Early Childhood Education Journal 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10643-026-02207-7); search summary, not opened).

**What this means.** Her session is ahead of the research base, so her own records will be the main evidence. Three refinements follow. Record which words were supported, not just the percentage, and fade support one word at a time as phonics grows; no study sets an ideal percentage. Pair each clock card (when) with a tense mark on the verb card itself (the -ed ending, or will plus the verb), because the evidence points to marking the word ending, not only the time. And end each sentence with the child saying and building the whole correct sentence.

## Strand 3: snap cubes make the phrase one piece

**What she said.** Use snap cubes and concrete-representational-abstract (CRA) with the formulas.

**What the research says.** CRA is an evidence-based practice for students with learning disabilities in math ([Bouck, Satsangi & Park 2018 listing](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/a-meta-analytic-review-of-the-concrete-representational-abstract-/); Tier 1, synthesis; search summary, not opened). A 2025 meta-analysis of 30 single-case math studies found a large overall effect. Integrated CRA (all three stages shown together) and separate-phase CRA differed by only 0.011, a negligible amount ([UKY scholars listing](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/a-meta-analytic-review-of-the-concrete-representational-abstract-/); Tier 1; search summary, not opened). The core move is to fade the objects and drawings "until students work with symbols" ([Flores, Hinton & Schweck 2014](https://simvilledev.ku.edu/sites/default/files/Video%20Resources/Flores_Hinton_Schweck_2014.pdf); search summary, not opened). **Nothing in the CRA research tests grammar or writing.** The literacy tools closest to it are Elkonin boxes, where children move a marker per sound and letters are added later ([UFLI Elkonin handout](https://ufli.education.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Using-Elkonin-Boxes-Handout.pdf); Tier 2 small studies; search summary, not opened), and Shape Coding, which encloses phrases in shapes and marks word classes in color ([Balthazar, Ebbels & Zwitserlood](https://researchgate.net/profile/Rob-Zwitserlood/publication/340512173_Explicit_Grammatical_Intervention_for_Developmental_Language_Disorder_Three_Approaches/links/63bc0f8203aad5368e78baca/Explicit-Grammatical-Intervention-for-Developmental-Language-Disorder-Three-Approaches.pdf); search summary, not opened). Moved by Reading has children act out sentences with toys, then imagine the toys, and reports large comprehension gains ([Glenberg, Improving reading](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~jerryzhu/pub/Improvingreading.pdf); Tier 2; search summary, not opened). That supports an "imagined" middle step. Gestures should match the word's job, because in adults a gesture that does not match the meaning interferes with learning ([Frontiers in Communication 2024](https://frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2024.1372033/pdf); search summary, not opened). Sentence cubes, linking cubes and word bricks exist only as products and practitioner activities, with no outcome studies ([This Reading Mama](https://thisreadingmama.com/writing-sentences-activity/); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). For safety, anything that fits the federal small-parts cylinder (1.25 in diameter) is a choking hazard for children under 3 and for students who mouth objects ([CPSC small parts](https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Business-Education/Business-Guidance/Small-Parts-For-Toys-And-Childrens-Products); search summary, not opened).

**What the corpus says.** Phrases are what grow, so the material should make a phrase visible as a single, movable unit. Openers are rare (3 to 7%), and sliding a phrase stick from the end to the front shows that choice physically.

**Draft design: the CRA progression.**

| Stage | What the student does | Prepositional phrase example: "The dog ran to the park." |
|---|---|---|
| C1: real objects | Act out the sentence with toys and the body | A toy dog moves to a toy park |
| C2: one cube per word | One cube per word, in the symbol color, laid out in sentence order. Cube count equals word count. Word groups get one cube (D-29: will run, such as; D-28: cricket bat is one cube, ocean sand is two) | 6 cubes: article, noun, verb, preposition, article, noun |
| C3: snap the phrase | Snap the phrase cubes into one rigid stick, with an optional sleeve or band in the preposition color (a physical "shape," as in Shape Coding). Slide the stick to the front and add a comma card to show an opener | "to the park" becomes one stick; it moves as one piece and never breaks apart |
| C4: code under the train | A code strip under the cubes (S1.A.PRP); this is the integrated option, since integration made no measurable difference in math | The strip reads S1.A.PRP |
| R1: cards over cubes | Symbol cards placed on the cubes, then the cards alone | The preposition card sits on the stick |
| R2: draw the symbols | Write the sentence, then draw the symbols above the words (Montessori style), with a bracket over the phrase | A bracket over "to the park" |
| Imagined | Tap a finger per word, and tap-and-slide for the phrase; "build it in your head" | 4 taps for The dog ran, then 1 slide for the phrase |
| A1: code to sentence | Given S1.A.PRP, write a new sentence | The cat sat on the mat. |
| A2: sentence to code | Given a written sentence from a passage, write its code | Students code a real G1 passage sentence |

Tense rides on the verb cube. A clock card placed beside the train shows when, and a tense mark clips onto the verb cube to show the word ending, much as Shape Coding uses arrows. A compound sentence is two trains joined by a conjunction cube. A complex sentence puts the because-train in a bracket tray.

**Fading criterion.** No research source gives a mastery level for moving from one CRA stage to the next. Set one locally, such as several correct independent builds on separate days, and record it as a curriculum decision, not a research figure.

**Access and OT.** Use cubes wider than 1.25 inches for any student who mouths objects. Pair color with shape so color-blind students are not shut out, as Montessori and Shape Coding both do. Offer alternatives: large blocks, magnetic or Velcro tiles, a slotted tray (Elkonin-style boxes), digital drag tiles in Literacy Manipulatives, or matching gestures. Some students seek or avoid the click of snap cubes. These are design inferences to confirm with the school OT.

**Open questions.** Should cube colors match the symbol colors exactly? Should the phrase sleeve be the preposition's color or a neutral "phrase" color? Is the phrase stick also used for adjective-plus-noun noun phrases? It would teach the same "one piece" idea, but it could blur the PRP lesson.

## Strand 4: oral storytelling carries the story while the pen catches up

**What she said.** Stay rooted in the mission: explicit before creative and artistic, with oral storytelling while the foundations form.

**What the research says.** The earlier work in this project found that dictation lets students with learning disabilities write longer, better texts (Gillespie & Graham's dictation ES = 0.55; Tier 1). This round adds detail on how oral work carries over to writing. "Oral rehearsal" is common in classrooms but "not well-theorised" ([Myhill 2009, PhilArchive record](https://dc2.philarchive.org/rec/MYHHTB-2); Tier 3, review and classroom video; search summary, not opened). Narrative intervention has moderate pooled effects on oral narrative: d = 0.51 and 0.54 in group designs and 1.24 in single-case designs ([LSHSS 2021](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2021_LSHSS-20-00160); Tier 1; search summary, not opened). Transfer to writing is small. A whole-class Story Champs replication had limited written results and concluded that oral and written narrative teaching may need to be combined ([Davison SRCLD 2020 poster](https://uwo.ca/fhs/lwm/files/srcld_poster/Davison_SRCLD2020.pdf); Tier 2; search summary, not opened). Oral language interventions for children with neurodevelopmental disorders average d = 0.27, with publication bias flagged ([Donolato et al., Campbell 2023](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10184356/1/Campbell%20Systematic%20Reviews%20-%202023%20-%20Donolato%20-%20Oral%20language%20interventions%20can%20improve%20language%20outcomes%20in%20children%20with.pdf); Tier 1; search summary, not opened). For complex syntax in children aged 4 to 8, recasts, expansions and cloze worked better than direct imitation ([ASHA Evidence Map](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/97f52286-4d3e-46bd-a21b-12455934bf9a); Tier 1, review; search summary, not opened). Interactive writing ("sharing the pen"), about 10 minutes a day, improved first graders' independent writing ([Education Week 2012](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/sharing-the-pen-helps-first-graders-learn-to-write-study-says/2012/02); Tier 2, quasi-experimental; search summary, not opened). In kindergarten it did no better than Writing Workshop ([Jones, Reutzel & Fargo, USU](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/teal_facpub/576); Tier 2; search summary, not opened). The IES foundational skills guide recommends explicitly teaching narrative language and grammatical structures ([WWC Practice Guide 21](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/PracticeGuide/21); search summary, not opened). The pattern across all of this: **oral work carries over to writing when it is tied directly to a written product in the same sitting.** It does not carry over on its own.

**What the corpus says.** Questions (1 to 6%), commands (2 to 4%) and exclamations (0 to 5%) are rare in reading texts but common in told stories and dialogue. The oral track is the natural home for Q, C and E, and for speech tags (D-27).

**Draft design: two connected parts.**

The **oral-first loop**, run in one sitting for every sentence a child composes:

1. **Say it.** The child says the sentence, with the cubes or cards in front of them.
2. **Build it.** One cube per word; snap the phrase.
3. **Tap it.** Touch and count the words. The count is the plan.
4. **Write it.** Supported spelling: the child tries the sounds they know, and the adult or a word card supplies the rest.
5. **Check it.** Reread aloud, touching each cube. Did every word make it onto the paper?
6. **Grow it.** Add a where or when phrase orally ("Where did he run?"), then rebuild. The adult expands; the child says the new sentence.

The **storytelling track** runs alongside and is never a waiting room. Students tell stories every week, with story-part icons (character, setting, problem, plan, attempt, outcome) and dictation to an adult or a tool. Each week, one sentence moves from the story into the oral-first loop, so the story feeds the formula work and the formula work feeds the story. The formula families give the oral track its sentence-level structure: the setting line is usually S5 or S1 plus a where-phrase ("A fox lived in the woods"), the feeling line is S6 ("The fox felt sad"), and the dialogue lines are Q, C or E with a speech tag. That gives Story Champs-style icons the written-sentence bridge the replication says they lack.

**Open questions.** Who scribes, and how does dictation fade? The research does not say. Should story shapes from more than one culture be included, given the critique that story grammar reflects a Western structure (noted in the earlier report)? At what level does a told story start being written?

## Strand 5: Levels B and C choose, and the decision tree makes choice visible

**What she said.** "Especially older students, level B and level C students, teaching formulas in a choice-based sense is going to be very important." She also described a future Literacy Manipulatives tool: a decision tree where students select a sentence and options for what could come next appear, "and so on into the story plot type sense."

**What the research says.** Grammar taught as a choice with effects on the reader worked in an RCT with 744 Year 8 students (ES = 0.21). The benefit went mainly to able writers and was neutral for below-average writers (earlier report; Tier 1). So choice needs extra scaffolding for the learners this curriculum serves first. Interactive fiction in education has a systematic review of 63 articles ([Wright & Weible 2024](https://doi.org/10.31756/jrsmte.713); Tier 1 review of mostly descriptive work; search summary, not opened). Choose-your-own-adventure writing is described as strengthening writing instruction ([Batchelor & Thompson 2022, Miami University](https://miamioh.edu/ehs/centers-institutes/create/publications/literacy-innovation-multimodality-digital-composition/digital-choose-your-own-adventure-writing.html); Tier 3; search summary, not opened). Teachers report that it "helps students recognize the different aspects of plot" ([Edutopia](https://www.edutopia.org/blog/new-tools-interactive-fiction-engaged-writing-matthew-farber/); Tier 4, practitioner voice; search summary, not opened). Story Champs adapted for children who use AAC worked in a proof-of-concept study, and the authors note that 60 to 90% of AAC interventions target requests rather than narrative ([KU Life Span Institute](https://lifespan.ku.edu/news/article/using-aac-with-storytelling-boosts-literacy-skills-in-proof-of-concept-study); Tier 3; search summary, not opened). **No study was found of a branching "pick the next sentence" tool for any population.** The tool is her design, and it fills that gap.

**What the corpus says.** Authors almost never repeat a formula (unique ratio 0.84 at G1, 0.99 at G10). Real writing is a run of choices, and the decision tree makes those choices visible one sentence at a time.

**Draft design: choice-based formulas at B and C.** Students stop asking "write an S2 sentence" and start asking "which family does this moment need?" Each choice has a reason students can say: a where-phrase sets the scene, a because part gives the reason, a question pulls in the reader, an opener changes the rhythm. Offer two or three options at a time, with symbols, to match AAC practice and manage working memory.

**Draft design: decision-tree data shape.** In Literacy Manipulatives, each node is one sentence tagged with its formula code (taken from the Symbolic Grammar Engine, the only shared piece), its family, its story part and its tense. The tool's code stays in Literacy Manipulatives.

```
StoryNode {
  id: "n3"
  sentence: "The fox ran into the dark woods."
  formula: "S1.A.PRP"            // code from the Symbolic Grammar Engine
  family: "S1"                    // kernel family
  moves: ["J", "PRP"]             // expansion moves used
  storyPart: "attempt"            // setting | problem | plan | attempt | outcome | ending
  tense: "past"                   // past | present | future
  next: ["n4", "n5", "n6"]        // 2 to 3 choices, never more
  choiceHint: "What happens in the woods?"
  studentWritten: false           // true when the student adds their own option
}
```

Branch points belong at the problem and attempt nodes, where practitioners say plot awareness forms. The tool fades in three stages. (1) Choose from given sentences. (2) Choose a formula, then write the sentence yourself. (3) Write without the tree and label your own sentences with codes, as a self-check in the SRSD style. A "pattern breaker" mode at Level C shows two versions of the same moment in different families and asks which works better here.

**Open questions.** Do Levels B and C share one tree with different fade stages, or does each level get its own story bank? Should the tree enforce a story-part order or allow free branching? Who writes the starter story banks?

## Strand 6: paragraph formulas assign a formula to each job

**What she said.** "We're going to have paragraph formulas": sentence formulas that support paragraph structures and frameworks, with recommended formulas or word structures.

**What the research says.** The major frameworks share one shape: a fixed opening or topic slot, detail slots and an ending slot. The Writing Revolution's Single-Paragraph Outline has a topic sentence, detail notes and a concluding sentence ([TWR](https://thewritingrevolution.org/?p=64186); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). Step Up to Writing color-codes the slots: green for the topic, yellow for reasons, red for explanation, and "one red for each yellow" by year's end ([Albany OR outline](https://albany.k12.or.us/media/2016/04/Step-Up-to-Writing-Outline.pdf); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). IEW adds one style element at a time ([IEW blog](https://iew.com/support/blog/jelping-reluctant-writers-with-style); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). TIDE (Topic, Important evidence, Detail, Ending) is scored 0 to 2 per part ([Writing Architect](https://writingarchitect2.azurewebsites.net/tideResources.html); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). The stronger evidence is for the categories behind these frameworks. In grades 1 to 5, strategy instruction has ES = 1.02 and text structure instruction ES = 0.59 ([ERIC ED659639](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED659639.pdf); Tier 1; search summary, not opened). SRSD with POW plus TREE has single-case support with autistic students ([Asaro-Saddler & Bak, SUNY](https://researchconnect.suny.edu/en/publications/teaching-children-with-high-functioning-autism-spectrum-disorders/); Tier 2; search summary, not opened). Research also flags overuse and misuse of transition words, not just missing ones ([IAFOR 2025](https://papers.iafor.org/wp-content/uploads/papers/iice2025/IICE2025_88234.pdf?t=8); Tier 3; search summary, not opened). Critics, such as Warner, call fixed forms a "straitjacket" that replaces thinking with imitation ([Education Week 2016](https://edweek.org/teaching-learning/is-the-five-paragraph-essay-history/2016/06); [Project MUSE, Why They Can't Write](https://muse.jhu.edu/book/61976); Tier 4; search summary, not opened). **No program was found that assigns grammatical formulas to paragraph slots by rule.** Programs assign sentence jobs only. Symbolic Grammar's paragraph formulas would be new, a design choice that fills that gap.

**What the corpus says.** The corpus counts sentences, not paragraphs, so it cannot test paragraph structure. It does show that openers are rare (3 to 7%). Time-order openers in story paragraphs ("After lunch,") give PRP,S1.A a natural job, which is a hypothesis to check by coding whole passages by sentence position.

**Draft design: paragraph formulas.**

| Paragraph | Slot | Recommended formula family | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Story (B) | Opening: who and where | S5 or S1 plus a where-phrase | A fox lived in the woods. |
| | Problem | S2 or S1 plus a because part | The fox lost its den because the river rose. |
| | Attempt, with a time order | PRP,S1 opener plus an action | After a long night, the fox dug a new den. |
| | Dialogue (D-27) | Q, C or E plus a speech tag | "Who is there?" asked the owl. |
| | Outcome and feeling | S6 | The fox felt safe. |
| Informational (B, C) | Topic | S3 or S4 (being families) | Frogs are amphibians. |
| | Detail 1 and 2 | S2 plus PRP, or a because/but/so join | Frogs lay eggs in ponds. Frogs swallow food whole, so they do not chew. |
| | Ending (restated in new words) | S3 or S6 | Frogs are amazing animals. |
| Opinion (C) | Point | S3 with should | Recess should be longer. |
| | Reasons | Because parts, (S)C[S] | Kids focus better because they move. |
| | Call to action | C (command) | Ask your principal for more recess. |

Pair each paragraph formula with an SRSD-style planning routine and a self-check ("Did I use at least two different families?"), because strategy instruction is the larger lever (1.02 versus 0.59). Teach transition words by their job (time order, reason, contrast, adding), attached to a slot, not as a long list. To answer the critics, present every paragraph formula as a starting recipe with named "upgrades": vary the families, merge two details, move the topic sentence, end with a question, and finally let students design their own paragraph formula.

**Open questions.** Is "paragraph formula" a Level B entry point or a Level C one? Should paragraph formulas get codes of their own (for example, a story paragraph coded as S5, S2, PRP,S1, S6)? Should the ending ever restate the topic, given the critique?

## Strand 7: measure sentences the way Datchuk does

**What she said.** The brainstorm names no measures, but every strand above is a new design that needs a pilot.

**What the research says.** Datchuk's studies score correct word sequences, incorrect word sequences and correct-minus-incorrect sequences, plus the number of complete simple sentences ([Datchuk 2017](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-Direct-Instruction-and-Precision-Teaching/9983993324302771); Tier 2; search summary, not opened). Allen and Datchuk (2026) scored use of sentence elements (noun, verb, adjective), length, accuracy and complexity, with planning steps, a self-checklist and word banks as supports ([LSHSS 2026](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2026_LSHSS-25-00149); Tier 2, n = 1; search summary, not opened). Researcher-made measures show larger effects than general ones ([UCC repository, 2025 meta-analysis](https://cora.ucc.ie/bitstreams/d8c8369f-a1bb-4ed3-9442-972f44ba33c6/download); Tier 1; search summary, not opened). Tense gains in Calder et al. were large on taught items but absent on standardized measures, so untrained probes matter.

**Draft design.** One 3-minute picture-prompt probe, given before the first lesson and then every two weeks, scored the same way every time (see Pilot measures below). Add one general measure, such as an untrained picture or a story retell, so the pilot does not count only what was taught.

## Conclusion

The brainstorm holds together better than it first looks, because every idea in it is the same idea at a different scale. Sound study, formula families, snap cubes, the decision tree and paragraph formulas all take a small, stable core and teach the moves that change it, the same way every time. The corpus shows that this is how real texts are built. The kernel is set by grade 1, and the rest is phrases, joins and choices. That makes the prepositional phrase family the backbone, not one unit among many. It is the move students will meet most often in their reading, and the one that most separates G1 text from G10 text.

The honest risk is the one every strand shares. The parts are borrowed from evidence, but the whole has never been tested, and the closest trials show taught-item gains that do not always reach general measures. Her advantage is that she is already running the pilot: one kindergartner, four formulas, clock cards and supported spelling. If that pilot is scored the Datchuk way from this week on, Symbolic Grammar will have data the field does not yet have. That data would show whether a sentence routine that works like sound study helps students write sentences.
