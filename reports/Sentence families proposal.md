# Sentence families: teach formulas that can stand in for each other

Prepared by Beth, 2026-11-21, in answer to her request of the same day: "How are we going to use this instructionally? How can we create new instructional procedures, such as creating sentence families that focus on groups of formulas that can work interchangeably, such as the sentences that I selected for my kindergartner."

**How to read this.** This is a design proposal, not a literature review. "Data" means her hand-checked corpus: 2,039 complete sentences from five reading-passage books, grades 1 to 10, with every word's symbol checked by hand. The counts below are automatic summaries over those symbols, so treat them as approximate. The few research claims are carried over from the earlier reports in this folder and keep their labels: every one is a **search summary, not opened**, with its tier (Tier 1 meta-analysis, systematic review or RCT; Tier 2 quasi-experimental or single-case; Tier 3 correlational, descriptive or theory; Tier 4 practitioner voice). Example sentences marked "her list" come from her_formula_list.json. Anything marked "my coding" is mine and should be run through the Symbolic Grammar Engine before use.

## Summary

A **sentence family** is a set of her formulas that share everything except one slot. Because only one slot changes, the members can be swapped for each other, the way word families share a rime and swap the first sound (-at: cat, hat, sat). Her kindergarten worksheet was very likely the first family: her Lesson 1.1 lists exactly four ways to fill the who slot of S1 (S1.A "The dog ran.", S1 "Dogs run.", S1.P "She runs.", S1.PN "Sam ran."), and the data shows those four cover about 76% of the subjects in real simple statements. Adding a describing word to the subject brings that to about 91%.

The proposal has four parts: a family principle with one rule (one slot changes, and the slot is lit), a catalog of eight families built from her list and the data, seven routines that use the same teacher words every time and plug into the six-stage fade path (Rebuild, Swap, Fill, Code, Choose, Free), and a Pre-A to C sequence. By the data, the families that matter most are **Who**, **Where/When** and **Object**, because almost every real sentence uses all three. **Time** matters because every verb carries it. The **Kind** family matters least by frequency: questions, negatives, commands and exclamations together are under 13% of the corpus. It is still taught early because it is the simplest way to show that one kernel can be changed.

## 1. The family principle

### 1.1 One slot changes, everything else stays

| | Word family | Sentence family |
|---|---|---|
| What stays the same | The rime: -at | The kernel and every slot but one: ___ ran. |
| What changes | The onset: c, h, s, fl | One slot: The dog / Dogs / She / Sam |
| What the student learns | A new word can be read by changing one sound | A new sentence can be written by changing one job |
| What is visible | The rime is written once, the onset card moves | The symbol strip is fixed, one symbol is lit and its word card moves |
| What it is not | A list of unrelated words | A list of unrelated formulas |

The Who family on a strip, with her symbols:

| Slot | who (lit) | does (fixed) | end (fixed) |
|---|---|---|---|
| S1.A | pink triangle + red pointing hand: The dog | green four-way arrows, past tense symbol: ran | . |
| S1 | red pointing hand: Dogs | green four-way arrows, past: ran | . |
| S1.P | yellow circling arrows: She | green four-way arrows, past: ran | . |
| S1.PN | red crown: Sam | green four-way arrows, past: ran | . |

### 1.2 Three differences from word families that change how we teach

1. **Her codes already name the swap.** In the Who family the letter after S1 is the changing slot: .A, nothing, .P, .PN. Students can see the family in the code itself, which no word family offers. This is the strongest reason to use families: the code becomes a picture of the swap.
2. **A swap can drag a second change with it.** In the present tense, "Dogs run" and "She runs" differ in the verb as well as the who (subject and verb agreement). In the past tense they do not: The dog ran, Dogs ran, She ran, Sam ran. Her own Lesson 1.1 examples mix past and present. **I propose teaching the Who family in the past tense first**, so only one slot moves, and then showing the -s appear and disappear in the present as a planned Who x Time mix (Section 3.5). This keeps the one-slot rule honest.
3. **Her codes list the parts, not their places.** "She ate the apple." and "The dog licked me." are both S2.A.P (her list), even though the pronoun is the who in one and the what in the other. So the code alone cannot say which slot moved. Each family strip needs a slot label (who, does, what, where, when, how) above the symbols, and the engine would need a slot marker to sort sentences into families automatically (Decision 5).

### 1.3 How sentence families relate to the kernel families in the earlier report

*Formula families and sentence expansion* proposed families by kernel: S1 doing, S2 doing to, S3 to S6 being and feeling, with moves added. That is the vertical view: one kernel, growing. Sentence families are the horizontal view: one formula, one slot swapped across its members. They fit together. A kernel family is the house; sentence families are the rooms where students practice. In practice the teacher teaches inside a sentence family until the slot is fluent, then the next move opens the next family.

There are two kinds of sentence family, and students should hear the difference:

- **Swap families** (Who, Object, Where/When, Be, Join): one slot keeps its place and the word in it changes.
- **Change families** (Time, Kind, Describe): one slot is changed in form, added, or the sentence is turned, but only one change is made at a time. Time changes the verb's tense symbol (and sometimes adds a helping verb). Describe adds one card to an empty slot. Kind changes the end mark and, for questions and commands, moves or removes one card.

The research base supports the shape of this without testing it directly. Caregivers speak in "variation sets," neighboring utterances that repeat part and change part, and items in those sets show up earlier in children's speech (PMC6174075; Tier 3; search summary, not opened). Learners with low working memory gained from repeated, low-variety input (Pulido 2024, SSLA; Tier 2; search summary, not opened). A sentence family is a variation set built on purpose. **No study was found that teaches sentence families for writing**, so this is a design choice, and her records will be the evidence.

## 2. The family catalog

Levels: Pre-A (oral, built and pointed, no writing required), A (first written simple sentences with full symbol support), B (families named, moves combined, codes read), C (choosing among families for a purpose, joined and complex sentences).

### 2.1 Who family (the subject slot)

**Fixed:** ___ ran. **Changing slot:** who.

| Order | Member | Code | Example | Share of subjects in simple statements (Data, 1,265) | Level |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Name | S1.PN | Sam ran. (her list) | 11% | Pre-A |
| 2 | Article + noun | S1.A | The dog ran. (her list) | 10% | Pre-A |
| 3 | Pronoun | S1.P | She runs. (her list); She ran. (my coding) | 43% | Pre-A to A |
| 4 | Noun alone (plural or mass) | S1 | Dogs run. (her list) | 12% | A |
| 5 | Noun with a describing word | S1.A.J | The small dog ran. (her list) | 15% | A |
| 6 | my/their + noun | S1.P | Their dog barks. (her list) | 3% | A |
| 7 | Owner word | S1.PN's, S1.A.N's | Sam's dog ran. The dog's bone fell. (her list) | in "other" counts | B |
| 8 | Compound subject | S1.PN.PN.C, S1.PN.P.C, S1.A.A.C | Sam and Mia ran. Sam and I ran. The cat and the dog ran. (her list) | 5% | B |
| 9 | There is / It is | my coding | There is a dog. | 1% | C |

**Why this order.** Frequency alone would start with the pronoun (43%, and 54% of subjects at G1). But a pronoun only makes sense once the child knows who it stands for, so the order starts with the two most concrete members (a name the child knows, then "the" + a picture noun), and brings the pronoun in third as a replacement for them: "Sam ran. He ran." The bare plural (Dogs run.) comes fourth because it is the member that triggers the verb change in the present tense. Pronoun share stays high across grades (G1 54%, G3 45%, G5 40%, Intermediate 33%, G10 54%; Data), so the pronoun member is never "done"; it recurs in every family after this one.

**Note on her code.** "Their dog barks." is S1.P in her list, so in her coding the pronoun member already includes my/their + noun, and her four cover about 79% of subjects, not 76%.

So the three-clock routine is right for teaching what tense is, but practice time should weight past and present. That gives students a clean rule: "If you add a helper, the code gets a V."

**Why this order.** In and on come first because they can be acted out with objects (speech-language locative work, carried over from the earlier report; Tier 3; search summary, not opened). "To" is the most frequent but its count mixes "to the park" with "to run," so only the place use is taught at A; "to" before a verb belongs to the Object family at C. "Of" is second by count but abstract ("one of them," "a cup of milk"), so it waits until B.

**Note on her code.** Lesson 1.1 writes S1.PN.R for "Luna lives in Texas." and "Max swims on Friday."; later lessons write PRP. I have assumed R and PRP mean the same and listed it under Decision 5.

Feel is rare in reading text, but it is the sentence students most need for talking about feelings, so it earns its place by job, not by count.

| 7 | if | C[S1.P], | If it rains, we will stay inside. (her list) | 50 | C |
| 8 | that | (S2.P)C[S3] | I think that dogs are smart. (her list) | 209 | C |

| 10 | Join part first, with a comma | C[S1.A], | When the bell rang, the dog barked. (her list) | | C |

Her code splits this family in two: and, but, so and or join equals with (X)C; when, because, if and that make a leaning part with brackets (X)C[Y]. Teach the equal joins as one sub-family first, then the bracket joins. By frequency "that" is second (209), but most of its uses ("I think that...", "the dog that ran") are Level C work, so it is not taught early. "Because" is less frequent in reading text (42) but is the join students need most for opinion and explanation writing; frequency and purpose disagree here, and I have followed purpose for its level. Sentence combining is the best-supported sentence method in the research (Graham and Perin 2007, ES 0.50, grades 4 to 12; Tier 1; search summary, not opened), so this family carries the most evidence of the eight.

That is the strongest case for teaching families rather than formulas one at a time.

## 3. Procedures that use families

Every routine opens with the same family language, so the routine stays predictable when the family is new: **"This is the ___ family. Everything stays. Only the ___ changes."** The words are my draft and are hers to change.

### 3.1 Family Introduction

- **Purpose:** show every member at once, with the changing slot lit.
- **Materials:** one long family strip (symbol cards in a row), one lit slot (a colored frame or a highlighter sleeve), word cards for each member, the code under each row.
- **Routine:** (1) "This is the Who family. Everything stays. Only the who changes." (2) Teacher builds the first member and reads it. (3) "Watch the who." Teacher swaps only the lit card and reads again, three or four times. (4) Point to the code: "The code changed here, too." (5) Students read each row chorally, then touch the lit slot and say its job ("who"). (6) "What stayed the same?" Students point to the fixed cards.
- **Fade path:** this is Stage 1, Rebuild, for a whole family instead of one formula.

### 3.2 Family Swap (her kindergarten activity, generalized)

- **Purpose:** fluent, accurate swapping in one slot.
- **Materials:** the fixed kernel on a strip, a closed bank of 2 or 3 picture word cards for the lit slot, timer.
- **Routine:** (1) Family language. (2) "Pick a card. Put it in the who. Read it." (3) Student reads the new sentence aloud, touching each symbol. (4) Teacher: "Same family?" Student: "Same family. Only the who changed." (5) Repeat; then a 1-minute timed round of correct swaps.
- **Fade path:** Stage 2, Swap. Mastery uses the existing rule (fluency aim set from her baseline, 2 of 3 sessions; a local rule, not a research figure).

### 3.3 Family Sort

- **Purpose:** recognize which family a sentence belongs to.
- **Materials:** 2 or 3 family header cards (strip plus lit slot), 8 to 12 sentence cards from her list and logged public-domain or student text.
- **Routine:** (1) "These are the families. Read the lit slot on each." (2) Student reads a sentence card, codes it with symbols, and asks "What changed?" (3) Places it under its family. (4) Odd one out: one card fits no family yet; student says "New family" and it is kept for later.
- **Fade path:** a check at Stage 4, Code, and a warm-up for Stage 5, Choose. Sorting is not labeling for its own sake: every sort ends with the student building one new member of the chosen family, because labeling-only grammar has negative effects on writing (Graham and Perin 2007, ES -0.32; Tier 1; search summary, not opened).

### 3.4 Family Hunt

- **Purpose:** find family members in real text and see how often they appear.
- **Materials:** a passage, the Log Formulas tab in Formula Search (Literacy Manipulatives), a tally card with the family's members.
- **Routine:** (1) Family language. (2) Teacher logs the passage; the table lists every formula with its count. (3) Students hunt one family: "Find every who." They tally which member each sentence used. (4) "Which member won?" (5) Each student writes one new sentence using the winning member.
- **Fade path:** Stages 5 and 6, where students meet the family in text they did not build.
- **Caution:** her rule for the reading-passage research data is that it "is merely for research and not for actual curriculum development." Family Hunt with students should use passages she logs herself (public domain or student writing), not the five research books.

### 3.5 Family Mix

- **Purpose:** cumulative review; two families in one sentence, one slot changed at a time.
- **Materials:** two lit slots in different colors on one strip, two small banks, the clock cards.
- **Routine:** (1) "Two families today: Who and Time. Change one at a time." (2) Student swaps the who and reads. (3) Student turns the clock card and changes the verb, then reads. (4) "Which one changed?" (5) Only then may both change in one move.
- **First planned mix:** Who x Time in the present tense, where the -s on the verb appears and disappears (Sam runs, Dogs run, She runs). This is where the agreement rule is taught on purpose rather than by accident. Other useful mixes: Who x Where (Sam ran to the park. She ran to the park.), Object x Describe, Be x Kind.
- **Fade path:** Stage 3, Fill, across two families.

### 3.6 Family Change (the Kind family)

- **Purpose:** turn one kernel into each kind, one change at a time.
- **Materials:** a kernel strip, end mark cards (. ? !), an orange "not" card, a "move" arrow, a "remove who" card.
- **Routine:** (1) "This is the Kind family. The words stay. We change the kind." (2) Teacher names the change: "Make it an exclamation. Change only the end." (3) Student changes it and reads with the matching voice. (4) "How many changes?" Student counts. (5) Back to the statement before the next change, so every change starts from the same kernel.
- **Fade path:** Stages 1 to 4, then Stage 5 jobs: "Ask the turtle a question."

### 3.7 Family Map

- **Purpose:** show how families connect, so students see that the same sentence lives in several families.
- **Materials:** a wall or tablet map: the kernel in the center (The dog ran.), each family as a branch with its lit slot and two members.
- **Routine:** once a week, the class adds the new family as a branch, then traces one sentence through three branches ("The dog ran. She ran. She ran to the park. She will run to the park.").
- **Fade path:** the Stage 5 family menu: when a student is stuck, the map shows which slot to change.

### 3.8 Two procedures the data suggests

- **Family Ladder.** The data shows subjects, objects and phrases growing across grades while kernels stay steady. A ladder strip climbs one family member at a time on the same sentence: Sam ran. / Sam ran to the park. / Sam and Mia ran to the park. / After lunch, Sam and Mia ran to the park. Each rung changes one slot. This is sentence expansion run through families, and its final rung is a sentence-combining move.
- **Family Count.** Once a week, students count members in their own writing (how many who members did I use?). If every sentence starts with "I," the count shows it, and the student's next goal is one new member. This turns the 43% pronoun finding into a self-check rather than a rule.

## 4. Scope and sequence, Pre-A to C

"Explicit first" means each family starts with the teacher's full model, a closed bank and the lit slot, and only then moves through the fade path. A family opens when the previous one reaches Stage 2 mastery, not Stage 6; families run in parallel at different stages.

| Level | Families and members | Why here |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-A** (oral, built, pointed) | Who: name, article + noun, pronoun (past tense only). Time: past and present with clock cards. Where: in, on with objects. Kind: statement vs exclamation (end mark only) | Concrete members, one change, actions and objects; her kindergarten session |
| **A** (first written sentences) | Who: bare plural, describing word, my/their + noun. Time: will. Where: to + place, at, with, for, from, then S5. Be: S3, S4, S5, feel. Object: article + noun, pronoun, name, noun alone. Describe: adjective, then adverb. Kind: negative, then question with be. Mix: Who x Time (agreement) | Highest-frequency members of the three priority families; Be bridges to the brown box |
| **B** (families named, codes read) | Who: owner word, compound subject. Time: can, could, would; was/is + -ing. Where: of, about, by, into, like, up/out/down/over; two phrases. Object: describing word + noun, phrase after object. Describe: several adjectives, very. Kind: command, do/did questions. Join: and, but, so, or, then when and because | The helping verbs and prepositions students read most after the first set; equal joins before bracket joins |
| **C** (choosing for a purpose) | Who: there is / it is. Time: had, have, has. Where: opener with a comma. Object: to + verb. Describe: adverb opener, comparing. Join: if, that, before, while, until, as, than; join part first with a comma | Openers are rare in print (3%) and must be taught on purpose; "that" and leaning joins carry complex writing |

This sequence agrees with the "SATPIN for sentences" order in *Formula families and sentence expansion* (names, then article + noun, then a where-phrase, then S2) and adds the pronoun and tense decisions that report left open: pronoun enters at Pre-A as a replacement for a name, and tense enters with the first family, as her kindergarten session already did.

## 5. Her kindergarten session as the template lesson for every family

Her session: a worksheet with four statement formulas, word flashcards, clock cards for past, present and future, spelling supported about 75% of the time. Generalized, every family lesson runs the same way:

1. **Family language.** "This is the ___ family. Everything stays. Only the ___ changes."
2. **Show the strip.** All members on one page or strip, the changing slot lit, the code under each row (Family Introduction).
3. **Model one member.** Teacher builds it with word flashcards on the symbols and reads it, touching each symbol.
4. **Swap with flashcards.** Student picks from a closed bank of 2 or 3 cards for the lit slot only, and reads each new sentence (Family Swap).
5. **Turn the clock.** With the clock cards, the student says the same sentence in past, present and future. In the Who family, hold the clock on past until the swap is fluent, then turn it (the agreement mix). In later families the clock is always available as the second slot.
6. **Write one.** Student writes one member. Spelling support stays at about 75%, as she did; record which words were supported, not only the percentage, and fade support one word at a time as phonics grows. No study sets an ideal percentage. Guided invented spelling, where the adult gives feedback a step above the child's try, helped kindergartners with weak phoneme awareness (Sénéchal et al.; Tier 1, small RCT; search summary, not opened).
7. **Say "same family."** Student reads the written sentence, touches the lit slot, and says "Same family. Only the ___ changed."
8. **Record.** Correct swaps per minute, prompt level from her Prompt Ladder, and which words were spelled with support.

The worksheet itself becomes a reusable template: a family header, the fixed strip, four rows with the lit slot empty, the code under each, and a clock in the corner.
