## 1. How syntactic complexity grows (T-units, clauses, phrases, subordination, nominal groups)

### Takeaway
The classic finding (Hunt 1965) is that the T-unit grows longer with grade, and that this happens mainly because clauses get longer (more phrases and modifiers packed inside them), with subordination growing more slowly. That fits the corpus: kernel shares stay flat while prepositional phrases, commas and length climb. Later work (Beers & Nagy; Nippold) adds two points. Which measure matters depends on genre. Expository tasks draw out more clauses than conversation does.

### Cited Findings
- Hunt's *Grammatical Structures Written at Three Grade Levels* (NCTE Research Report No. 3, 1965) defined the T-unit as one main clause plus all subordinate clauses attached to it. It measured sentence length, clause length, T-unit length, clauses per T-unit and T-units per sentence. **T-unit length was the best indicator of grade level, then clause length, then the clauses-to-T-unit ratio.** Clause length and the subordination ratio both rose from grade to grade (grades 4, 8, 12 and skilled adults). [search result only, not opened] — [T-unit, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-unit); [core.ac.uk secondary PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/478286483.pdf)
- Beers, S. F. & Nagy, W. E., "Syntactic complexity as a predictor of adolescent writing quality: Which measures? Which genre?", *Reading and Writing* 22, 185-200 (2009; online first 2007), DOI 10.1007/s11145-007-9107-5. The study coded middle school students' narratives and essays. **Words per clause correlated with essay quality but not with narrative quality. Clauses per T-unit correlated positively with narrative quality and negatively with essay quality.** [search result only, not opened] — [Springer DOI page](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11145-007-9107-5)
- A later review of syntactic complexity measures (Reading and Writing, 2020) cites Beers & Nagy and calls the link between syntactic complexity and writing quality "inconclusive", depending on the measure and the genre. [search result only, not opened; exact article not confirmed] — [Springer s11145-020-10057-x](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11145-020-10057-x)
- Nippold, Mansfield, Billow & Tomblin (2008), *American Journal of Speech-Language Pathology*, studied 444 adolescents. **Mean length of T-unit, and the use of nominal, relative and adverbial clauses, were greater in an expository task than in conversation** for all groups. Clausal density is "the average number of clauses (main and subordinate) per T-unit". [search result only, not opened] — [ASHA DOI 10.1044/1058-0360(2008/07-0049)](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1058-0360(2008/07-0049))
- Nippold et al. (2009), same journal: 15-year-olds with typical language development outperformed peers with SLI or NLI histories on mean T-unit length, clausal density and nominal clauses. [search result only, not opened] — [ASHA DOI 10.1044/1058-0360(2008/08-0022)](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1058-0360(2008/08-0022))
- A Nippold historical review describes "later language development" as advances in spoken and written language from ages 5 to 25, with expository refinement continuing into the 20s. [search result only, not opened] — [talkbank.org nippold2009.pdf](https://talkbank.org/childes/access/Eng-NA/0docs/nippold2009.pdf)
- Myhill, D. (2008), "Towards a linguistic model of sentence development in writing", *Language and Education* (volume and pages not confirmed). Using an ESRC-funded corpus of secondary-school writing, it analyses sentence-level patterns by age and ability. It argues that "clear developmental trajectories in writing can be determined which have implications for appropriate pedagogical or instructional designs", and it proposes a linguistic model of sentence development. [search result only, not opened; detailed findings not seen] — [Exeter repository PDF](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/bitstream/handle/10036/4476/2008MyhillTowardsaLinguisticModelLanguageandEducation.pdf); [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/12826084.pdf)
- Hsiao, Dawson, Banerji & Nation (2024), "A corpus-based developmental investigation of linguistic complexity in children's writing", *Applied Corpus Linguistics*, DOI 10.1016/j.acorp.2024.100084. It used more than 100,000 short stories written by UK children aged 5 to 13 (Oxford Children's Language Corpus). **Older children used longer sentences, longer T-units and longer clauses.** [search result only, not opened; figures by age not seen] — [Birmingham PDF](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/218479457/1-s2.0-S2666799124000017-main.pdf); [Oxford record](https://www.psy.ox.ac.uk/publications/1612991/modal)

### Inferences
- The corpus's flat kernel shares (S1 about 21-22%, S2 45-57%, linking 12-16%), set against rising prepositional phrases (0.57 to 2.41 per sentence) and commas, match Hunt's ranking that clause length tracks grade better than the subordination ratio does. Much of the growth is expansion inside the kernel (phrases, modifiers, nominal groups), not new kernel types.
- The compound/complex share rises (12% to 49%), but less steeply than phrase counts (about 4x). That is consistent with subordination being a secondary driver.
- The 1,811 different formulas show that the kernel stays stable while its surface keeps varying, because expansion slots multiply. Teaching would aim at the kernel plus a small set of expansion moves, not at whole-sentence formulas.
- Beers & Nagy and Nippold suggest that expository (informational) writing tasks will pull out more clauses. Expansion and combining practice might be sequenced per genre.

### Gaps
- Hunt's actual T-unit means by grade, Loban (1976) and O'Donnell, Griffin & Norris (1967) could not be opened or found in search results. From memory (unverified): Hunt reported roughly 8.6 words per T-unit at grade 4, 11.5 at grade 8, 14.4 at grade 12 and 20.3 for skilled adults. **Check these before use.**

- Myhill's specific trajectories (for example, finite verbs and sentence openings) were not seen.

## 2. How text complexity is measured (Common Core Appendix A, Lexile, Coh-Metrix)

### Takeaway
The main quantitative tools use sentence length as the stand-in for syntax: Lexile uses mean sentence length, and Flesch-Kincaid uses sentence and word length. Coh-Metrix adds structure-based indices (words before the main verb, modifiers per noun phrase). The corpus's jump from about 12 words (G3 to intermediate) to 22.5 (G10) is exactly the kind of difference these tools register. The G3-G5 plateau shows that sentence length alone can miss growth in phrases and punctuation.

### Cited Findings
- Common Core Appendix A: "The quantitative dimension of text complexity refers to those aspects—such as word frequency, sentence length, and text cohesion (to name just three)—that are difficult for a human reader to evaluate". Also: "Flesch-Kincaid uses word and sentence length as proxies for semantic and syntactic complexity respectively." [search result only, not opened; corestandards.org blocked] — [NYSED copy of Appendix A](https://www.nysed.gov/sites/default/files/programs/standards-instruction/appendix-a-common-core-standards-for-ela-literacy-supporting-research-glossary.pdf); [Oregon copy](https://www.oregon.gov/ode/educator-resources/standards/ELA/Documents/ela-appendix-a.pdf)
- Appendix A cites Hayes, Wolfer & Wolfe (1996), "Schoolbook simplification and its relation to the decline in SAT-Verbal Scores", which found "precipitous declines (relative to the period from 1946 to 1962) in average sentence length and vocabulary level in reading textbooks for a variety of grades". It also cites Chall, Conard & Harris (1977) on a decline from 1963 to 1975 in grade 1, 6 and especially grade 11 texts. [search result only, not opened] — [NYSED Appendix A](https://www.nysed.gov/sites/default/files/programs/standards-instruction/appendix-a-common-core-standards-for-ela-literacy-supporting-research-glossary.pdf)
- Contradicting evidence: a later historical reanalysis found that "the claim of a general, steady decline in the complexity levels of textbooks published since 1962 was not supported". [search result only, not opened] — [ERIC EJ1109230](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1109230.pdf); [Penn State record, "Challenging the Research Base of the CCSS"](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/challenging-the-research-base-of-the-common-core-state-standards-/)
- Lexile: measures rest on word frequency (semantic difficulty) and sentence length (syntactic complexity). Sentence length is treated as a proxy for syntax on the grounds that longer sentences make the reader hold more in short-term memory. The Analyzer reports mean sentence length (MSL) and mean log frequency (MLF). Longer sentences and rarer words give higher Lexile measures. Texts without conventional punctuation (lists, poems, lyrics) are not analysed. [search result only, not opened; cdn.lexile.com blocked] — [MetaMetrics, The Lexile Framework for Reading](https://cdn.lexile.com/cms%5fpage%5fmedia/135/The%20Lexile%20Framework%20for%20Reading.pdf); [Lexile validity technical manual 2022](https://partnerhelp.metametricsinc.com/image/Lexile%20Framework%20for%20Reading%20Validity%20Evidence_2022.pdf)
- Coh-Metrix (Graesser, McNamara and colleagues) measures syntactic complexity by **the number of modifiers per noun phrase and the number of words before the main verb**. "Syntactic simplicity" is one of five factors that explain most variance in texts across grade levels. A Texas Education Agency STAAR study used syntactic simplicity as one of three readability indices, with grade-band ranges. [search result only, not opened] — [PubMed: Coh-Metrix, Graesser et al. 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15354684/); [TEA STAAR study](https://tea.texas.gov/student-assessment/reports-and-studies/staarstudypart2final.pdf)
- McNamara, Crossley & McCarthy (2010), "Linguistic Features of Writing Quality", *Written Communication*: the most predictive indices of essay quality included syntactic complexity measured as words before the main verb. [search result only, not opened] — [PDF mirror](https://glotta.ntua.gr/NLP/NLP-PostGrad/2018/State-Of-The-Art-Creative_Writing/Linguistic_Features_of_Writing_Quality.pdf)

### Inferences
- The curriculum's prepositional-phrase and comma counts are close to Coh-Metrix's "modifiers per noun phrase" and "words before main verb", because front-loaded prepositional phrases and appositives add commas. The corpus already measures what sentence length alone misses. This would explain why G5 (11.2 words) is not shorter in structure than G3 (11.9).
- If the corpus figures match Lexile-style grade bands, the large G5-to-G10 step (11 to 22.5 words) is where explicit expansion and combining instruction has to bridge the gap.

### Gaps
- Appendix A's Lexile grade-band table and its wording on qualitative "sentence structure" were not seen.
- Coh-Metrix grade norms for words before the main verb were not found.

## 3. Kernel-first, then expansion and combining, and the gap between reading syntax and writing syntax

### Takeaway
Sentence combining (joining kernel sentences into more complex ones) is one of the evidence-backed writing practices, with a moderate effect on quality. Book language exposes children to syntax (for example, relative clauses) that they rarely hear in speech, and that exposure rises with the intended age of the text. Reading texts therefore model structures students still need to be taught explicitly to produce.

### Cited Findings
- Graham & Perin (2007), *Writing Next* meta-analysis (*Journal of Educational Psychology* 99, 445-476): sentence combining, "teaching students to construct more complex, sophisticated sentences", had an effect size of **0.50 across 5 studies** on adolescent writing quality. [search result only, not opened; figure from the Oregon synopsis] — [Oregon DOE Writing Next synopsis](https://www.oregon.gov/ode/educator-resources/essentialskills/Documents/essentialskillwriting_hs_level2_writing_next_synopsis.pdf)
- Saddler & Graham (2005), "The effects of peer-assisted sentence-combining instruction on the writing performance of more and less skilled young writers", *Journal of Educational Psychology* 97, 43-54. It is a primary study in that meta-analysis, and the What Works Clearinghouse has reviewed it. [search result only, not opened] — [IES WWC study record](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Study/74410)
- Tavsanli, Graham and colleagues (2023, Educational Psychology Review): in a replication with grade 2-4 students in Turkey, sentence-combining students scored higher than business-as-usual peers on sentence fluency, writing quality and essay length. [search result only, not opened] — [Springer s10648-023-09806-5](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-023-09806-5)
- Walter et al. (2021, Reading and Writing) report that sentence-combining research generally shows moderate to large effects for ages 5 to 18. [search result only, not opened] — [Springer s11145-021-10135-8](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-021-10135-8)
- Hsiao, Dawson, Banerji & Nation (2022), "The nature and frequency of relative clauses in the language children hear and the language children read", *Journal of Child Language*, DOI 10.1017/S0305000921000957. It compared child-directed speech, picture books and children's reading books. **Relative clauses were more common in both kinds of book language than in speech, rose with the books' intended age, and were more frequent in nonfiction than fiction.** Book language "provides unique access to grammar not easily encountered in speech". [search result only, not opened] — [Cambridge abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-child-language/article/abs/nature-and-frequency-of-relative-clauses-in-the-language-children-hear-and-the-language-children-read-a-developmental-crosscorpus-analysis-of-english-complex-grammar/D2BF23A38ADCC8D5C3A6A429BC244585)
- Oxford University Press (2012), analysis of about 74,000 children's stories (BBC 500 Words) in the Oxford Children's Corpus: children often misused punctuation (apostrophes especially), and the exclamation mark was among the most-used marks. No breakdown by age was given. [search result only, not opened] — [phys.org report](https://phys.org/news/2012-05-oup-reveals-children-language.pdf)

### Inferences
- Kernel first, then expansion and combining, fits the corpus. Kernel types are already present at G1 in stable proportions. What grows is expansion (phrases, commas) and joining (compound/complex sentences). Combining research is the direct instructional answer to the growth in clauses and phrases.
- Hsiao et al. (2022) suggest that reading texts lead students' own speech and writing in syntax. The corpus's G10 features (22.5 words, semicolons and colons, 49% compound/complex) are likely beyond what students write unaided. Explicit, symbol-mapped modelling of those structures is justified.
- Because semicolons and colons appear almost only at G10 in the reading texts, students meet them rarely before then. That argues for teaching them late and explicitly, after the comma patterns tied to phrases and clauses.

### Gaps
- No source was found that directly measures the gap between reading-text syntax and the same students' writing syntax at matched grades (for example, mean T-unit in textbooks versus student essays).
- No study of sentence expansion alone (as distinct from combining) was found. The Hochman / Writing Revolution approach was not searched.
- Punctuation by age (commas, semicolons) in children's texts or writing was not found.
- Saddler & Graham 2005's own effect sizes and the grade 1 to 3 combining evidence were not seen.
