## 1. Meta-analyses and reviews of writing interventions for students with LD, autism and ADHD

### Takeaway
For students with learning disabilities, explicit writing instruction has solid meta-analytic support. Strategy instruction (SRSD-type) has the largest effect, and dictation and goal setting have moderate effects. For autism the evidence is mostly single-case: explicit, prompted, scaffolded instruction (sentence frames, graphic organizers, SRSD) helps. For ADHD the writing intervention evidence is very thin.

### Cited Findings
- Gillespie, A., & Graham, S. (2014). "A Meta-Analysis of Writing Interventions for Students With Learning Disabilities." *Exceptional Children*, 80(4), 454-473. DOI 10.1177/0014402914527238. The analysis pooled 43 studies with grades 1-12 students with documented LD. The overall effect on writing quality was ES = 0.74. Four treatment types had effects reliably different from zero: strategy instruction (ES = 1.09), goal setting (ES = 0.57), dictation (ES = 0.55) and process writing (ES = 0.43). Treatments aimed at writing processes such as planning and revising worked only when explicit instruction was provided. — [ASHA Evidence Map summary](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/e880f6d4-8590-4dc1-b405-7a285aea8e67); [CEC journal page](https://exceptionalchildren.org/journal/meta-analysis-writing-interventions-students-learning-disabilities)
- Texas LD Center commentary on the same meta-analysis: some simple techniques, such as dictation, are moderately effective and can be put in place quickly. — [texasldcenter.org](https://texasldcenter.org/?p=338)
- Datchuk, S. M., & Kubina, R. M. (2013). "A Review of Teaching Sentence-Level Writing Skills to Students With Writing Difficulties and Learning Disabilities." *Remedial and Special Education*, 34(3), 180-192. The review examined sentence-level interventions that targeted handwriting, sentence construction and grammar/usage. Sources disagree on its size: one says 22 studies through 2010 (9 on sentence writing, 13 on grammar and handwriting), another says 19 studies. — [Penn State Pure record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/a-review-of-teaching-sentence-level-writing-skills-to-students-wi/); [author PDF hosted by CentralReach](https://centralreach.com/wp-content//uploads/2019/04/2013-Datchuk-and-Kubina.pdf)
- A 2023 follow-up review of sentence-writing instruction covered 16 studies. It excluded sentence combining because "it may not be productive for students who are unable to construct simple sentences independently." This supports teaching simple sentences explicitly before combining them. — [Teaching Students to Write Sentences: A Review of the Literature (PDF)](https://alliedhealth.ceconnection.com/files/TeachingStudentstoWriteSentencesAReviewoftheLiterature-1698085822400.pdf)
- A 2018 systematic review by Saddler and colleagues found sentence combining effective for improving sentence construction in students with LD. This finding comes from search summaries only. — [ASHA Evidence Map entry, "Using Sentence Combining Instruction to Enhance the Writing Skills of Children With Learning Disabilities"](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/fbbaea2c-7af4-4ed7-9cce-946c9382bbe6)
- Datchuk, Kubina and Mason (2015) tested sentence instruction plus frequency building to a performance criterion with elementary students with behavioral concerns or EBD. Datchuk (2017) tested Direct Instruction plus precision teaching for sentence construction in middle schoolers. Both use explicit, fluency-based sentence instruction, and both are single-case designs. — [Datchuk, Kubina & Mason 2015 PDF](https://centralreach.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/2015-Datchuk-Kubina-and-Mason.pdf); [Univ. of Iowa record, DI and precision teaching](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-Direct-Instruction-and-Precision-Teaching/9983993324302771)
- Autism: Pennington and Delano (2012) concluded that students with ASD benefit from explicit writing instruction, but that more research is needed to establish an evidence base. — [ASHA Evidence Map, "Writing Instruction for Students With Autism Spectrum Disorders: A Review of Literature"](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/bd787c79-7f46-4cb1-8096-51e828209dc8); [Univ. of Kentucky record](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/writing-instruction-for-students-with-autism-spectrum-disorders-a/)
- Autism: Accardo and colleagues' research synthesis (listed as 2019 or 2020) found six interventions reported to improve written expression: SRSD, constant time delay, response prompting with sentence frames, video modeling, sentence combining with reinforcement, and prompting with graphic organizers. Almost all studies combined several components, so the effect of each discrete element is unknown. — [ASHA Evidence Map, "Writing Interventions for Individuals With Autism Spectrum Disorder: A Research Synthesis"](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/2fc2c0a4-ab38-4560-90f5-2f488144782a)
- Autism: Asaro-Saddler (2016) published a systematic review of writing instruction and self-regulation for students with ASD in *Topics in Language Disorders*, 36(3), 266-283. Related SRSD reviews report that students with ASD taught with SRSD improved overall writing quality, discourse elements and length. — [Springer encyclopedia entry](https://link.springer.com/referenceworkentry/10.1007/978-1-4614-6435-8_102456-1); [Univ. of Kentucky record, evidence base for writing strategies](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/evaluating-the-evidence-base-supporting-writing-instruction-strat/)
- Autism: McClure, Pennington and Bewley (2024) ran a systematic review that applied two sets of quality indicators, which earlier reviews had not done. The specific recommendations were not retrieved. — [Springer PDF s40474-022-00262-4, as listed in search results](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40474-022-00262-4.pdf)
- ADHD: no SRSD meta-analysis for ADHD was found. The most direct study is a single-case design with 2 students over 5-8 sessions, whose authors call it "limited evidence" that SRSD works for children with ADHD symptoms. A 2022 rapid systematic review of 18 studies identified writing quality and planning/editing as challenges for students with ADHD. — [ECU ScholarShip item](https://thescholarship.ecu.edu/items/fc02ccb6-bb69-46ed-9d40-d6f3d8729b4c); [Behaviorist Book Club summary of the rapid review](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/assessment-research/a-rapid-systematic-review-of-attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder/)

### Inferences
- The strongest LD evidence supports explicit teaching with modeling, self-regulation and goals, plus easing transcription through dictation. An explicit "build the sentence, then write" sequence fits this pattern. "Explicit grammar before creative writing" is consistent with it, but no study directly tests that ordering.
- The 2023 review's exclusion of sentence combining for students who cannot yet write simple sentences supports a simple-sentence-first sequence.
- Sentence frames and graphic organizers, which are visual scaffolds, appear among the effective autism components, but always inside packages.

### Gaps
- No full text was opened, so moderators (grade, LD subtype, dosage) and study-quality ratings are unverified.
- No meta-analysis was found specifically for dyslexia or dysgraphia writing outcomes separate from "LD".
- ADHD evidence is essentially limited to single-case studies.

## 2. Visual, colour and shape coding of parts of speech and sentence parts

### Takeaway
Shape Coding (Ebbels) has the best evidence of any visual grammar system, with several small RCTs in DLD and severe language impairment. Colourful Semantics is widely used, but its peer-reviewed evidence is a single small single-case study (n = 6). Montessori grammar symbols and the Fitzgerald Key have no controlled outcome studies that this search could find.

### Cited Findings
- Ebbels, S. H., van der Lely, H. K. J., & Dockrell, J. E. (2007). "Intervention for verb argument structure in children with persistent SLI: A randomized control trial." *JSLHR*, 50(5), 1330-1349. The trial randomized 27 pupils aged 11;0-16;1 to Shape Coding, Semantic or Control therapy, with blind assessment and nine weekly 30-minute individual sessions. Both the Shape Coding and Semantic groups made significant progress (d > 1.0), which was maintained at follow-up and generalized to untrained control verbs. The control group made no progress. Shape Coding "tended to increase use of optional arguments." Visual coding did as well as, but not clearly better than, a semantic approach. — [ASHA journal record](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1092-4388(2007/093)); [UCL accepted-version PDF (not opened)](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10111888/1/Ebbels%20et%20al%202007%20arg%20struc%20therapy%20%28accepted%29.pdf)
- Ebbels, S. H., Marić, N., Murphy, A., & Turner, G. (2014). *International Journal of Language & Communication Disorders*, 49(1), 30-48. An RCT with 14 adolescents (11;3-16;1) with severe receptive language impairment, targeting comprehension of coordinating conjunctions. The therapy group received eight 30-minute explicit Shape Coding sessions, and waiting controls were treated afterwards. The authors concluded that 4 hours of Shape Coding produced significant gains in comprehension of the targeted conjunctions. The sample is very small. — [UCL DevLang intervention page](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/pals/projects/research/develang/intervention); [CORE PDF, as listed in search results (not opened)](https://core.ac.uk/download/110962093.pdf)
- Calder and colleagues (2021): a crossover RCT of Shape Coding for past tense marking in 21 children with DLD aged about 5-7. This shows the method being tested in younger children. — [JSLHR 2020_JSLHR-20-00132](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/full/10.1044/2020_JSLHR-20-00132); [UCL green OA PDF (not opened)](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10128234/1/Calder%20et%20al.%20%282021%29_GREEN.pdf)
- A multicentre, single-blind Shape Coding RCT for children aged 5-10 in mainstream schools is registered (ISRCTN96114973). Results were not retrieved. — [ISRCTN registry](https://www.isrctn.com/ISRCTN96114973)
- Bolderson, S., Dosanjh, C., Milligan, C., Pring, T., & Chiat, S. (2011). "Colourful semantics: A clinical investigation." *Child Language Teaching and Therapy*, 27(3), 344-353. DOI 10.1177/0265659011412248. A single-case multiple-baseline design with 6 children aged 4-6 with expressive language difficulties, given up to six 30-minute clinic sessions with a speech and language therapist. Scores on standardised expressive language tests (including the RAPT) and on an informal verb-argument assessment rose after intervention but not during baseline. The full text is paywalled. — [SAGE record](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265659011412248); [RCSLT presentation citing it](https://www.rcslt.org/wp-content/uploads/media/Project/RCSLT/lorna-wilson.pdf)
- Colourful Semantics originates with Bryan (1997). It colour codes the predicate-argument (thematic role) structure of sentences, such as who, what doing, what and where, and was developed for children with severe SLI before being adapted for mainstream classrooms. The Speech and Language UK directory rates its evidence as "moderate." — [Speech and Language UK directory](https://speechandlanguage.org.uk/area-of-need/language/page/2); [Exeter PenCRU "What's the Evidence" sheet (not opened)](https://exeter.ac.uk/media/universityofexeter/medicalschool/subsites/pencru/pdfs/WTE_Colourful_Semantics.pdf)
- A 2015 Sri Lankan study of class-delivered Colourful Semantics with children with intellectual disabilities reported positive narrative outcomes, but it used pre/post measures only, with no control group. — [Integrated Treatment Services PDF](https://integratedtreatmentservices.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/Sri-Lanka-Narraitve-Study-2015.pdf)
- No 2020s randomised evaluation of Colourful Semantics was found. A commercial site cites "Atwell (2024)", but the original could not be located, so this is unverified. — [structural-learning.com](https://structural-learning.com/post/colourful-semantics-activities)
- Montessori grammar symbols: the pages found (mostly commercial or blog) describe consistent shapes and colours for each part of speech, and children drawing the symbols while rereading a sentence. None cites a controlled study of the symbols themselves. A *Journal of Montessori Research* review discusses Montessori literacy outcomes generally, not the symbols. — [AMI page](https://montessori-ami.org/node/9256); [Journal of Montessori Research article](https://journals.ku.edu/jmr/article/download/24162/22529/99869)
- Fitzgerald Key: the search found no outcome research.

### Inferences
- The curriculum's grammar symbols are closest to Shape Coding (shapes for phrases and roles, colours for word classes, explicit visual rules), and that is the only visual-grammar family with RCT support. Even so, those trials are small (n = 14 to 27), mostly adolescents with severe or receptive language disorder, and usually run 1:1 by SLTs in a specialist school.
- In the 2007 RCT the visual (Shape Coding) and non-visual semantic therapies did about equally well. This suggests the active ingredient may be explicit, systematic teaching of sentence structure rather than colour or shape as such. The symbols are best framed as a vehicle for explicit instruction, not as proven on their own.
- Colourful Semantics evidence should be described honestly as "promising and widely used, thin peer-reviewed evidence."

### Gaps
- Exact Shape Coding effect sizes for the 2014 and 2021 trials were not seen.
- No study compared Montessori symbols or the Fitzgerald Key against a control.
- No visual grammar study was found that measures written (rather than spoken) sentence outcomes for dyslexia, dysgraphia or ADHD.

## 3. Evidence for explicit grammar and syntax intervention in DLD

### Takeaway
Reviews conclude that explicit, targeted grammar intervention produces measurable gains for school-age children with DLD, including adolescents. Gains on the targeted structures are consistent, but evidence for durable, functional and receptive gains is sparse.

### Cited Findings
- Ebbels, S. (2014). "Effectiveness of intervention for grammar in school-aged children with primary language impairments: A review of the evidence." *Child Language Teaching and Therapy*, 30(1), 7-40. DOI 10.1177/0265659013512321. The review covers grammar intervention for children over 5, grouped into studies targeting specific grammar areas (expressive and receptive) and studies of language more generally. It names the small amount of evidence for children with receptive as well as expressive impairment as the most concerning gap. — [UCL Discovery record](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1469502); [British Library record](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100055616597_0x000001)
- A later Ebbels and colleagues study of 1:1 SLT for older children enrolled 72 students aged 9-17, 88% of whom had receptive impairment, and builds on the 2014 review. — [UCL Discovery PDF listing](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1529253/1/Ebbels%20Effectiveness%20of%201-1%20speech%20and%20language%20therapy%20for%20older%20children.pdf)
- Balthazar, C. H., & Scott, C. M. (2024). "Sentences Are Key: Helping School-Age Children and Adolescents Build Sentence Skills Needed for Real Language." *AJSLP*. Studies have consistently shown measurable improvement in complex sentence production on proximal outcomes, regardless of treatment approach, but evidence of durable, functional change remains sparse. — [PMC11001191](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11001191/); [AJSLP DOI 10.1044/2023_AJSLP-23-00038](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2023_AJSLP-23-00038); [GovState OPUS](https://opus.govst.edu/fac/302)
- A complex sentence treatment trial at two dosages with 30 children aged 10-14 with SLI (*JSLHR* 2018, vol. 61) found focused intervention improved complex sentence production. The registry entry (NCT01337232, Governors State University) compares one versus two sessions per week. Authorship was not confirmed from the snippet, though the registry is Balthazar's institution. — [JSLHR 2017_JSLHR-L-17-0105](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2017_JSLHR-L-17-0105); [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT01337232](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01337232)
- Balthazar, Ebbels and Zwitserlood (2020) reviewed explicit grammatical interventions, including Complex Sentence Intervention and Shape Coding, with evidence for children with DLD aged 5-16. — [PMC7225018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7225018)
- Ebbels and colleagues (2024) published a paper on targets and dosage for grammar intervention. Details were not retrieved. — [UCL Discovery PDF listing](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10193044/1/2024%20Ebbels%20et%20al%20targets%20and%20dosage%20accepted.pdf)

### Inferences
- Explicit, metalinguistic grammar teaching is supported for DLD into adolescence, which contradicts the idea that older students are "too old" for grammar intervention. Programs should plan for generalization and transfer into real writing, because that is where the evidence is weakest.

### Gaps
- Pooled effect sizes and dosage recommendations from these reviews were not seen in full text.
- Few studies measure written-language transfer after oral grammar intervention.

## 4. Transcription supports (speech-to-text, dictation) and handwriting/spelling instruction

### Takeaway
Dictation has a moderate pooled effect on writing quality for students with LD (ES = 0.55). Speech-to-text technology shows large but heterogeneous effects in small, mostly single-case studies, and some young children do not benefit. Explicit handwriting instruction itself improves writing quality.

### Cited Findings
- Dictation ES = 0.55 on writing quality for students with LD. — Gillespie & Graham 2014, [ASHA Evidence Map summary](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/e880f6d4-8590-4dc1-b405-7a285aea8e67)
- Perelmutter and colleagues (2017) meta-analysed assistive technology for adolescents and adults with LD. Word-processing AT had a large effect on writing error rates (g = 1.626). Speech-to-text studies showed large positive effects, but heterogeneity prevented pooling. — [informahealthcare reference listing](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/ref/10.1080/17483107.2022.2149865); [NCEO Accommodations Toolkit](https://publications.ici.umn.edu/nceo/accommodations-toolkit/speech-to-text-research)
- The NCEO Accommodations Toolkit reports that, across studies with elementary and secondary students with LD, speech-to-text produced longer texts with fewer errors and may reduce cognitive load. — [NCEO Accommodations Toolkit](https://publications.ici.umn.edu/nceo/accommodations-toolkit/speech-to-text-research)
- NCEO Report 414 (Liu et al., 2019) reviewed 2008-2018 research. All included studies were single-subject designs, and only 4 measured writing outcomes. — [NCEO Report 414](https://nceo.umn.edu/publications/OnlinePubs/report414/default.html)
- Mixed results: a small thesis (Adams, 2017, n = 6, ages 7-9) found no significant writing gain from speech-to-text over paper and pencil. Reading Rockets cautions that speech recognition can add frustration for struggling or very young writers. — [Rowan thesis](https://rdw.rowan.edu/etd/2481); [Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/assistive-technology/articles/speech-recognition-learning)
- Santangelo, T., & Graham, S. (2016). "A Comprehensive Meta-Analysis of Handwriting Instruction." *Educational Psychology Review*, 28(2), 225-265. DOI 10.1007/s10648-015-9335-1. Handwriting instruction improved writing quality (ES = 0.84), length (ES = 1.33) and fluency (ES = 0.48). Motor-only instruction did not improve legibility, but individualized and technology-based handwriting instruction did. — [ACU Research Bank](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/85wx5/a-comprehensive-meta-analysis-of-handwriting-instruction); [Reading Rockets summary](https://www.readingrockets.org/research-by-topic/comprehensive-meta-analysis-handwriting-instruction)

### Inferences
- For dysgraphia and dyslexia, offering dictation or speech-to-text while sentence structure is being taught is consistent with the evidence. It frees working memory for grammar. Teaching handwriting and spelling alongside should continue rather than relying on the technology alone.
- Speech-to-text benefits look stronger for older students. With young children, outcomes should be monitored individually.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of speech-to-text specifically for K-12 students with LD was found.
- No studies were found pairing speech-to-text with visual or symbolic sentence building.
- The spelling instruction effect on writing quality could not be attributed to a verified source.
