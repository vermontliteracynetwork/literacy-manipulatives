## 1. Cognitive-developmental models: knowledge-telling to knowledge-transforming to knowledge-crafting, deliberate practice, automaticity

### Takeaway
The main developmental models describe a path from writing down what you know (knowledge-telling), to reworking ideas while writing (knowledge-transforming), to writing with the reader in mind (knowledge-crafting). Moving up depends on freeing working memory. When transcription (handwriting, spelling, sentence production) becomes fluent, capacity is left over for higher-level composing, and expertise grows through long, deliberate practice. This is the strongest theoretical argument that explicit foundational skill is a precondition for artistic and purposeful writing rather than a rival to it.

### Cited Findings
- Bereiter, C. & Scardamalia, M. (1987). *The Psychology of Written Composition*. Lawrence Erlbaum (now Routledge), ISBN 9780805800388. Its chapter "Two Models of Composing Processes" contrasts knowledge-telling with knowledge-transforming. — [Routledge listing](https://www.routledge.com/The-Psychology-of-Written-Composition/Bereiter-Scardamalia/p/book/9780805800388); [course PDF of the text](https://eclass.uoa.gr/modules/document/file.php/PHS122/%CE%91%CF%81%CE%B8%CF%81%CE%B1/writing_composition/written_composition.pdf) (not opened)
- **Knowledge-telling:** content is produced by probing memory with topical cues. **Knowledge-transforming:** content generation is subordinated to work in two problem spaces. The content space is where belief and logical consistency are worked out; the rhetorical space is where goal-related composition problems are handled. The interaction between them is a dialectic, hypothesized to explain the "surprise and discovery" expert writers report. Experts can draw flexibly on both models, while novices mostly use knowledge-telling. — search summary of [Galbraith & Baaijen 2018, "The Work of Writing: Raiding the Inarticulate," *Educational Psychologist*](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/425716/1/Galbraith_Baaijen_2018_The_Work_of_Writing_Raiding_the_Inarticulate.pdf) and the Bereiter & Scardamalia text
- Galbraith & Baaijen (2018) state that the 1980s models still "form the basis for the assumptions that inform current research and its application to the teaching of writing." They also challenge parts of the models. — [Southampton eprint](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/425716/1/Galbraith_Baaijen_2018_The_Work_of_Writing_Raiding_the_Inarticulate.pdf)
- Kellogg, R. T. (2008). "Training writing skills: A cognitive developmental perspective." *Journal of Writing Research*, 1(1), 1–26. DOI 10.17239/jowr-2008.01.01.1. — [JoWR article page](https://jowr.org/index.php/jowr/article/view/759) (**blocked**)
  - It describes three stages: knowledge-telling, knowledge-transforming and knowledge-crafting.
  - The first two stages are well documented. Knowledge-crafting is more speculative and describes mature adults aiming to become professional writers.
  - In knowledge-crafting, the writer routinely holds three representations in working memory: the planned content, the text itself, and the reader's interpretation of the text.
  - Knowledge-transforming, and especially knowledge-crafting, arguably occur only when enough executive attention is available.
  - Kellogg proposes that students learn best through "cognitive apprenticeship" programs that emphasize deliberate practice.
  - Sources: search summary of the [JoWR page](https://jowr.org/index.php/jowr/article/view/759) and the [UiTM/DOAJ record](https://openaccess.library.uitm.edu.my/Record/doaj-405ec1e3cf0d4257af15bc43b0383858)
- McCutchen, D. (1996). "A capacity theory of writing: Working memory in composition." *Educational Psychology Review*, 8, 299–325. DOI 10.1007/BF01464076. — [doi.org](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/BF01464076)
  - It documents capacity limits in all three component processes: planning, translating and reviewing.
  - Later work cites it for two points. Low-level motor transcription is demanding for children whose handwriting is not yet automatic. Writers without fluent text production are "severely constrained."
  - The search summary also notes that later work challenges a purely resource-limited account in favor of a "resource-coordination" view of working memory.
- **Not found:** the commonly repeated claim that writing expertise takes about 10 to 20 years of deliberate practice (Kellogg 2006/2008, Ericsson). No search result confirmed it. See Gaps.

### Inferences
- These models support explicit, early instruction in sentence construction and transcription, so that those skills become automatic. The point is to free capacity for content, audience and craft, which is where creativity lives. The models do not show that explicit instruction by itself produces creative writing. They show that a lack of automaticity blocks the higher stages.
- Kellogg's "knowledge-crafting," which means keeping the reader's interpretation in mind, matches what creative-writing pedagogy calls craft. It is placed late, in adult professional development, which suggests the path is long and needs explicit apprenticeship.

### Gaps
- The full text of Kellogg 2008 was not opened (jowr.org is blocked), so the exact wording about deliberate practice, years to expertise and stage transitions is unverified.
- Bereiter & Scardamalia's own text was not opened.
- No source found here tests directly whether transcription automaticity leads to more creative or more original writing, as opposed to longer or better-organized writing.

## 2. Strategy instruction with self-regulation (SRSD) and transfer across genres, plus writing for research and learning

### Takeaway
SRSD has among the largest effect sizes in writing research (about ES 1.17 in elementary meta-analyses). Transfer to untaught genres does happen, but it is modest and inconsistent. In the SRSD Plus trial it was about ES .22 to .34 into opinion writing. It tends to fade without continued practice and seems to need explicit teaching of transfer itself.

Writing about texts improves reading (about 0.40 SD in one reported comparison, Graham & Hebert 2010). Writing to learn in content subjects gives small-to-moderate gains: Bangert-Drowns et al. 2004 found a "small, positive" effect, and Graham et al. 2020 found g = 0.30. This is the "research purposes" payoff of foundational writing skill.

### Cited Findings
- Graham, McKeown, Kiuhara & Harris (2012), meta-analysis of elementary writing instruction, as summarized by the Texas LD Center:
  - SRSD: 14 studies, ES = 1.17, 95% CI [0.81, 1.53].
  - Strategy instruction overall: 20 studies, ES = 1.02.
  - The summary's confidence interval for non-SRSD strategy instruction looks like a transcription error, so check the original Table 2.
  - Source: [texasldcenter.org summary](https://texasldcenter.org/?p=311) (not opened)
- SRSD's own advocacy site reports ES 1.59 for SRSD versus 0.34 for traditional instruction. **This is a promotional source and I could not trace it to a specific analysis.** — [srsdonline.org](https://srsdonline.org/science-of-writing/)
- A randomized trial of SRSD Plus (grades 1–2) reported "a positive transfer effect on writing outcomes in the opinion genre (.22 ≤ ES ≤ .34)", even though opinion was not the target genre. — search summary of [Reading and Writing 2025 article](https://ddd.uab.cat/pub/artpub/2025/321812/s11145-025-10721-0.pdf) and related results. The authors and title were not confirmed from the snippet.
- A longitudinal SRSD study with 2nd and 4th graders stressed "the need for continued support and practice to maintain benefits." — [s11145-025-10721-0 (UAB repository)](https://ddd.uab.cat/pub/artpub/2025/321812/s11145-025-10721-0.pdf)
- A Spanish strategy-instruction study (126 fourth graders, compare-contrast program) found better structure and coherence in untaught opinion texts at post-test, but not 8 months later. The authors concluded that students need teachers to teach them how to transfer strategies. — search summary of [revistas.um.es (Anales de Psicología)](https://revistas.um.es/analesps/article/download/571031/360971)
- Dostal, Wolbers & Weir (2021) studied Strategic and Interactive Writing Instruction (SIWI) with 37 deaf and hard of hearing elementary students. Students transferred what they learned from recount writing to "other untargeted genres." This is not SRSD, but it is close and relevant to disabled learners. — [siwi.utk.edu Transfer.pdf](https://siwi.utk.edu/wp-content/uploads/Transfer.pdf)
- Graham, S. & Hebert, M. (2010). *Writing to Read: Evidence for How Writing Can Improve Reading*. Carnegie Corporation of New York / Alliance for Excellent Education.
  - It was the first meta-analysis of writing practices' effects on reading.
  - It drew on 29 studies with norm-referenced tests and 55 with researcher-designed tests, in grades 1–12.
  - It makes three recommendations: have students write about texts; teach writing skills and processes; increase how much students write.
  - Writing about texts gave roughly 0.40 SD higher reading scores in one reported comparison. That was one example, not necessarily the headline figure.
  - Sources: [LINCS profile](https://lincs.ed.gov/professional-development/resource-collections/profile-432); [synopsis PDF](https://core-docs.s3.amazonaws.com/documents/asset/uploaded_file/776383/Synopsis_Writing_to_Read.pdf); [Vanderbilt news release](https://news.vanderbilt.edu/2010/04/15/new-report-finds-writing-can-be-powerful-driver-for-improving-reading-skills-112797). The report PDF itself was **blocked**.
- Bangert-Drowns, R. L., Hurley, M. M. & Wilkinson, B. (2004). "The effects of school-based writing-to-learn interventions on academic achievement: A meta-analysis." *Review of Educational Research*, 74(1), 29–58. DOI 10.3102/00346543074001029.
  - It covered 48 programs and found that writing "can have a small, positive impact on conventional measures of academic achievement."
  - It cautioned that the effects may depend on the context in which the writing occurs.
  - Sources: [SUNY Research Connect](https://researchconnect.suny.edu/en/publications/the-effects-of-school-based-writing-to-learn-interventions-on-aca/); [metafor dataset doc](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/metadat/html/dat.bangertdrowns2004.html). The pooled d was not confirmed.
- Graham, S., Kiuhara, S. A. & MacKay, M. (2020). "The effects of writing on learning in science, social studies, and mathematics: A meta-analysis." *Review of Educational Research*.
  - It covered 56 experiments and found g = 0.30.
  - The authors describe this as higher than previous meta-analyses.
  - Sources: [ASU record](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/the-effects-of-writing-on-learning-in-science-social-studies-and-/); [TUM Clearinghouse summary](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/CHU-KR-29_ENG_Graham-2020.pdf) (**blocked**)

### Inferences
- Explicit strategy and structure instruction transfers to new genres only partly, and often not durably, unless transfer is taught on purpose: name the shared structure, practice it across genres, and revisit it. A curriculum should plan cross-genre "bridge" lessons rather than assume transfer will happen.
- Writing to learn and writing about texts show that foundational writing skill pays off in research and learning. Those effects are moderate, not large, and depend on context.

### Gaps
- I could not find a single SRSD meta-analysis that reports a pooled effect for transfer to untaught genres.
- The SRSD Plus trial's authors, title and year are not confirmed from source.
- The exact effect sizes in *Writing to Read* for each type of writing about text (responses, summaries, notes, questions) were not obtained.

## 3. Constraints and creativity; imitation and mentor-text approaches

### Takeaway
Experimental and theoretical work suggests that well-chosen constraints can increase creative output rather than suppress it. Stokes's paired constraints work by ruling out the familiar while pointing toward the new. In Haught-Tromp's experiments, constrained rhymes were rated more creative, and the benefit carried over to later unconstrained writing.

The evidence on imitating models is weaker than for sentence combining. In Graham & Perin's adolescent meta-analysis, study of models had ES 0.25, sentence combining 0.50, and traditional grammar instruction −0.32.

### Cited Findings
- Haught-Tromp, C. (2017; online 2016). "The Green Eggs and Ham hypothesis: How constraints facilitate creativity." *Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts*.
  - In two experiments, participants wrote two-line rhymes for a special occasion, sometimes required to include a given noun.
  - There was a main effect of constraints on creativity, plus an order interaction that suggests constrained practice carries over to later unconstrained work.
  - The paper ties this to a "preclude–promote" model.
  - Sources: search summary of [rider.edu PDF](https://www.rider.edu/sites/default/files/docs/tromp_haught-tromp_PACA_2016.pdf) (**blocked**) and [scite report](https://scite.ai/reports/the-green-eggs-and-ham-KYz9VM)
- Press coverage quotes Haught-Tromp saying that constraints allow "a deeper exploration of fewer alternatives." This is secondary, not peer reviewed. — [Pacific Standard](https://psmag.com/?p=22040); [Big Think](https://bigthink.com/mind-brain/why-imposing-restrictions-can-actually-boost-creativity/)
- Caveat: the evidence is two small experiments, and I found no replications in search.
- Stokes, P. D. (2005). *Creativity from Constraints: The Psychology of Breakthrough*. Springer.
  - Building on Reitman, a pair of constraints has two jobs. One precludes (limits search among) reliable existing solutions. The other promotes (directs search toward) novel, often opposite, ones.
  - Her Monet analysis is in Stokes (2001), "Variability, constraints and creativity: Shedding light on Claude Monet," *American Psychologist*. It argues that Monet stayed variable across his career by constantly imposing new constraints.
  - Sources: [Pat Stokes, Barnard page](https://cool.barnard.edu/pat-stokes/?p=85); [Stokes, constraints and business PDF](https://psychology.barnard.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/ConstraintsBusiness.pdf); [HuffPost summary of Kaufman](https://www.huffpost.com/entry/does-creativity-require-c_b_948460)
- Graham, S. & Perin, D. (2007). *Writing Next* (Carnegie / Alliance for Excellent Education) and the companion "A meta-analysis of writing instruction for adolescent students," *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 99, 445–476. Reported effect sizes:
  - Sentence combining: ES 0.50.
  - Study of models: ES 0.25, the smallest of the eleven positive interventions.
  - Traditional (isolated) grammar instruction: ES −0.32.
  - Sources: [Typeset copy of JEP meta-analysis](https://typeset.io/pdf/a-meta-analysis-of-writing-instruction-for-adolescent-55m4vy9ytc.pdf); [Oregon DOE synopsis](https://www.oregon.gov/ode/educator-resources/essentialskills/Documents/essentialskillwriting_hs_level2_writing_next_synopsis.pdf); [Datchuk & Kubina 2015](https://centralreach.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/2015-Datchuk-and-Kubina.pdf)

### Inferences
- Structure framed as a playful constraint ("use this formula," "include this word type") is consistent with Stokes's and Haught-Tromp's findings that constraints can help creativity. The carryover result suggests that constrained practice may improve later free writing, though this has not been tested with children or with disabled learners.
- Grammar that is practiced inside sentence building and combining helps writing. Grammar taught as isolated labeling has been found to hurt (−0.32). Imitation of models helps a little. All of this favors manipulative, sentence-building instruction over drills on labeling grammar.

### Gaps
- I did not reach Corbett's rhetorical-imitation work or any empirical study of classical imitation.
- No creative-writing pedagogy research (for example Joyce or NAWE) on constraints surfaced in search.
- Haught-Tromp's participants were adults, so applying the result to children is an inference.

## 4. Creative writing pedagogy on grammar and craft (Myhill and others)

### Takeaway
Myhill's "grammar as choice" approach, which teaches grammar in context and links it to rhetorical purpose and authentic texts, has RCT evidence of benefit: ES about 0.21, with larger gains for more able writers. It reframes explicit grammar as the means of authorial choice and agency. That is a direct research bridge from explicit grammar to purposeful, crafted writing.

### Cited Findings
- Myhill, D. A., Jones, S. M., Lines, H. & Watson, A. (2012). "Re-thinking grammar: the impact of embedded grammar teaching on students' writing and students' metalinguistic understanding." *Research Papers in Education*, 27(2), 139–166.
  - It was a mixed-methods study: an RCT plus text analysis, interviews and observations.
  - There was a positive effect on writing, reported as ES 0.21 (p < .001), with the benefit "felt more strongly by the more able writers."
  - It is described as "robust evidence for the first time of a positive benefit derived from the teaching of grammar."
  - Sources: [Exeter ORE record](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10036/4479); [Exeter PDF](https://www.exeter.ac.uk/media/universityofexeter/collegeofsocialsciencesandinternationalstudies/education/research/centres/writing/teacherresources/Re-thinking_Grammar_Research_Papers_in_Education.pdf)
- Jones, S., Myhill, D. & Bailey, T. (2013), *Reading and Writing*, DOI 10.1007/s11145-012-9416-1, reported similar findings. — [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11145-012-9416-1)
- A 2016 cluster RCT (Mitchell et al.) also reported effects of contextualised grammar teaching on 11-year-olds' writing. **This came from a search snippet only; the citation details are unverified.** — [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/100235/1/document.pdf)
- Myhill draws on Carter & McCarthy's split between grammar as structure and grammar as choice: "every choice carries a different meaning, and grammar is concerned with the implications of such choices."
- Her pedagogy stresses "the fundamental importance of establishing a link between linguistic choice and rhetorical purpose," so that young writers can make their own authorial choices. It uses published texts as models.
- She cites Kellogg's view of writing as constant decision-making.
- Sources: [Exeter "Grammar as Choice"](https://www.exeter.ac.uk/research/centres/languageandliteracy/grammar-teacher-resources/grammaraschoice/); [Grammar for Writing pedagogy page](https://www.exeter.ac.uk/research/centres/languageandliteracy/grammar-teacher-resources/grammaraschoice/thegrammarforwritingpedagogy/); [Utbildning & Demokrati article](https://journals.oru.se/uod/article/download/1121/1110/1405); [Myhill Bergen 2018 slides](https://www.hvl.no/contentassets/14b45e73b134430591ff07abf981b9b8/01-debra-myhills-bergen-hvl-2018.pdf)

### Inferences
- Explicit grammar supports creative and purposeful writing when it is taught as choices with effects on the reader, in the context of real writing. Grammar taught as rules or labels does not. The finding that more able writers benefited more is a caution for neurodivergent and struggling learners: they may need more scaffolding, such as SRSD-style supports, to turn grammar knowledge into choices.

### Gaps
- I found no research in this search on adult or professional creative writers' accounts of learning sentence craft, and no NAWE position statements. Writers' craft essays were not reached.
- Whether "grammar as choice" benefits disabled or neurodivergent students specifically was not found.
