## 1. Parts of speech and sentence parts (prepositions, conjunctions, helping verbs, songs, jingles, pronouns, articles)

### Takeaway
The best-known cues are lists turned into acronyms or physical scenes: "anywhere a mouse can go" or "anything a squirrel can do to a tree" for prepositions, FANBOYS for coordinating conjunctions, AAAWWUBBIS / A WHITE BUS for subordinating conjunctions, and Shurley jingles plus Schoolhouse Rock songs for definitions. Nearly every cue covers only part of the category. Some (FANBOYS, the location-based preposition cues) teach a rule that is wrong at the edges, so a predictable symbol system will need to label them as "starter lists", not definitions.

### Cited Findings
**Prepositions**
- I found no exact-phrase hit for "anywhere a squirrel can go." The widespread version is "anywhere a mouse can go". Lesson Planet teacher pages and a Goodreads author blog both describe teachers giving that chart (practitioner voice) — [Lesson Planet](https://lessonplanet.com/teachers/playing-with-prepositions-of-place); [Goodreads blog, "Anywhere a Mouse Can Go"](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/5602421-anywhere-a-mouse-can-go)
- Explore Language uses "anything a squirrel can do to a tree" and lists above, across, behind and through in a squirrel-and-tree sentence (practitioner/curriculum voice) — [Explore Language, 15 prepositions](https://explorelanguage.org/15-preps)
- Teach Starter sells anchor charts that split prepositions into place, time and direction (practitioner voice) — [Teach Starter preposition anchor chart pack](https://teachstarter.com/us/teaching-resource/preposition-anchor-chart-pack)
- Shurley English defines a preposition, in its Level 1 Preposition Jingle (Jingle 7), as a connecting word that links a noun or pronoun to the rest of the sentence. Later levels add Object of the Preposition, Prepositional Phrase, Preposition Flow and Noun Job jingles — [Veritas Press, Shurley Level 1 workbook sample](https://store.veritaspress.com/site/assets/515030-shurley-level-1-hs-workbook-lis.pdf); [Exodus Books, Shurley Level 7 sample](https://www.exodusbooks.com/Samples/Shurley/cd7.pdf)
- No search result showed the plane-and-cloud cue ("anything a plane can do to a cloud"), so it is not sourced here (see Gaps).

**Coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS)**
- In standard teaching, a comma goes before a FANBOYS word that joins two complete ideas. A comma alone between independent clauses is a comma splice — [Lumen Learning, Coordinating Conjunctions: FANBOYS](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/olemiss-writing100/chapter/conjunctions-and-lists/); [Naval Postgraduate School Graduate Writing Center](https://library.nps.edu/web/gwc/commas-fanboys)
- **Accuracy flag:** according to Vocabulary.com's summary of Reynolds's analysis, only and, but, nor and or meet all the criteria for coordinating conjunctions; for, so and yet meet only a few. The Cambridge Grammar treats so and yet as closer to conjunctive adverbs and sees for as close to "because" in meaning — [Vocabulary.com, "The Trouble with FANBOYS"](https://www.vocabulary.com/articles/wc/the-trouble-with-fanboys/)
- **Accuracy flag:** Oxford University Press dates the acronym to about the mid-twentieth century and says it misleads learners into thinking all seven words behave alike — [OUP blog, "Conjunction dysfunction"](https://blog.oup.com/2020/08/conjunction-dysfunction/)
- The FANBOYS comma can be dropped when both clauses are short and cannot be misread. A 2013 corpus study (Marjanović, Nordic Journal of English Studies) questions whether the comma rule is a firm law — [Vocabulary.com](https://www.vocabulary.com/articles/wc/the-trouble-with-fanboys/); [Nordic Journal of English Studies (publicera.kb.se)](https://publicera.kb.se/njes/article/download/29464/23707/67774)
- "Never start a sentence with and/but" is a common belief, not a real rule — [Grammarly, coordinating conjunctions](https://www.grammarly.com/blog/coordinating-conjunctions/)

**Subordinating conjunctions (AAAWWUBBIS, A WHITE BUS, I SAW A WABUB)**
- One common AAAWWUBBIS list is Although, As, After, When, While, Until, Because, Before, If, Since. Other versions swap in "unless". AWUBIS is the short form without the repeated letters — [ClassWeekly teaching wiki](https://www.classweekly.com/teaching-wiki/subordinating-conjunction); [Shmoop](https://www.shmoop.com/grammar/conjunctions/subordinating.html)
- Teachers pair AAAWWUBBIS with a comma rule: when one of these words starts a sentence, a comma comes later in it, at the end of the dependent clause (practitioner voice) — [SlideShare, subordinating conjunctions](https://de.slideshare.net/TimCreighton4/subordinating-conjunctions-249265282)
- A WHITE BUS (some versions say ON A WHITE BUS) groups words by first letter: A = after, although, as; W = when, whenever, where, while; H = how (one flashcard set also lists "however", which is a conjunctive adverb, so that is an accuracy flag); I, T, E, B, U, S = if, though, even though, because, before, until/unless, since — [Sotheby's Institute page](https://sa.sothebysinstitute.com/acronym-for-subordinating-conjunctions); [Mrs Learning Bee posters (practitioner voice)](https://mrslearningbee.com/products/a-white-bus-poster-displays-for-subordinating-conjunctions); [Sophia tutorial](https://app.sophia.org/tutorials/compound-complex-sentences-3)
- Twinkl (UK) uses "I SAW A WABUB" (practitioner voice) — [Twinkl, What is I SAW A WABUB](https://www.twinkl.com/teaching-wiki/i-saw-a-wabub)
- **Coverage flag:** AAAWWUBBIS leaves out "unless", "even though" and "whenever", and A WHITE BUS misses "than" and "whereas" — [ClassWeekly](https://www.classweekly.com/teaching-wiki/subordinating-conjunction); [Sotheby's Institute page](https://sa.sothebysinstitute.com/acronym-for-subordinating-conjunctions)

**Helping and linking verbs**
- Shurley Level 6 audio includes a track called "The 23 Helping Verbs Mean Lean Verb Machine". Shurley's reference sheet says a helping verb helps the main verb and stands directly before it — [Exodus Books, Shurley Level 6 sample](https://www.exodusbooks.com/Samples/Shurley/cd6.pdf); [Shurley AAG reference sheet](https://sequoyaheagles.socs.net/vimages/shared/vnews/stories/57b13163e2a00/Shurley%20AAG_noun_%20PNA.pdf)
- Shurley's method is to teach definitions as rhythmic jingles. The publisher says the rhythm helps students learn and retain concepts (publisher claim, not research) — [Shurley English blog, free jingles](https://quigley-shurley-gacr.squarespace.com/blog/2022/2/14/free-grammar-jingles-from-shurley-english); [Shurley English Jingles words-only PDF](https://www.tcatitans.org/cms/lib/CO50010872/Centricity/Domain/143/Shurley%20English%20Jingles%20-%20Words%20Only.pdf)
- Vocabulary.com and the OUP blog both have articles on helping verbs. The "forgotten helping verbs" piece suggests the usual chant lists are incomplete — [Vocabulary.com, "The forgotten helping verbs"](https://www.vocabulary.com/articles/dictionary/the-forgotten-helping-verbs/); [OUP blog, helping verbs](https://blog.oup.com/2023/07/helping-verbs-are-curious-and-fascinating/)

**Schoolhouse Rock "Grammar Rock"**
- There are nine Grammar Rock songs: "A Noun Is a Person, Place or Thing", "Verb: That's What's Happening", "Unpack Your Adjectives" (sung by Blossom Dearie), "Lolly, Lolly, Lolly, Get Your Adverbs Here", "Conjunction Junction" (sung by Jack Sheldon), "Interjections!", "Rufus Xavier Sarsaparilla" (pronouns), and two later additions, "Busy Prepositions" and "The Tale of Mr. Morton" (subject and predicate) — [Wikipedia, List of Schoolhouse Rock episodes](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Schoolhouse_Rock_episodes); [Ken's Home Page list (U Michigan)](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~varnum/Schoolhouse.html); [Wikipedia, Schoolhouse Rock! Soundtrack](https://Www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Schoolhouse_Rock!_Soundtrack)

**Articles (a vs an)**
- The choice follows sound, not spelling: "a" goes before a consonant sound and "an" before a vowel sound. So it is "an hour" (silent h) but "a university" and "a one-time fee" (consonant sounds "yoo" and "wuh"). Acronyms follow the same rule, by how they are said — [Purdue OWL, a versus an](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/grammar/articles_a_versus_an.html); [MIT Mayfield handbook, a or an](https://stuff.mit.edu/afs/athena.mit.edu/course/21/21.guide/a-or-an.htm); [Merriam-Webster, is it a or an](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/is-it-a-or-an)

**Pronouns**
- Schoolhouse Rock's "Rufus Xavier Sarsaparilla" teaches pronouns by showing how awkward it is to keep repeating names — [Ken's Home Page list](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~varnum/Schoolhouse.html)
- Possessive pronouns (his, hers, ours, its) never take an apostrophe — [NREL editorial standards, its and it's](https://www.nrel.gov/comm-standards/editorial/its-and-its)

### Inferences
- **Preposition cue accuracy flag (my analysis, based on how the cues work):** "mouse/squirrel/plane" cues only capture prepositions of place and direction. They miss of, during, since, until, despite, about, for, with, except and like, and they let in words that are not prepositions in a given sentence (for example adverb uses such as "He came in"). Teach Starter splitting place, time and direction into separate charts suggests some practitioners already work around this. A more accurate predictive cue is structural: "a preposition starts a phrase that ends in a noun or pronoun (its object)". That matches the Shurley definition and fits a symbol "bridge" from preposition to object.
- FANBOYS works fine as a K-5 starter list for "joining words that need a comma when both sides are complete sentences". For older or adult learners the curriculum could mark and/but/or/nor as the core group, and for/so/yet as "acts like a joiner".
- The AAAWWUBBIS / A WHITE BUS comma rule ("starts the sentence, then comma") makes a good predictable formula cue: [subordinator + clause] , [main clause].
- Schoolhouse Rock and Shurley give the category definitions in sung form, which suits a chant layer on top of the symbols.

### Gaps
- No source found for "anything a plane can do to a cloud" or the exact "anywhere a squirrel can go" wording.
- No source gave the full text of the 23 helping verbs chant (is, am, are, was, were, be, being, been / has, have, had / do, does, did / may, might, must / can, could / shall, should / will, would). That list is from general knowledge and needs a source before use.
- No research found on whether FANBOYS or AAAWWUBBIS specifically improve outcomes.
- Shurley jingle lyrics were not retrieved and are copyrighted, so they should not be reproduced.

## 2. Punctuation and capitalization (commas, apostrophes, dialogue, capitals, semicolons and colons, end marks)

### Takeaway
The strongest punctuation memory aids are physical or visual: Kung Fu Punctuation (a move and a sound for each mark), the "semicolon = period on top of a comma" picture, and substitution tests for apostrophes. Comma rules are usually taught as a short list of situations (lists, FANBOYS, introductory words, appositives, after a fronted dependent clause).

### Cited Findings
- **Kung Fu Punctuation** comes from Ros Wilson (founder of Big Writing). Each punctuation mark gets a kung fu sound and action. Examples: full stop = a short punch with "Ha!" (one school uses "Yah!"); comma = a bent arm with a wrist twist; capital letter = fingertips touching in an "A" shape plus a bow, with "Kai Shi". Lessons go up by belts, from 4 marks at white belt to 9 at black belt. There are three-player "bouts" (two combatants and a referee) — [Weis, Kung Fu Punctuation](https://weis.squarespace.com/blog/kung-fu-punctuation); [TES, "Kung fu commas" (Steve Eddison)](https://www.tes.com/news/english-kung-fu-commas); [TES, "Punctuation with a kick"](https://www.tes.com/news/english-punctuation-kick); [St John the Baptist Primary parent zone PDF](https://st-johnthebaptist-pri.s-lanark.sch.uk/sjtbps_parentzone_47_3433564580.pdf) (practitioner voice)
- Moves and sounds vary from school to school, so a curriculum should fix one set — [same sources as above](https://weis.squarespace.com/blog/kung-fu-punctuation)
- **Semicolon visual:** several handouts describe it as a period sitting on a comma, a "stop-pause" between the two marks or a "soft period". They also say a period would usually work as well — [Elite Content Marketer, "What do you get when a period climbs over a comma?"](https://elitecontentmarketer.com/semicolons); [UHCL Writing Center, "The Secret Life of Semicolons"](https://www.UHCL.EDU/writing-center/documents/tip-sheets/thesecretlifeofsemicolons.pdf); [TED-Ed, Emma Bryce, How to use a semicolon](https://www.ted.com/talks/emma_bryce_how_to_use_a_semicolon/transcript)
- **Comma with FANBOYS:** a comma goes before the conjunction when it joins two independent clauses, and a comma alone is a splice (see section 1) — [NPS Graduate Writing Center](https://library.nps.edu/web/gwc/commas-fanboys)
- **Comma after a fronted subordinate clause** (the AAAWWUBBIS comma) — [SlideShare](https://de.slideshare.net/TimCreighton4/subordinating-conjunctions-249265282)
- **Apostrophes, contraction vs possession:** for it's vs its, test whether "it is" or "it has" fits. Possessive "its" has no apostrophe, like "his". The apostrophe spelling of the possessive was very common in the 17th century, which is one reason the error feels natural — [Merriam-Webster, When to Use It's vs. Its](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/when-to-use-its-vs-its); [Merriam-Webster, Tangled History of It's and Its](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/the-tangled-history-of-its-and-its); [NREL](https://www.nrel.gov/comm-standards/editorial/its-and-its)
- In Kung Fu Punctuation, the capital letter has its own move, so capitalization gets taught together with punctuation — [Weis](https://weis.squarespace.com/blog/kung-fu-punctuation)

### Inferences
- Kung Fu Punctuation's "belt" ladder is already a predictable, levelled sequence, and it matches a symbol curriculum that introduces marks one at a time.
- A uniform "substitution test" cue (say the long form aloud; if it fits, use the apostrophe) works for it's, they're, you're and who's. That gives one predictable rule instead of four separate mnemonics.
- The semicolon picture explains the meaning (stronger than a comma, weaker than a period) as well as the shape, so it is accurate and also predictive.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve sources for: rules for quotation marks and dialogue punctuation (punctuation inside the quotes, a new paragraph for each new speaker); comma uses with appositives and introductory words; colon visual aids; capitalization mnemonics (names, "I", sentence start). Purdue OWL and Grammar Girl cover these, but those pages were not searched in this session.
- Talk for Writing (Pie Corbett) punctuation actions came up only alongside Kung Fu Punctuation. I found no source for its specific action set.
- I found no research on whether gesture-based punctuation programs improve outcomes.

## 3. Usage and spelling-grammar overlap (homophones, affect/effect, who/whom, agreement, irregular past, plurals, i before e)

### Takeaway
Most usage mnemonics are either letter-pattern hooks (there contains "here"; RAVEN; him/whom both end in m) or substitution tests (they are, it is, he/him). Substitution tests rely on the rule itself, so they are the most dependable. "I before e" is the clearest case of a famous mnemonic that is inaccurate unless it is narrowed.

### Cited Findings
- **There/their/they're:** "there" contains "here", so it is about place. The apostrophe in "they're" means "they are", which you can check by substituting it. "Their" shows ownership — [Washington State University, Paul Brians, Common Errors](https://brians.wsu.edu/2016/05/25/theyre-their-there/); [Open Oregon, Quick Fixes](https://openoregon.pressbooks.pub/coalescence/chapter/quick-fixes-their-there-theyre-them-and-those/); [Purdue OWL, words that sound alike](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/grammar/spelling_common_words_that_sound_alike.html)
- **Then/than:** THAN = comparison; THEN = sequence or time, and "then" answers "When?" — [Ellii, teaching tips on than and then](https://ellii.com/blog/teaching-tips-on-than-and-then); [ThriftyFun grammar tip](https://thriftyfun.com/Grammar-Tip-Than-vs-Then-1.html)
- **A lot / alot / allot:** "alot" is not a word; "allot" means to divide and share. One mnemonic: start with a lot, have it allotted, and you may end up with not much. (The "alligator" cue found in a search is a joke pairing, not a mnemonic for "a lot.") — [LibreTexts, Conventions 101 (Ramsey), A Lot / There / To Two Too](https://human.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Composition/Grammar/Conventions_101%3A_A_Functional_Approach_to_Teaching_(and_Assessing)_Grammar_and_Punctuation_(Ramsey)/01%3A_Chapters/1.13%3A_A_Lot_There_They're_Their_To_Two_Too); [Book Riot](https://bookriot.com/invented-words-for-common-writing-mistakes/)
- **Affect/effect (RAVEN):** "Remember Affect Verb, Effect Noun." Grammar Girl (Mignon Fogarty) adds "The craven raven flew down the avenue" as a letter hook (A-V-E-N). CDC clear-writing tips and the University of Waterloo writing centre use RAVEN too — [Quick and Dirty Tips (Grammar Girl)](https://quickanddirtytips.com/?p=41683); [CDC clear writing tip, 2024-07-31](https://www.cdc.gov/nceh/clearwriting/writing-tips/2024/writing-tip-wed-07-31-2024.html); [U Waterloo Writing and Communication Centre](https://uwaterloo.ca/writing-and-communication-centre/node/230)
- **RAVEN accuracy flag:** "effect" can be a verb ("to bring about"), and "affect" can be a noun in psychology. An educator also notes RAVEN fails for students who cannot yet tell nouns from verbs, and suggests a substitution test with "outcome" instead — [WeAreTeachers, Affect or Effect](https://www.weareteachers.com/affect-or-effect/); [Quick and Dirty Tips](https://quickanddirtytips.com/?p=41683)
- **Who/whom:** Grammar Girl's tip is that "him" goes with "whom", since both end in m. Substitute he/she to choose who, and him/her to choose whom. Example: "Whom do I trust? I trust him." Most speakers now use "who" even in object position, so the test matters mainly for formal writing — [Goodreads, Grammar Girl "Who vs. Whom"](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/1453502-who-vs-whom-which-is-correct); [Mental Floss](https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/598333/who-vs-whom-when-to-use); [Grammar Monster](https://www.grammar-monster.com/lessons/who_whom.htm)
- **Its/it's:** use the "it is / it has" test, and compare with "his" (see section 2) — [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/when-to-use-its-vs-its)
- **Irregular past tense:** irregular verbs do not follow the regular -ed pattern. Learners are told to look for shared patterns across the principal parts within each set, for example sing/sang/sung, where the past and past participle differ — [UNM Core Writing, Irregular Verbs](https://pressbooks.pub/unmcorewriting/?p=1966); [U Minnesota Voices handbook, 1.3 Verb Tense](https://open.lib.umn.edu/voiceshandbook/chapter/1-3-verb-tense)
- **i before e accuracy flag:** the short rule has many common exceptions. It works when limited to words where ie/ei says a clear long-e sound; otherwise sufficient, veil and their look like exceptions. One variant adds "or when sounded as 'a' as in neighbor and weigh". Language Log and a Cambridge excerpt discuss the rule, the latter saying the underlying norm (i before e) is sensible — [Wikipedia, I before E except after C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_before_E_except_after_C); [Merriam-Webster, I Before E Except After C](https://merriam-webster.com/grammar/i-before-e-except-after-c); [Merriam-Webster, Why "I Before E" Won't Always Help You](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/i-before-e-isnt-always-correct); [Language Log](https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=32229); [Government of Canada, Writing Tips Plus](https://our-languages.canada.ca/en/writing-tips-plus/spelling-words-with-ei-and-ie)

### Inferences
- For a predictable curriculum, prefer substitution tests (they are, it is, you are, he/him) over letter-trivia hooks, because they follow from the grammar. RAVEN works only after learners can already sort noun from verb, so it belongs after the noun and verb symbols are secure.
- "i before e" should be taught only in its narrowed form (long-e sound), with the common exceptions shown as a visible "rule-breaker" set, or left out.
- Irregular past forms fit visual "families" (sing/sang/sung, ring/rang/rung) better than a single rule.

### Gaps
- Not retrieved: subject-verb agreement mnemonics; plurals rules (-s/-es, y to ies, f to ves, irregular plurals); to/too/two cues (the common "too has too many o's" was not sourced); your/you're beyond the general substitution test. All need sources.
- I found no corpus count of ie/ei words that follow vs break the rule (Norvig's analysis is mentioned but was not retrieved).

## 4. Verb tense visuals (timelines, past/present/future symbols, tense charts)

### Takeaway
Timelines with one anchor chart per tense are the standard practitioner visual. Sources support pairing the same verb across past, present and future on a line, but I found no single standard symbol set for tense.

### Cited Findings
- Timelines help students picture the context for each tense and aspect and are paired with an anchor chart per tense. Regular past adds -ed, but irregular verbs must be learned separately (practitioner voice) — [Teacher Thrive, Verb Tenses and Timelines](https://teacherthrive.com/verb-tenses-timelines/)
- Textbooks model one verb across tenses, for example "Lauren keeps / kept / will keep all her letters" — [Saylor, Business English for Success 1.3 Verb Tense](https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_business-english-for-success/s05-03-verb-tense.html)
- Twinkl sells a past/present/future simple verbs poster (practitioner voice) — [Twinkl poster](https://www.twinkl.com/resource/t-e-298-simple-verbs-past-present-and-future-poster)
- The Government of Canada HyperGrammar gives a reference treatment of how tenses are formed and used — [HyperGrammar, forming and using verb tenses](https://our-languages.canada.ca/en/hypergrammar-2/forming-and-using-verb-tenses)

### Inferences
- A fixed left (past), middle (present), right (future) arrow, with perfect aspect as a box or bracket reaching back from a point, would give a predictable visual grammar. It is consistent with the timeline practice above but is my synthesis, not a sourced standard.
- The project's rule to say "past, present, future", never "yesterday, now, tomorrow", lines up with timeline labelling in these sources.

### Gaps
- I found no research comparing tense timelines with other methods for students with disabilities.
- I did not retrieve 12-tense grid charts (simple, progressive, perfect, perfect progressive by past, present, future) as a sourced standard.

## 5. Visual systems (Reed-Kellogg diagramming, Montessori grammar symbols, colour coding, anchor charts, gestures)

### Takeaway
Montessori grammar symbols (black triangle noun, red circle verb, and so on) and Colourful Semantics (colour by sentence role) are the two established symbol and colour systems most like Symbolic Grammar. Reed-Kellogg diagramming has a long history but weak evidence that it improves writing. Colour schemes vary across providers, so one system needs to fix a key.

### Cited Findings
**Montessori grammar symbols**
- Noun = large black triangle (linked to the pyramids). Verb = large red circle, the biggest symbol, placed at the centre because the other words depend on it (linked to the sun's constant movement). Article = small light-blue triangle. Adverb = small orange circle — [Montessori Mom, Grammar Symbols](https://montessorimom.com/grammar-symbols-lesson-62/); [Carrots Are Orange, Montessori grammar symbols](https://carrotsareorange.com/montessori-grammar-symbols/) (page could not be opened; host blocked); [Montessori Album talk page](https://www.montessorialbum.com/montessori/index.php/Talk:Grammar_Symbols); [AMI](https://montessori-ami.org/node/9256)
- Sizes and colours differ between suppliers — [Issuu, Montitute sample module](https://issuu.com/montessorian/docs/sample_module_6); [Montessori Services flipbook](https://s.montessoriservices.com/flip/inc/html/page28.html)
- From general Montessori knowledge (not confirmed in the retrieved summaries), the other symbols are usually adjective = medium dark-blue triangle, pronoun = purple isosceles triangle, preposition = green crescent/bridge, conjunction = pink bar, interjection = gold keyhole. **Verify before use** (listed again in Gaps).

**Colourful Semantics (Alison Bryan, speech and language therapist)**
- Colour by sentence role: orange = who, yellow = what doing (the verb, required in every sentence), green = what, blue = where, brown = when. Some services add purple for describing words. It is used with children who have speech, language and communication needs, including those not yet joining words — [Bradford Schools Online, Colourful Semantics info](https://bso.bradford.gov.uk/userfiles/file/!Learning%20Support/Colourful%20Semantics%20-%20Info%20and%20Resources.pdf); [Leicestershire Partnership NHS](https://www.leicspart.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Colourful-Semantics.pdf); [Just One Norfolk NHS](https://www.justonenorfolk.nhs.uk/speech-and-language/using-sentences/colourful-semantics/)
- **Evidence:** a 2018 PenCRU evidence briefing found small studies promising but the evidence base limited. Colour keys vary between services, and Bryan's 1997 coding has been updated — [Structural Learning teacher guide](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/colourful-semantics-a-teachers-guide); [Integrated Treatment Services PDF](https://integratedtreatmentservices.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/CS.pdf)

**Reed-Kellogg diagramming**
- The 1960 Encyclopedia of Educational Research concluded diagramming "teaches nothing beyond the ability to diagram". In 1985 NCTE called repetitive grammar drills, including diagramming, "a deterrent to the improvement of students' speaking and writing" — [Education Week opinion, "No, Virginia, Diagramming Will Not Improve Students' Writing" (2005)](https://www.edweek.org/ew/articles/2005/03/30/29schuster.h24.html); [Wikipedia, Sentence diagram](https://Www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentence_diagram)
- A 2021 action-research study with four middle-grade teacher candidates reported gains in grammatical knowledge and confidence after diagramming. The sample was tiny and there was no control group — [Georgia Southern, "Dotting, Slanting and Crossing Lines"](https://digitalcommons.georgiasouthern.edu/teach-secondary-facpubs/203)
- A Georgia Tech paper reports near-consensus among educators that diagramming helps some students, especially visual learners (opinion, not experimental evidence) — [Georgia Tech repository](https://repository.gatech.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/9fd693d5-ff43-42e6-96da-1602843731a6/content)
- Linguists often prefer modern tree diagrams to Reed-Kellogg — [Wikipedia, Reed-Kellogg sentence diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reed%E2%80%93Kellogg_sentence_diagram); [Language Log, Diagramming sentences](https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=4568); [NPR, The Fading Art of Diagramming Sentences](https://www.npr.org/sections/ed/2014/08/22/341898975)

**Gestures**
- Kung Fu Punctuation (see section 2) is the best-documented gesture system — [TES](https://www.tes.com/news/english-kung-fu-commas)

### Inferences
- Montessori symbols label word class (what a word is). Colourful Semantics labels sentence role (what job a word does: who, doing, what, where, when). A symbolic curriculum built around "reading a word's job" is closer to Colourful Semantics, so a two-layer design is worth considering: shape for word class, colour or position for role.
- Since both systems differ between providers, Symbolic Grammar should publish one fixed key and say how it maps to Montessori and Colourful Semantics, so students coming from either are not confused (for example, Montessori orange = adverb but Colourful Semantics orange = who).
- The research on diagramming warns that a visual system on its own will not transfer to writing unless it is tied to writing practice.

### Gaps
- I did not confirm Montessori symbols for adjective, pronoun, preposition, conjunction and interjection in retrieved summaries.
- Talk for Writing action sets, colour-coded grammar programs beyond Colourful Semantics (for example Fitzgerald Key, Shurley colour use), and modern diagramming alternatives were not retrieved.

## 6. Research on mnemonics, dual coding and songs/chants

### Takeaway
Mnemonic instruction has strong support for students with learning disabilities, mainly for remembering facts and vocabulary in content areas rather than grammar rules specifically. Dual coding gives a theoretical reason to pair words with meaningful visuals. Evidence that songs or chants help grammar is promising but thin.

### Cited Findings
- Scruggs and Mastropieri (1990) present mnemonic instruction as aimed at semantic memory difficulties, which they call among the most commonly cited traits of students with LD. They argue it has produced some of the largest gains in LD intervention research. This is a narrative review claim, not a pooled effect size — [Scruggs & Mastropieri 1990, KU-hosted PDF](https://simvilledev.ku.edu/sites/default/files/researc/SC32Scruggs.Mnemonic%20instruction%20for%20students.1990.pdf)
- Wolgemuth, Cobb and Alwell (2008) systematically reviewed 20 studies with 669 youth with LD, emotional and behavioural disorders, or mild developmental disabilities. They found support for mnemonic interventions improving academic performance across methods, settings, ages and disability types — [USF Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/esf_facpub/188)
- Swanson's 1999 meta-analysis of LD interventions reported an overall mean effect size of 0.79, with strategy instruction models ahead of the alternatives. That figure is for interventions in general, not mnemonics alone. **Attribution caution:** the search summary did not say which of these result pages holds the 0.79 figure — [PubMed, single-subject LD meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15505942/); [Review of Educational Research (SAGE)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.3102/00346543068003277)
- A Mastropieri/Scruggs co-authored content-area meta-analysis covered 68 investigations with 2,514 students, 80% with LD (summary did not give an effect size; **attribution caution:** the summary did not name which result page this came from, and journals.ku.edu is a best guess) — [journals.ku.edu](https://journals.ku.edu/focusXchild/article/download/6785/6139)
- **Dual coding:** Paivio (1971) proposed separate but connected verbal and nonverbal systems. Clark and Paivio (1991), "Dual Coding Theory and Education", is the key education paper. In teaching, visuals help when they add meaning, not as decoration. Propositional theorists dispute the theory's mechanism — [Wikipedia, Dual-coding theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual-coding_theory); [Clark & Paivio 1991, Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF01320076); [Central Michigan University teaching blog, 2026](https://www.cmich.edu/blog/office-of-curriculum-and-instructional-support-blog/2026/09/25/take-2-for-teaching-learning-dual-coding-helping-students-learn-through-words-and-visuals)
- **Songs and chants:** a 2018 Frontiers in Psychology classroom study had recently migrated primary children learn two songs over three sessions. They improved significantly, including on transfer of grammatical skills, and the gains held at retention testing. In a primary EFL study, children who sang outperformed children who only spoke the lyrics on spelling, vocabulary and grammar — [Frontiers in Psychology 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02386/full)
- A 2024 review of song, chant and nursery-rhyme interventions found no evidence of harm but could not show songs beat other methods. Grammar is studied less often than vocabulary or pronunciation. A 2020 synthesis of lyrics-based studies (1972 to 2019) asked whether a "song advantage" exists or whether singing has a cost — [Oxford Education Deanery digest](https://www.education.ox.ac.uk/oxford-education-deanery/digest/can-children-in-classrooms-learn-new-languages-through-songs-a-look-at-what-research-on-this-topic-can-tell-us/); [Oxford ORA](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:05751923-ec6e-4de7-bb2f-17860aa26e14/files/sgb19f835g)

### Inferences
- The mnemonic research supports using acronyms and keyword cues for memorizing lists (prepositions, subordinators, helping verbs) with LD learners. It does not show that mnemonics teach the underlying rule. So pair each list mnemonic with a structural test (for example, a preposition has an object; a subordinator makes a clause that cannot stand alone).
- Dual coding supports Symbolic Grammar's core idea, provided each symbol carries meaning (the verb circle at the centre "because the other words depend on it") rather than being arbitrary decoration.
- Chants are low risk and plausibly helpful for retention. They work best as one layer alongside symbols and practice.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve a specific Mastropieri and Scruggs keyword-mnemonic effect size (for example from Mastropieri et al. 1996). Do not quote a figure until it has been read in the source.
- I found no study of mnemonics for grammar or punctuation rules specifically with LD students.
- I found no controlled research on Montessori grammar symbols or Kung Fu Punctuation.
