## 1. Evidence/quote paragraph templates (quote sandwich, ICE, RACE/RACES, PEEL/TEEL, CER, They Say / I Say)

### Takeaway
All of these templates share one move sequence: a claim or topic, a lead-in that introduces the source, the evidence itself, the writer's explanation of the evidence, and a link back or close. That sequence matches the curriculum developer's five-sentence Level C evidence paragraph almost exactly. The research base for the named acronyms themselves is thin (RACE, PEEL/TEEL and They Say / I Say have practitioner support and critique, not controlled studies). The one acronym with real experimental evidence on fading is CER in science (McNeill et al., 2006).

### Cited Findings
- **Quote sandwich:** university writing centers agree on a three-part structure. Top bread: introduce the quote with a signal phrase naming the author and giving context. Filling: the quote with its citation. Bottom bread: explain what the quote means and how it supports your point, because readers will not make that connection on their own. Carnegie Mellon frames it as four moves (present your argument, introduce the quote, place it, interpret it). The CMU and Normandale handouts draw on They Say / I Say. Search summary, not opened. Sources: [St. Louis CC Quote Sandwich](https://stlcc.edu/student-support/academic-success-and-tutoring/writing-center/writing-resources/quote-sandwich.aspx); [PLU Integrating Quotations](https://www.plu.edu/writingcenter/integrating-quotations/); [CMU direct quotations handout](https://www.cmu.edu/student-success/other-resources/handouts/comm-supp-pdfs/direct-quotations-using-source-material.pdf); [Antioch tips for integrating quotations](https://www.antioch.edu/santa-barbara/wp-content/uploads/sites/4/2016/12/WRITING-CENTER_1.-Tips-for-Integrating-Quotations-final.pdf); [Ivy Tech quote sandwich](https://www.ivytech.edu/media/3hofh24q/quote-sandwhich.pdf); [Normandale Integrating Quotations](https://www.normandale.edu/current-students/tutoring-and-support/academic-support-centers/files/Integrating%20Quotations%20into%20Your%20Paragraphs.docx); [UW Odegaard Effective Quote Integration](https://depts.washington.edu/owrc/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/Effective-Quote-Integration.pdf); [CUNY City Tech Quote Sandwich](https://openlab.citytech.cuny.edu/composition/files/2021/03/The-Quote-Sandwich.pdf).
- **Source / citation sandwich variants** exist (Lake Superior College "Parts of a Paragraph and Source Sandwich", "Credibility Source Sandwich"; Western Washington University Libraries "Citation Sandwiches"). Search summary, not opened. Sources: [LSC Parts of a Paragraph and Source Sandwich](https://www.lsc.edu/wp-content/uploads/Parts-of-a-Paragraph-and-Source-Sandwich.pdf); [WWU Citation Sandwiches](https://library.wwu.edu/citation-sandwiches).
- **RACE / RACES:** Restate the prompt, Answer it, Cite evidence, Explain how the evidence supports the answer. RACES adds a final Summary sentence, and some versions use Explain as the last step. Teacher materials say it should be adjusted to students' age and ability. The search found **no empirical study of RACE**, only teaching guides. Search summary, not opened. Sources: [Purdue College of Science K-12 RACES](https://purdue.edu/science/K12/races.html); [Edutopia RACE video](https://www.edutopia.org/video/building-better-responses-with-the-race-writing-strategy); [Twinkl RACE](https://www.twinkl.com/teaching-wiki/race-writing-strategy); [Neshaminy RACE review](https://www.neshaminy.org/cms/lib/PA01000466/Centricity/Domain/1610/RACE%20Strategy%20Review.pdf). The last two are practitioner or vendor material.
- **TEEL / PEEL / PEE (Australia, UK):** Teacher educators writing in EduResearch Matters (Deakin repository: "Death by TEEL: Are formulas for writing harmful?") say TEEL is widely used in Australian schools and refer to the suggestion that students in England are experiencing "Death by PEEL". Search summary, not opened. Source: [Deakin DRO, Death by TEEL](https://dro.deakin.edu.au/articles/media/Death_by_TEEL_Are_formulas_for_writing_harmful_/20694850).
- **Practitioner voice (Leon Furze, English educator, blog):** TEEL "creates the illusion of success" and works like a cloze exercise rather than writing. He also says using it to support a struggling student or build confidence is "perfectly acceptable", that it gives early writers a toehold, and that scaffolds are meant to be removed. He notes examiners warn against "formulaic approaches". Practitioner opinion; search summary, not opened. Sources: [TEEL is a four-letter word](https://leonfurze.com/2021/09/11/teel-is-a-four-letter-word/); [In defence of TEEL? (Sort of. Not really)](https://leonfurze.com/2022/03/28/in-defence-of-teel-sort-of-not-really/); [Breaking out of TEEL](https://leonfurze.com/2022/04/29/breaking-out-of-teel/).
- Deakin's own Study Support guide still teaches TEEL/TEAL, but adds that styles differ by discipline and writer, so not every paragraph has to follow the exact structure. Search summary, not opened. Source: [Deakin paragraph structure guide](https://www.deakin.edu.au/students/study-support1/study-resources/study-support-guides/paragraph-structure).
- An Exeter PGCE English blog says the dominance of PEE/PEEL scaffolds is a key focus of research in English teaching. Search summary, not opened; the specific studies were not visible. Source: [Exeter PGCE English, research issues PEE/PEEL/PEELA](https://sites.exeter.ac.uk/pgceenglish/2020/01/13/research-issues-pee-peel-peela/).
- **CER (claim, evidence, reasoning) in science:** McNeill, Lizotte, Krajcik and Marx (2006), *Journal of the Learning Sciences* 15(2), DOI 10.1207/s15327809jls1502_1, compared continuous written scaffolds with faded written scaffolds for middle school students writing scientific explanations. Both groups gained on claim, evidence and reasoning. On posttest items **without** scaffolds, the faded group wrote stronger reasoning. The authors concluded that fading prepared students better to write explanations without support. Reasoning, meaning why the evidence supports the claim, was the hardest component. Search summary, not opened; sample size and effect sizes not verified. Sources: [Univ. of Arizona experts record](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/supporting-students-construction-of-scientific-explanations-by-fa/); [Deep Blue (UMich) full text](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/handle/2027.42/57509/20201_ftp.pdf?sequence=1).
- A 2018 *Instructional Science* follow-up looked at how teacher scaffolding complemented the faded material scaffolds. Search summary, not opened. Source: [Springer s11251-018-9474-0](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11251-018-9474-0).
- **They Say / I Say (Graff and Birkenstein):** Pomona's "Why Templates?" says less skilled writers often do not know the basic argumentative moves, that templates can teach them, and that templates are a stepping stone rather than an end point. Fuller and Pence (BYU, *Locutorium*, 2013) warn that templates can work against their purpose if used incorrectly. A Syracuse blog says writing teachers are sharply divided over the book. **No controlled study with basic or struggling writers was found.** Search summary, not opened. Sources: [Pomona, Why Templates?](https://www.pomona.edu/node/4633); [BYU Locutorium, Contemplating Templates](https://locutorium.byu.edu/issues/volume-8-2013-2/contemplating-templates); [Syracuse blog](https://ksoakes.expressions.syr.edu/blog/2016/02/10/they-sayi-say); [Illinois Writers Workshop template list](https://writersworkshop.illinois.edu/resources-2/writer-resources/academic-writing/they-say-i-say-templates/).
- The U.S. adult-education TEAL guide (LINCS) "Make Use of Frames" recommends writing frames as scaffolds that build confidence on unfamiliar writing tasks, and lists support for struggling writers among their benefits. This applies to her adult band. Search summary, not opened. Source: [LINCS TEAL, Make Use of Frames](https://lincs.ed.gov/state-resources/federal-initiatives/teal/guide/makeuse).

### Inferences
- Her five-sentence Level C evidence paragraph maps onto the quote sandwich like this: (1) introduction or topic sentence = claim, P or T; (2) sentence introducing the quote = top bread, signal phrase; (3) the quote = filling, E or C; (4) restating or supporting sentence = bottom bread, explain, the second E or R; (5) conclusion = link, L. She can say her model matches the most widely taught college and secondary structure without adopting any one acronym.
- CER's finding that reasoning is the weakest part points to slot 4 (restate or support) as the slot needing the most modeling and the richest set of formulas.
- Example formulas per slot. These are this researcher's design suggestions, not from a source, and need her symbol codes:
  - Slot 1, topic: a simple or compound claim sentence (subject + verb + complement), for example "Bats help farmers."
  - Slot 2, introduce the quote: introductory prepositional phrase or signal-verb frame. "In [title], [author] explains..." / "According to [source], ..." The fixed parts are a signal phrase plus a reporting verb (says, explains, shows, argues).
  - Slot 3, the quote: copied exactly, inside quotation marks, with its citation. This is the only slot with no formula to compose, but it trains quotation-mark punctuation.
  - Slot 4, restate or support: "This means that..." / "In other words, ..." (paraphrase), or a "because" complex sentence (reasoning). They Say / I Say "I say" frames fit here.
  - Slot 5, conclusion: "Therefore, ..." / "This shows that..." plus a restatement of slot 1 in new words.
- Fading path suggested by McNeill et al. and the writing-center sources: fixed frames for every slot, then frames for slots 2 and 4 only, then a choice from a menu of formulas, then no frames, with a checklist of moves only.

### Gaps
- No experimental or quasi-experimental studies found for RACE/RACES, ICE, ACE, PEEL/TEEL or They Say / I Say. The evidence is practitioner material.
- Could not open the Deakin "Death by TEEL" piece or the Exeter research summary, so their underlying studies are not named.
- McNeill et al. (2006) sample size, grade and effect sizes were not verified.
- No source specifically on ICE or ACE was retrieved.

## 2. Response-to-text and text-based writing research (Writing to Read, SRSD TIDE/TREE/POW+TREE, WWC secondary guide)

### Takeaway
Writing about texts improves reading comprehension (about 0.40 SD across 11 studies in Writing to Read). Summarization and strategy instruction are the two strongest adolescent writing interventions in Writing Next (ES 0.82 each). SRSD is the best-supported way to teach paragraph and essay part-mnemonics (TIDE, TREE, POW+TREE) to students with or at risk for disabilities, though evidence specific to TIDE is still mostly in progress. The WWC secondary guide recommends explicit strategy teaching through a Model-Practice-Reflect cycle and integrating reading and writing around the key features of text.

### Cited Findings
- **Writing to Read** (Graham and Hebert, 2010, Carnegie Corporation / Alliance for Excellent Education) is a meta-analysis drawing on 29 studies using norm-referenced tests and 55 using researcher-designed tests. Students who wrote about texts they read scored about 0.40 SD higher on reading tests than those who did not (average of 11 studies). The 0.40 figure comes from a synopsis, not the report table. Search summary, not opened. Sources: [LINCS profile](https://lincs.ed.gov/professional-development/resource-collections/profile-432); [Synopsis PDF](https://core-docs.s3.amazonaws.com/documents/asset/uploaded_file/776383/Synopsis_Writing_to_Read.pdf) (fetched, but it returned binary and could not be read); [Vanderbilt news release](https://news.vanderbilt.edu/2010/04/15/new-report-finds-writing-can-be-powerful-driver-for-improving-reading-skills-112797).
- **Writing Next** (Graham and Perin, 2007): strategy instruction ES 0.82 and summarization ES 0.82 were the two strongest of 11 interventions. Sentence combining was ES 0.50 (n = 5). Search summary, not opened. Sources: [Writing Next full text, Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org/sites/default/files/guide/writing-next-2007_0.pdf); [Oregon ODE synopsis](https://www.oregon.gov/ode/educator-resources/essentialskills/Documents/essentialskillwriting_hs_level2_writing_next_synopsis.pdf); [AdLit summary](https://www.adlit.org/topics/writing/summary-writing-next).
- **WWC practice guide "Teaching Secondary Students to Write Effectively"** (NCEE 2017-4002, Nov 2016, panel chaired by Steve Graham, grades 6-12, based on 15 studies meeting WWC standards). Recommendations: (1) explicitly teach appropriate writing strategies using a Model-Practice-Reflect instructional cycle; (2) integrate writing and reading to emphasize key writing features; (3) use assessments of student writing to inform instruction and feedback. The evidence level for each recommendation was not visible (the guide host was blocked). Search summary, not opened. Sources: [WWC summary PDF](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_secwrit_summary_053117.pdf); [WWC practice guide page](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/practiceguide/22); [IES blog](https://ies.ed.gov/learn/blog/recommendations-teaching-secondary-students-write-effectively); [Nebraska DOE Recommendation 1](https://www.education.ne.gov/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/Teaching-Secondary-Students-to-Write-Effectively-Recommendation-1.pdf).
- **TIDE (Topic, Important details, Explain details, Ending):** "Turning the TIDE" is an IES-funded project testing SRSD professional development and coaching (in person versus online versus business as usual) for co-teachers of students with or at risk for disabilities. It plans for 72 teachers and 180 students. The listing cites prior evidence with grade 3 general education teachers (Collins et al., 2021). No outcomes are published in the listing. Search summary, not opened. - **WWC SRSD intervention report:** evidence from single-case design studies with students with specific learning disabilities is rated "potentially positive effects" on writing achievement. 88% (15 of 17) of the writing-achievement single-case experiments showed a positive effect. Search summary, not opened. Source: [WWC SRSD report 2017](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/InterventionReports/wwc_srsd_111417.pdf).
- **SRSD meta-analytic figures** (secondary citations): Graham and Harris (2003), average ES 1.17 for writing quality, cited by the vendor SRSD Online. Graham et al. (2013), ES 2.37 for SRSD with students with LD, cited secondhand in a review. Source: [UNC Write On NC, Middle School Writing Project](https://www.med.unc.edu/ahs/writeonnc/the-middle-school-writing-project/).
- An IES award describes a scaffolded, procedural writing intervention for adolescents with behavior disorders. Search summary, not opened, no results seen. Source: [IES award, behavior disorders writing](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/writing-instruction-adolescents-behavior-disorders-scaffolding-procedural-learning-extended).

### Inferences
- TIDE (informative) and TREE (opinion) are paragraph-part mnemonics with the same shape as her evidence paragraph: topic, detail or reason, explain, ending. Her five slots could be taught with SRSD's stages (develop background knowledge, discuss it, model it, memorize it, support it, independent performance). That puts her formulas inside an evidence-rated delivery method rather than leaving them as a stand-alone template.
- Because summarization (0.82) and writing about text (about 0.40 on reading) are among the best-supported practices, slot 4 (restating or paraphrasing the quote) carries both writing and reading benefits. It is worth extra lessons.
- The "Explain" step appears in TIDE, TREE, RACE, CER and the quote sandwich alike, and CER research found it hardest. Every framework flags the same slot as the one that needs teaching.

### Gaps
- Evidence levels for each WWC secondary guide recommendation were not visible.
- Turning the TIDE results are not yet published, so no TIDE-specific effect size for students with disabilities was found.
- No study found that teaches quoting and paraphrasing source text specifically to students with disabilities. A search for one returned SRSD, sentence fluency and technology studies instead.
- "PLAN & WRITE" (SRSD) and text-dependent analysis (TDA, e.g., Pennsylvania) were not covered; no searches were left in the budget.

## 3. The five-paragraph essay: history, defenses, critiques, and fading paths

### Takeaway
The five-paragraph essay grew out of older theme-writing traditions (Nunes, 2013). Critics such as Wiley (2000) and Warner (2018) say the harm comes less from the structure than from dependency on it, made worse by testing. Defenders call it a useful beginner and timed-writing scaffold. The most concrete fading path found is The Writing Revolution's outline sequence: Single Paragraph Outline, then Pre-Transition and Transition Outlines (2.0 edition, 2025), then Multiple Paragraph Outlines of varying paragraph counts. That sequence lets the number of paragraphs vary rather than fixing it at five.

### Cited Findings
- Matthew Nunes (2013, *Rhetoric Review*) argues the form grew out of theme-writing with roots in English education and classical rhetoric, not only current-traditional rhetoric. Search summary, not opened. Sources: [Rhetoric Review abstract](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/07350198.2013.797877); [O'Donnell blog, The History of the Form Revisited](https://people.uleth.ca/~daniel.odonnell/blog/the-history-of-the-form-revisited).
- Mark Wiley, "The Popularity of Formulaic Writing (And Why We Need to Resist)", *English Journal* (2000). He interviewed high school teachers about why they kept teaching the form, and concluded the problem is less the structure than the dependency teachers build on it. Search summary of a secondary document, not opened. Source: [Scribd document citing Wiley](https://www.scribd.com/document/487851414/wp2final).
- John Warner, *Why They Can't Write: Killing the Five-Paragraph Essay and Other Necessities* (Johns Hopkins UP, 2018), blames the form's spread on standardized assessment and accountability policy, and calls the format a prescriptive process. Search summary, not opened. Sources: [Project MUSE listing](https://muse.jhu.edu/book/61976); [Slashdot excerpt of Pacific Standard piece](https://news.slashdot.org/story/18/12/24/1226232/the-five-paragraph-essay-must-die).
- NCTE blog (2021), "Unmasking the Mythical Narrative Surrounding the Five-Paragraph Essay": discusses defenders who call it a useful beginner and timed-writing scaffold, and quotes Warner. Education Week (2016), "Is the Five-Paragraph Essay History?", reports critics' view that it can turn from support into a straitjacket. Search summary, not opened. Sources: [NCTE blog](https://ncte.org/blog/2021/11/unmasking-mythical-narrative-surrounding-five-paragraph-essay/); [Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/is-the-five-paragraph-essay-history/2016/06).
- **The Writing Revolution (Hochman and Wexler):** the Single Paragraph Outline is a "linear and simple" planning tool for one paragraph, also used for summarizing (summary sentence plus SPO). Chapter 7 of the original book moves to compositions through the Multiple Paragraph Outline, which comes in 3-, 4- and 5-paragraph versions plus a book-report version. The 2.0 edition (Wiley, April 2025) adds a Pre-Transition Outline and a Transition Outline (2- to 5-paragraph variants) between the SPO and the MPO. The method stresses explicit, sequenced instruction from sentences to compositions, with planning and revising as the key phases. Search summary, not opened (the host is blocked). Sources: [TWR method page](https://thewritingrevolution.org/?p=507); [TWR Chapter 8 resources](https://www.thewritingrevolution.org/resources/book-resources/chapter-8/); [MPO 5 template](https://www.thewritingrevolution.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/MPO-5.pdf); [Wiley listing, TWR 2.0](https://www.wiley-vch.de/en/areas-interest/humanities-social-sciences/the-writing-revolution-2-0-978-1-394-35990-5).
- No independent effectiveness study of The Writing Revolution was found in this session's searches. Treat it as a practitioner program.

### Inferences
- A fading path consistent with these sources and with McNeill et al. (2006): (a) one evidence paragraph with all five slots framed; (b) the same paragraph planned on an SPO-style outline using key words, abbreviations and her symbols instead of full sentences; (c) two evidence paragraphs joined by a transition, as in the TWR Transition Outline; (d) a multiple-paragraph outline with 3 to 5 body paragraphs chosen by the writer; (e) a structure-choice step (compare/contrast, cause/effect, problem/solution) so five paragraphs becomes one option rather than the rule.
- The critiques (Wiley's dependency, Furze's cloze-exercise point) argue for building the removal of each frame into the curriculum from the start: a visible "frame off" level for every template, not optional enrichment.

### Gaps
- No controlled study comparing fixed five-paragraph instruction with a faded or flexible approach was found.
- The exact contents of the TWR outline templates were not confirmed (host blocked).
- Michelle Tremmel's history of the genre appears only as a reference-list title; not reviewed.

## 4. Research paper and summary writing for older or struggling students (summarizing, paraphrasing, quote integration, notes to paragraph)

### Takeaway
Summarization instruction has one of the largest effects in adolescent writing (ES 0.82, Writing Next), and writing summaries and extended responses about texts supports reading. The clearest notes-to-paragraph routine found is TWR's summary sentence and Single Paragraph Outline. Quote-integration teaching rests almost entirely on writing-center handouts (the quote sandwich), with no intervention studies found for struggling writers.

### Cited Findings
- Summarization ES 0.82 (Writing Next, Graham and Perin, 2007). Search summary, not opened. Source: [Writing Next, Reading Rockets PDF](https://www.readingrockets.org/sites/default/files/guide/writing-next-2007_0.pdf).
- Writing about texts improves reading by about 0.40 SD (Writing to Read, 11 studies). Search summary, not opened. Source: [LINCS profile](https://lincs.ed.gov/professional-development/resource-collections/profile-432).
- TWR teaches summarizing in two formats, the summary sentence and the SPO. Search summary, not opened. Source: [TWR method page](https://thewritingrevolution.org/?p=507).
- Quote integration steps (signal phrase, quote with citation, interpretation) are standard writing-center content. A signal phrase can carry part of the in-text citation. Search summary, not opened. Sources: [LSC Credibility Source Sandwich](https://www.lsc.edu/wp-content/uploads/Credibility-Source-Sandwich.pdf); [Moorpark College Quotes](https://www.moorparkcollege.edu/sites/moorparkcollege/files/media/document/2026/Quotes.pdf); [Lumen, Bringing Sources into the Conversation](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/wm-writingskillslab/chapter/bringing-sources-into-the-conversation/).
- Frames and scaffolds for adult learners on unfamiliar writing tasks: [LINCS TEAL, Make Use of Frames](https://lincs.ed.gov/state-resources/federal-initiatives/teal/guide/makeuse). Search summary, not opened.

### Inferences
- For Level C research writing, a notes-to-paragraph routine could run: source → key-word notes (with her symbols marking each note's job) → SPO with T (topic), one detail line per slot, C (conclusion) → five-slot paragraph → frame-free paragraph. Slot 4 (restate or paraphrase) doubles as summarization practice, the highest-effect element found.
- Paraphrase can be taught as a formula-change task: keep the meaning, change the sentence formula (for example, turn a quoted simple sentence into a "because" complex sentence). This fits a symbol-based curriculum. It is a design suggestion, not from a source.

### Gaps
- No intervention study on teaching paraphrasing or quote integration to students with disabilities or adult struggling writers was found.
- The GIST summarization strategy and the Writing to Read practice-by-practice effect sizes (extended response, note-taking, answering questions) could not be retrieved; the synopsis PDF returned unreadable binary.
- The Purdue OWL was not reached (purdue.edu blocked).
