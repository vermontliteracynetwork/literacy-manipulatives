## 1. Kernel sentences and sentence expansion (The Writing Revolution, Keys to Literacy, Hochman sequence, research on expansion)

### Takeaway
Kernel-then-expand is a consistent practitioner routine across The Writing Revolution (TWR), Keys to Literacy and Reading Universe: start from a bare noun + verb kernel, then answer question words (who/what/when/where/why/how) and fold the answers into one sentence. Most where/when answers are prepositional phrases, so this is the natural home for her PP growth data. Evidence specifically for "sentence expansion" as an isolated technique is thin; TWR's evidence is developer-commissioned and quasi-experimental.

### Cited Findings
- TWR sentence expansion starts from a short "kernel" (examples: "She started a movement," "Pyramids were built") carrying only basic noun + verb information; students answer who/what/when/where/why/how prompts and combine the answers into one expanded sentence. Teachers often supply a kernel with a clear who/what and prompt only for when/where/why. Uses: content review, comprehension checks, editing own writing. (search summary, not opened) — [BCPS TWR sentence expansion page](https://sites.google.com/bcps.k12.md.us/ewhs-pl-and-literacy/writing-revolution/sentence-expansion); [TWR Rosa Parks Sentence Expansion template](https://www.thewritingrevolution.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/Rosa-Parks-Sentence-Expansion.pdf); [TWR Spring Sentence Expansion](https://www.thewritingrevolution.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/Spring-Sentence-Expansion.pdf)
- Some TWR templates use only who/where/when/why; others use where/when/why/how, i.e., the prompt set is adjusted to the kernel. (search summary, not opened) — [TWR Rosa Parks template](https://www.thewritingrevolution.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/Rosa-Parks-Sentence-Expansion.pdf)
- Reading Universe (WETA) has a "Sentence Expansion Skill Explainer" with a lesson plan, an independent-practice page, and a video titled "using function questions to write expanded sentences" (the question words are framed as "function questions"). (search summary, not opened) — [Reading Universe lesson plan](https://readinguniverse.org/resources/pdf/sentence-writing/sentence-expansion-skill-explainer/lesson-plan-for-sentence-expansion); [Reading Universe video](https://readinguniverse.org/resources/video/sentence-writing/sentence-expansion-skill-explainer/using-function-questions-to-write-expanded-sentences); [Explicitly teaching sentence expansion](https://readinguniverse.org/skill-explainer/sentence-writing/sentence-expansion-skill-explainer/explicitly-teaching-sentence-expansion)

Example: "turtle dives" becomes "The small, green turtle dives quickly." - A UK Research School article describes kernel expansion as asking "where, when, how, and why," with the kernel defined as subject + verb with no modifiers or connectives. (search summary, not opened) — [Research School Town End, "Stringing strong sentences"](https://researchschool.org.uk/town-end/news/stringing-strong-sentences)
- University of Iowa Reading Research Center publishes elementary and middle-school "Sentence Expanding" example lessons; the middle-school version models answering when/where/why/how to expand a simple sentence. (search summary, not opened) — [Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/scientific-evidence-on-how-to-teach-writing-is-slim/)

### Inferences
- Question-word expansion maps directly onto her corpus finding: where/when (and often how) answers are typically prepositional phrases, so "expand the kernel with a where/when phrase" is the instructional mechanism by which PP density can rise from ~0.6 to ~2.4 per sentence.
- Keys to Literacy's fixed order (subject elaboration, predicate elaboration, add a phrase, combine) is the closest existing precedent for a procedurally fixed sentence routine; it could be the "skeleton" of a formula-family lesson.
- Evidence level for kernel expansion alone: practitioner consensus plus developer-commissioned quasi-experimental data. It rides on the stronger evidence for sentence combining and sentence-construction instruction (section 2) rather than standing on its own.

### Gaps
- Full step-by-step Hochman sequence (kernel, because/but/so, appositives, subordinating conjunctions, sentence types, fragments) could not be opened. From background knowledge (not verified this session): TWR's sentence strategies also include because/but/so, appositives, subordinating conjunctions and sentence types; verify against Hochman & Wexler (2017), *The Writing Revolution* (Jossey-Bass) before citing.
- No peer-reviewed study isolating "sentence expansion" (as opposed to combining) as an intervention was found.
- NORC study effect sizes not retrieved.

## 2. Sentence combining, sentence construction for struggling writers, frames, imitation/mentor sentences, Image Grammar

### Takeaway
Sentence combining is the best-evidenced sentence-level practice (Graham & Perin 2007, ES = 0.50 across 5 studies; earlier Hillocks 1984; foundational Mellon 1969 and O'Hare 1973), and it outperforms traditional grammar instruction, which showed negative effects on text quality in older meta-analyses. Datchuk's Direct Instruction + Precision Teaching sentence-construction work is the most relevant line for students with disabilities (small-group and single-case designs, positive but sometimes inconsistent). Imitation/mentor-sentence approaches (Killgallon, Anderson's Patterns of Power, Noden) are practitioner-driven with little controlled evidence.

### Cited Findings
**Sentence combining**
- Graham & Perin (2007, *Writing Next*, grades 4-12): sentence combining ES = 0.50 (n = 5 studies); grammar instruction did not improve writing. (search summary, not opened) — [Oregon DOE Writing Next synopsis](https://www.oregon.gov/ode/educator-resources/essentialskills/Documents/essentialskillwriting_hs_level2_writing_next_synopsis.pdf)
- Koster et al. review: three meta-analyses consistently found grammar instruction had a negative effect on text quality, ES from -.29 (Hillocks, 1984) to -.41 (Graham et al., 2012); Hillocks (1984) and Graham & Perin (2007) both found sentence combining positive. (search summary, not opened) — [Journal of Writing Research article](https://jowr.org/jowr/article/download/661/627/557)
- Conflict: a publisher blog summarizing a more recent meta-analysis reports grammar instruction ES = 0.77; this contradicts the older results and was not verified against the original (secondary, commercial source). (search summary, not opened) — [Savvas research recap](https://savvas-sc-assets.savvas.com/resource-center/blogs-and-podcasts/savvas-insights/science-of-reading-research-recap/a-study-on-most-effective-writing-strategies); contradicted by [Koster et al. in JoWR](https://jowr.org/jowr/article/download/661/627/557)

- Saddler, Behforooz & Asaro (2008) studied sentence combining with fourth graders with writing difficulties; Saddler & Graham (2005) studied peer-assisted sentence combining with more and less skilled young writers. ### Inferences
- Evidence tiers for the report: (A) sentence combining: meta-analytic, moderate positive; (B) explicit sentence-construction instruction for students with disabilities (Datchuk): single-case/small-group, positive; (C) kernel expansion (TWR/Keys): practitioner consensus, developer-commissioned quasi-experimental; (D) imitation/mentor sentences (Killgallon, Anderson, Noden): practitioner, no controlled trials found; (E) traditional decontextualized grammar: negative or null in most meta-analyses, one contested newer positive report.
- The negative grammar-instruction findings are about naming/parsing in isolation; Symbolic Grammar's justification should rest on the combining/construction lineage (manipulating real sentences toward a target structure), not on parsing alone.
- Killgallon's four operations (unscramble, imitate, combine, expand) are a ready-made set of fixed daily "moves" that could be applied to each formula family.

- No effect sizes for Datchuk studies; no meta-analysis of sentence frames/stems found.
- Bob Shepherd: no source found in this session.
- IES Rec. 3 evidence level not confirmed (believed "strong" from memory; verify).

## 3. Explicit teaching of prepositions and prepositional phrases

### Takeaway
Practitioner methods converge on: define prepositions explicitly, teach PPs as answering where/when, act them out with objects or bodies, and move phrases around in the sentence. Research on preposition learning comes mostly from SLP/DLD work (locative prepositions, errorless learning, input variability, classroom spatial-term programs) and is small-scale. No writing-intervention study targeting PP use in written sentences was found.

### Cited Findings
- Victoria (Australia) Dept. of Education example lesson: explain that words such as "at," "between," "over" are prepositions and phrases beginning with them are prepositional phrases; students move phrases to test sentence positions; acting out prepositions for classmates to guess (noted as helpful for English learners). (search summary, not opened) — [Victoria DET teaching grammar example](https://www.education.vic.gov.au/school/teachers/teachingresources/discipline/english/literacy/writing/Pages/exampleteachgrammar.aspx)
- Practitioner materials frame PPs as telling where or when something happens; one guide recommends about one to three PPs per sentence, and a Hawaii handout notes long PP strings sound poor. (search summary, not opened) — [St. Louis CC Prepositional Phrases handout](https://stlcc.edu/docs/student-support/academic-support/college-writing-center/prepositional-phrases.pdf); [U. ### Inferences
- For her learners: a concrete-to-symbolic path (move real objects in/on/under, then picture, then symbol card, then written PP) is supported by SLP practice and fits CRA; the DLD work suggests keeping object/label variability low at first, then widening.
- Teaching PPs explicitly as "the where/when piece you can move" connects the SLP spatial-concept work, the TWR question-word expansion, and Victoria's move-the-phrase activity into one family operation ("add a PP," "move the PP").
- Temporal PPs (before/after/during) may need separate, later teaching than locative ones, given DLD difficulty with before/after.

### Gaps
- No controlled study of explicit PP instruction improving written sentences in school-age writers was found.
- No sourced developmental acquisition order of English prepositions (in/on/under before behind/next to, etc.) was retrieved this session; widely cited in SLP norms but not verified here.

## 4. Linguistic and school-grammar justifications for grouping formulas into families

### Takeaway
Two traditions justify "families": school grammar's small set of basic sentence patterns (Kolln: 7 or 10 patterns such as S-V, S-V-O, S-LV-PN, S-LV-PA) defined by verb type, with modifiers like PPs layered on; and construction grammar (Goldberg), where sentence-level argument-structure constructions are form-meaning pairs independent of the particular words. Croft explicitly recommends studying "a single construction or family of constructions." Both support grouping by kernel pattern and treating PPs as add-ons, which matches her corpus (stable kernels, growing PPs, rarely repeated exact formulas).

### Cited Findings
- Martha Kolln's *Rhetorical Grammar* lays out seven basic sentence patterns per one guide; another, citing Kolln & Funk (2006), lists ten, covering statements only (not questions or interjections). Sources conflict on the count, likely due to different books/editions. (search summary, not opened) — [U. Arkansas Pressbooks "16 Common Sentence Patterns"](https://uark.pressbooks.pub/journalismgsp/chapter/sentence-patterns/); [UHV sentence patterns](https://www.uhv.edu/curriculum-student-achievement/student-success/student-resources/q-z/sentence-patterns)
- Pattern definitions: S-V (intransitive, no complement); S-V-O (transitive, direct object receives action); S-LV-PN (linking verb + noun renaming subject; subject and PN can often be flipped); S-LV-PA (linking verb + adjective describing subject; test by substituting is/are). Terminology varies (subject complement, S-LV-AC). (search summary, not opened) — [TCC grammar sentence patterns](https://www.tcc.edu/wp-content/uploads/archive/writing-center-handouts/grammar-sentence-patterns.pdf); [Towson OWS sentence patterns](https://webapps.towson.edu/ows/sentpatt.htm)
- Goldberg (1995) defines constructions as conventionalized form-meaning pairs; Goldberg (2006, *Constructions at Work*) drops non-compositionality as a requirement; argument-structure constructions (ditransitive, caused-motion, way-construction) can supply meaning beyond the verb. (search summary, not opened) — [Goldberg 2003, Trends in Cognitive Sciences](https://legacy.cs.indiana.edu/~port/teach/sem05/Goldberg.constrctns.TrCgSci.03.pdf); [UCLouvain paper](https://research.dial.uclouvain.be/bitstreams/f07584ba-acb2-4d84-acc1-fe2978451f07/download)
- Croft (LLAS guide): no construction-grammar textbook exists; recommends learners use corpora to select and analyze "a single construction or family of constructions." (search summary, not opened) — [LLAS Good Practice Guide 141](https://web-archive.southampton.ac.uk/www.llas.ac.uk/resources/gpg/141.html)
- Pedagogical construction grammar: Kim & Rah (TESOL Quarterly, ~2020/21) tested construction-based instruction on Korean EFL learners' production of argument structure constructions; Boas (ed., 2022) *Directions for Pedagogical Construction Grammar*; a review notes L2 teaching is under-researched within construction grammar. (search summary, not opened; dates inconsistent in metadata) — [Cambridge Element, Constructionist Approaches](https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/constructionist-approaches/4F37E9A2B97EB663F532B4068702055C); [John Benjamins, Pragmatics & Language](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/pl.25006.rom); [Université de Neuchâtel paper](https://libra.unine.ch/server/api/core/bitstreams/f99fe539-0054-4ac7-ad15-3f7e6f6eaf67/content)

### Inferences
- A defensible family scheme: family = kernel pattern (S-V, S-V-O, S-LV-N, S-LV-Adj, plus question/command variants); members = the kernel plus add-on slots (PP-where, PP-when, adjective, adverb, conjunction). This explains why exact formulas rarely repeat while families stay stable.
- The phonics analogy holds structurally: like a word family (-at: cat, hat), a formula family has a fixed "rime" (the kernel) and variable "onsets/add-ons." Construction grammar's form-meaning pairing also supports teaching what each family means (doing, being, describing), not only its shape.
- Evidence that teaching sentences grouped by pattern improves school-age L1 writing was not found; this is a theoretically grounded design choice, not an empirically validated one.

### Gaps
- Kolln's exact list (7 vs 10) unverified; primary text not opened.
- No L1 child-writing study of construction-based instruction found.

## 5. Structured-literacy routines (UFLI, OG, cumulative review, gradual release) applied to sentences and writing

### Takeaway
UFLI's 8-step lesson (review steps 1-4, new concept step 5, apply in words/text steps 6-8 including sentence dictation) and generic structured-literacy principles (explicit, systematic, cumulative, daily review) are well documented, but no packaged program was found that applies a UFLI-style fixed routine to sentence patterns. The closest are Datchuk's 10-15 minute explicit sentence block with fluency review, Keys to Literacy's fixed elaboration order, and Patterns of Power's fixed weekly day sequence.

### Cited Findings
- UFLI Foundations: Steps 1-4 warm-up and review of previously taught concepts; Step 5 explicit introduction of a new concept with guided reading and spelling; Steps 6-8 apply at word and text level (Step 7 irregular words, Step 8 connected text). Step 8 is ~15 minutes: read sentences, then dictated sentences (example "Tom fed the big pet."). ### Inferences
- A UFLI-parallel "formula family" lesson can be assembled from evidenced parts: (1) review prior families with quick symbol-card reads (cumulative review); (2) explore with objects/manipulatives (SLP/CRA); (3) connect: name the new family/add-on explicitly with examples and non-examples (explicit instruction); (4) see: mentor sentence from a passage, notice the pattern (Patterns of Power Day 1); (5) write: unscramble, then imitate (Killgallon); (6) apply: expand a kernel with question words, combine two kernels (TWR, Keys, sentence combining); (7) dictate one sentence in the family (UFLI Step 8); (8) review/fluency timing (Datchuk precision teaching).
- Her "explore, connect, see, write, apply, review" sequence aligns closely with this assembly; the main novelty is applying the fixed order to sentence families, which the field has not packaged.
