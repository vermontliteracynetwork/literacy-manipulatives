## (a) Oral sentence rehearsal, shared/interactive writing, and what makes oral work transfer to writing

### Takeaway
"Say it, then write it" is widely recommended and has some theory behind it (Myhill), plus early correlational evidence that preschoolers' written sentences match their rehearsed oral sentences better when their transcription (spelling, handwriting) is stronger. Controlled evidence for young children is thin. Interactive writing ("sharing the pen") improved first graders' independent writing in one quasi-experimental study, but in kindergarten it did no better than Writing Workshop. The pattern across sources is that oral work transfers when it is tied directly to a written product, not done on its own.

### Cited findings
- Myhill (2009, *British Journal of Educational Studies*) says "oral rehearsal" is commonplace in English classrooms and policy documents but "not well-theorised" as a concept. The paper combines a systematic literature review with video of early years classrooms showing young writers rehearsing orally before and during writing. (search summary, not opened) — [How talk becomes text (PhilArchive record)](https://dc2.philarchive.org/rec/MYHHTB-2); [Exeter repository record](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10036/4474?show=full)
- A Quinn and colleagues study of 245 children aged 3 to 5 reportedly found that children with more advanced transcription skills had a closer match between their pre-writing oral sentence and the sentence they then wrote. A later Quinn and Rohloff study of 165 children aged 3 to 6 reportedly linked more sophisticated spelling strategies to better connection between ideas before and after writing. (search summary only; a second, targeted search could not confirm the exact 2021 paper, so treat author/year as unverified) — [search result citing this work, Springer 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10643-026-02207-7)
- Related Quinn-group papers found in a faculty listing: Quinn, Bingham & Gerde (2021) "Who writes what when?: Examining children's early composing," *Reading and Writing*; Rohloff, Ridley, Quinn & Zhang (2024) on "idea transformations" from pre-writing to post-writing verbalizations; Quinn & Rohloff (2024) framing composition as transcription, connection and discourse. (titles from search summary, not opened) — [Margaret Quinn, Texas A&M directory](https://dp.education.tamu.edu/directory/view/1770)
- A 2024 study (per search summary) found children's oral and written story retellings strongly correlated (not a preschool sentence study). (search summary, not opened) — [Frontiers in Psychology 2023/2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1236652/pdf)
- Sanchez (2019), a first-grade action research project, reported that oral rehearsal "alleviated the cognitive challenges" emergent writers face holding ideas in mind while writing. Action research, low rigor. (search summary, not opened) — [UNH Scholars record](https://scholars.unh.edu/dissertation/1937) (note: the search summary attributes the high school study to Aubry 1997 at a UNH dissertation link; the exact mapping of authors to links was not verifiable)
- EEF guidance: saying ideas aloud before writing means pupils "are not hindered by handwriting or spelling" and gives "low-stakes opportunities for pupils to orally rehearse" a written response. Guidance, not a trial. (search summary, page blocked) — [EEF, From talk to text](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/talk-to-text-integrating-oral-language)
- Roth & Guinee (2011/2012), Interactive Writing in first grade: 101 first graders in five Boston public schools; Interactive Writing for as little as about 10 minutes a day, on top of Writing Workshop, improved independent writing across traits including organization and spelling. Caveat: teachers volunteered to learn the method, limiting generalization. (press summaries, not opened) — [Education Week 2012](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/sharing-the-pen-helps-first-graders-learn-to-write-study-says/2012/02); [New America summary](https://www.newamerica.org/insights/study-shows-gains-in-writing-by-sharing-the-pen-in-first-grade/)
- Jones, Reutzel & Fargo (2010), kindergarten: Interactive Writing vs Writing Workshop; both groups grew significantly over time, with no significant difference between them (outcomes were reading skills). (search summary, not opened) — [Comparing two methods of writing instruction (USU)](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/teal_facpub/576)
- Language Experience Approach (child dictates, adult writes, child rereads): no systematic review of LEA outcomes was found. A Victorian (Australia) education department page presents it as a "high impact, explicit teaching approach" for early years, which is guidance, not evaluation. The REL Southeast review of 20 years of early literacy research (132 interventions, 109 studies, 38 effective in at least one domain) did not show in summary whether any LEA-style programs were among the effective ones. (search summaries, not opened) — [Victoria Dept of Education, Language experience](https://www.education.vic.gov.au/school/teachers/teachingresources/discipline/english/literacy/Pages/language-experience.aspx); [REL Southeast 2021-084](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/rel/regions/southeast/pdf/REL_2021084.pdf)
- EEF's 2019 Literacy Development Evidence Review cautions that literacy "is unlikely to be achieved without explicit and prolonged instruction" (relevant caution for approaches that rely mostly on child-generated text). (search summary, not opened) — [Literacy Development Evidence Review (UCL)](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10083973/1/Literacy_Development_Evidence_Review.pdf)
- IES Foundational Skills K-3 practice guide (2016, NCEE 2016-4008), Recommendation 1: teach academic language, meaning inferential language, narrative language (clearly describing a sequence of events), and academic vocabulary and grammatical structures; steps include conversations that build inferential language and *explicitly* teaching narrative language. Evidence rating for Rec 1 not seen in search results. (state summary, not the guide itself) — [WWC Practice Guide 21](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/PracticeGuide/21); [Nebraska practice guide summaries](https://www.education.ne.gov/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Intro-Nebraska-Practice-Guide-Summaries.pdf)

### Inferences
- The Quinn findings fit the teacher's kindergarten session: the oral sentence is the plan, and the written sentence drifts from it when transcription is weak. Supporting spelling (her ~75%) is what lets the written formula match the spoken one. A simple check: did the written sentence keep every word card the child said?
- The safest "oral-first" design is not oral practice in isolation but a tight loop in one sitting: say the sentence (with symbols/cards), touch-count the words, write it (shared pen or supported spelling), reread it aloud against the cards. That loop combines rehearsal, interactive writing and LEA-style rereading, which is where the transfer evidence points.
- Interactive Writing's null result vs Writing Workshop in kindergarten suggests the shared pen alone is not a strong lever; the explicit sentence target (her formulas) is what should carry the teaching.

### Gaps
- No controlled trial was found that isolates "say it, then write it" with kindergartners or with disabled/neurodivergent young writers.
- Myhill's secondary-school grammar-in-context and Talk for Writing evidence were not retrieved in this pass (earlier project research covered Talk for Writing as pilot-only).
- Sentence repetition/imitation as a *teaching* method for writing: no study measuring transfer to written sentences was found (see b).
- Exact sample, design and author/year of the 245-child Quinn study could not be confirmed.

## (b) Oral narrative and syntax interventions with visual supports; teaching sentence structure to kindergarten and early learners

### Takeaway
Narrative intervention with visual story-grammar icons (Story Champs and similar) has moderate pooled effects on oral narrative (d about 0.5 in group designs), but transfer to writing is small and inconsistent unless written practice is added. For complex syntax, scaffolded methods (recasts, expansions, cloze) beat direct imitation in young children, and for grammar targets an explicit cueing procedure that makes the child produce the correct form beat recasting alone. Explicit sentence-writing instruction with word banks and planning steps works in single-case pilots with students with LD, and researchers say early-grade sentence construction is a recognized research gap.

### Cited findings
- 2021 LSHSS systematic review/meta-analysis of narrative language interventions for school-age children (26 studies): pooled d = 0.51 and 0.54 for group designs and d = 1.24 for single-case designs, with production and comprehension analyzed separately. (abstract via search summary, not opened) — [LSHSS 2021](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2021_LSHSS-20-00160); [PubMed 34352185](https://www.pubmed.gov/34352185/)
- Campbell systematic review (Donolato et al., 2023) of oral language interventions for children with neurodevelopmental disorders (42 publications): mean d = 0.27 at post-test and 0.18 at follow-up, with publication bias and likely overestimation flagged. (search summary, not opened) — [Campbell Systematic Reviews 2023 (UCL)](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10184356/1/Campbell%20Systematic%20Reviews%20-%202023%20-%20Donolato%20-%20Oral%20language%20interventions%20can%20improve%20language%20outcomes%20in%20children%20with.pdf)
- A 2025 meta-analysis of oral language comprehension interventions found researcher-made measures show larger effects than generalized measures. (search summary) — [UCC repository](https://cora.ucc.ie/bitstreams/d8c8369f-a1bb-4ed3-9442-972f44ba33c6/download)
- Spencer & Petersen (2018), Story Champs, first grade: 7 children, baseline/intervention/maintenance design; preliminary results suggested the step from oral story grammar to written narrative "may be smaller than once thought." A 2020 whole-class replication had limited written-language results and concluded combining oral and written narrative instruction may be needed. A kindergarten RCT reported treatment students scored significantly higher on all outcome measures (outcomes not specified in the summary). (search summaries, not opened) — [Davison SRCLD 2020 poster (replication)](https://uwo.ca/fhs/lwm/files/srcld_poster/Davison_SRCLD2020.pdf); [WWC study record 94850](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Study/94850); [LSHSS 2021-20-00162](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2021_LSHSS-20-00162); [AJSLP 2023](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/abs/10.1044/2023_AJSLP-23-00004)
- Systematic review of complex syntax interventions for 4- to 8-year-olds with language impairment: positive effects; recasts, expansions and cloze procedures worked better than direct imitation, within conversation-based and narrative-based approaches. (ASHA Evidence Map summary, not opened) — [ASHA Evidence Map: Complex Syntax Interventions for Young Children With Language Impairments](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/97f52286-4d3e-46bd-a21b-12455934bf9a)
- Intensive complex-syntax recast therapy for ages 4 to 7 with DLD has early efficacy data, measured in spoken language only. (search summary) — [AJSLP 2020](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/pdf/10.1044/2020_AJSLP-19-00090)
- School-age sentence complexity programs combine priming, modeling, recasting, contextualization, metalinguistic instruction and sentence combining. (search summary of a review) — [PMC11001191](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11001191/)
- 2015 LSHSS trial (31 five-year-olds with SLI, eight small-group classroom sessions): a cueing procedure that elicits a correct production after an error beat recasting (no production required); medium-large effect for cueing vs negligible for recasting; no group difference in maintenance 8 weeks later. (abstract via search summary, not opened) — [LSHSS 2015](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2015_LSHSS-14-0041)
- Allen & Datchuk (2026, LSHSS) single-case pilot: one fifth grader with LD, 10 daily lessons on complete sentences with adjectives describing pictures; supports were planning steps, a self-checklist and word banks; large effects on use of sentence elements (noun, verb, adjective), length, accuracy and complexity; language and self-regulation supports increased consistency. (abstract via search summary) — [LSHSS 2026](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2026_LSHSS-25-00149); [Iowa repository](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Sentence-Composition-Instruction-With-Language-Supports/9985218543802771)
- Datchuk-group 2024 *Teaching Exceptional Children* article "Five Steps to Teach Simple Sentence" describes explicit instruction plus fluency practice to improve simple sentence writing fluency for students with LD. (listing only) — [Iowa repository](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Five-Steps-to-Teach-Simple-Sentence/9984371292202771)
- A 2026 Springer pilot taught explicit sentence writing to one first grader with LD (length, accuracy, complexity) and states "there still remains a gap in the research literature on sentence construction in early elementary grades." (search summary) — [Early Childhood Education Journal 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10643-026-02207-7)
- An IES award is funding development of a sentence-writing intervention for young struggling writers, noting K-3 studies have focused mainly on single-word spelling and narrative story writing. — [IES award](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/developing-sentence-writing-intervention-young-struggling-writers)
- Sentence-combining trial with 71 struggling writers aged 7 to 10: sentence-combining group improved on the sentence-combining measure relative to a spelling intervention and a waitlist. (search summary) — [Reading and Writing 2021](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11145-021-10135-8)

### Inferences
- The kindergarten word-card play is ahead of the research base: there is no kindergarten trial of explicit sentence construction with word cards, so the teacher's own probes (sentence elements used, words kept, formula matched) will be the main evidence. The Allen & Datchuk measures (sentence elements, correct word sequences, incorrect word sequences) are a ready-made, published way to score it.
- Because "make the child produce the correct form" beat recasting, formula practice should end with the child saying (and building) the full correct sentence, not just hearing the adult's corrected version.
- Story Champs-style icons help oral narrative but need an explicit bridge to writing; symbol formulas could be that bridge at the sentence level.

### Gaps
- No study found that combines symbol or color coding with kindergarten *written* sentence outcomes.
- No study found that measures whether sentence imitation or recasting gains carry over to written sentences.
- Favot et al. 2021 was named in the brief; the 2021 LSHSS review found may or may not be that paper (authors not shown in the summary).

## (c) Teaching past, present and future tense explicitly to young children and children with DLD

### Takeaway
The strongest evidence is SHAPE CODING with a systematic cueing hierarchy for regular past tense in 5- and 6-year-olds with DLD: a crossover RCT found a very large effect on taught past tense marking, maintained 5 weeks, but no transfer to standardized grammar tests. The system uses arrows to show tense. No study testing time cards, clocks or a timeline alone for preschool tense production was found; one time-as-space movement study helped mainly future-tense comprehension.

### Cited findings
- Calder, Claessen, Ebbels & Leitão (2020/2021, JSLHR) crossover RCT: 21 children with DLD aged 5;9 to 6;9 (intervention n = 10, waitlist n = 11); once-weekly sessions over 10 weeks using SHAPE CODING plus a systematic cueing hierarchy for past tense; significant Time x Group interaction (p < .001), d = 3.03 in the ASHA abstract (a UCL preprint snippet reportedly gives d = 1.22; not reconciled); gains maintained 5 weeks; **no significant differences on standardized grammar measures or extension/control measures.** Conflict of interest: Ebbels's employer (Moor House School & College) sells SHAPE CODING courses and resources. (abstract via search summary, full text blocked) — [JSLHR 2020](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2020_JSLHR-20-00132); [figshare](https://asha.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Explicit_intervention_for_ed_in_children_with_DLD_Calder_et_al_2020_/13345202); [UCL preprint](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10128234/1/Calder%20et%20al.%20%282021%29_GREEN.pdf)
- SHAPE CODING uses specific visual cues within sentences, and arrows depict tense. (search summary) — [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/428359696.pdf)
- Calder et al. (2020, LSHSS) single-case study, 9 children: improved past tense on trained verbs (8 of 9) and untrained verbs (7 of 9); only 5 of 9 improved on grammaticality judgment or receptive measures. (search summary) — [LSHSS 2019-19-00060](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/abs/10.1044/2019_LSHSS-19-00060)
- Earlier single-case work: all 18 children with DLD aged 4 to 10 improved regular past tense. (search summary) — [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/428359696.pdf)
- A dose study found all past tense allomorphs (/t/, /d/, /id/) improved similarly, and suggested twice-weekly may be more effective. (search summary) — [Calder et al. 2021 IJLCD (UCL)](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10176535/1/Calder%20et%20al.%20%282021%29%20IJLCD_GREEN.pdf)
- Ebbels et al. (2024, LSHSS) studied individualized SHAPE CODING targets and dosage. (listing only) — [UCL accepted manuscript](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10193044/1/2024%20Ebbels%20et%20al%20targets%20and%20dosage%20accepted.pdf)
- A source notes limited evidence for this approach with younger school-aged children aged 6 to 7. (search summary) — [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/195692915.pdf)
- A grade-two study using coloured visual prompts for verb tense with children with oral language difficulties did not support its hypothesis, though it reinforced that explicit, repetitive teaching helps. (search summary, Melbourne student project, low rigor) — [F Lai project, University of Melbourne](https://students.education.unimelb.edu.au/LiteracyResearch/pub/Projects/F_Lai.pdf)
- An embodied time-as-space intervention for preschoolers with DLD showed significant posttest differences mainly in future-tense semantics "with forward motion," not past-tense production. (search summary) — [MDPI Journal of Intelligence 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-3200/13/6/61)

### Inferences
- Clock or time cards are a reasonable way to show *when* (meaning), but the evidence says the morpheme (the -ed ending, will + verb) needs its own explicit visual on the verb itself, plus cued production. Pair the time card with a tense mark on the verb card (her tense symbols), as SHAPE CODING pairs arrows with the verb.
- Expect taught-item gains first and slow generalization. Probe untrained verbs and spontaneous sentences separately, and plan repeated cycles rather than one unit.
- Start with regular past (-ed) on verbs the child already knows, since all allomorphs responded similarly; irregular past and future can follow as separate formula variants.

### Gaps
- No study found testing time cards, clocks or a past/present/future timeline alone for tense production in kindergarten.
- No study found linking explicit tense teaching to written sentences in young children.
- The d = 3.03 vs 1.22 discrepancy for Calder et al. could not be resolved without the full text.

## (d) Supported spelling during composing: how much support

### Takeaway
Guided invented spelling (child tries, adult gives developmentally appropriate feedback a step above the attempt) helped at-risk kindergartners learn to read more words than phoneme segmentation or storybook reading in a small RCT, and invented spelling in kindergarten uniquely predicts later reading. The research supports feedback on the child's own attempt rather than either leaving spelling alone or simply giving the correct spelling every time. No study gives a target percentage of support.

### Cited findings
- Sénéchal, Ouellette, Pagan & Lever (2011/2012, *Reading and Writing* 25, 917-934): 56 kindergartners with weak phoneme awareness assigned to invented spelling, phoneme segmentation or storybook reading training; invented-spelling children learned to read more words; both invented-spelling and segmentation groups gained more phoneme awareness than storybook. Abstract: with guidance and developmentally appropriate feedback, invented spelling lets children explore the relation between oral language and written symbols. (search summary, not opened) — [Springer full text page](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-011-9310-2/fulltext.html); [WWC study record 82934](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Study/82934)
- Longitudinal study (~170 children, K to grade 1): invented spelling was a unique predictor of growth in early reading beyond alphabet knowledge and phonological awareness. (press summary) — [Education Week 2017](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/invented-spelling-leads-to-better-reading-study-says/2017/05); [Reading Rockets summary](https://www.readingrockets.org/resources/resource-library/invented-spelling-kindergarten-predictor-reading-and-spelling-grade-1)
- Ouellette, Sénéchal & Haley (2013), "Guiding children's invented spellings: A gateway into literacy learning," *Journal of Experimental Education* 81, 261-279; summarized by Shanahan as showing appropriate feedback on invented spelling leads to greater progress. Ouellette distinguishes this from whole-language use of invented spelling without explicit phonics. (secondhand) — [Shanahan on Literacy](https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/blog/explicit-spelling-instruction-or-invented-spelling); [Carleton parent booklet](https://carleton.ca/cllr/wp-content/uploads/sites/172/2025/11/parentbooklet2009.pdf)
- Quinn & Rohloff: more sophisticated spelling strategies linked to better connection between oral ideas and written sentences (see section a). (search summary)

### Inferences
- For the formula path, a reasonable rule: the child attempts sounds they know (with Elkonin boxes or letter tiles), the adult gives the next-step feedback, and the adult or a word card supplies words beyond the child's phonics (irregular words, long words). That way the formula (the target) is never lost to spelling, which is the Quinn concern.
- Her ~75% support is not wrong by any study found, but the research would push toward recording *which* words were supported and fading support word by word as phonics grows, rather than holding a fixed percentage.

### Gaps
- No study found that sets an optimal amount or percentage of spelling support during sentence composing.
- No study found on supported spelling specifically for disabled or neurodivergent kindergartners while composing sentences.
