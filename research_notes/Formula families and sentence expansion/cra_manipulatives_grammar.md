## 1. CRA evidence base (mainly math, students with disabilities): reviews, sequence, fading

### Takeaway
CRA (concrete, then representational, then abstract) is treated as an evidence-based practice for students with learning disabilities in math, built on multiple single-case and group studies. Its theory traces to Bruner's enactive, iconic, symbolic stages, and its core design move is to fade the concrete and drawn supports until students work with symbols alone. Nothing in the CRA research tests grammar or writing.

### Cited Findings
- Bouck, Satsangi & Park (2018), "The concrete–representational–abstract approach for students with learning disabilities: An evidence-based practice synthesis," *Remedial and Special Education*, 39(4), 211–228. The search returned the citation only, not its findings (search summary, not opened). — [Scribd copy listing](https://www.scribd.com/document/542315745/0741932517721712); [UKY scholars listing](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/a-meta-analytic-review-of-the-concrete-representational-abstract-/)
- A 2025 meta-analytic review of CRA in math included 30 single-case design studies. It reported an overall Tau-BC effect of 0.9965, and a meta-regression found that using a non-integrated (separate-phase) CRA approach changed the effect size by only 0.011, a negligible amount. Authors were not shown in the summary, so do not attribute it to Bouck or Peltier (search summary, not opened). — [UKY scholars listing](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/a-meta-analytic-review-of-the-concrete-representational-abstract-/)
- Peltier and colleagues wrote a meta-analysis of single-case research on math manipulatives with students at risk for or identified with a disability. Between-case standardized mean differences for individual studies ranged from 0.03 to 18.58, so effects varied widely (search summary, not opened). Bouck & Park also published a 2019 single-case meta-analysis on manipulatives in the *Journal of Special Education* (search summary, not opened). — [SAGE figshare collection](https://sage.figshare.com/collections/A_Meta-Analysis_of_Single-Case_Research_Using_Mathematics_Manipulatives_With_Students_At_Risk_or_Identified_With_a_Disability/4517738)
- Witzel, Mercer & Miller (2003), "Teaching Algebra to Students with Learning Difficulties," *Learning Disabilities Research & Practice* 18(2), 121–131. They selected 34 matched pairs of 6th and 7th graders from 358 participants. The explicit-CRA group outscored traditional instruction on the posttest and at follow-up for solving algebra transformation equations, and made fewer procedural errors (search summary, not opened; abstract only). — [Ovid abstract](https://www.ovid.com/journals/ldrap/abstract/00012204-200301820-00011~teaching-algebra-to-students-with-learning-difficulties-an)
- Fading: CRA is described as "teaching algorithmic procedures using manipulatives and drawings, and by eventually fading these aids until students work with symbols." That description comes from Flores, Hinton & Schweck (2014), not from the 2003 paper (search summary, not opened). — [Flores, Hinton & Schweck 2014 PDF](https://simvilledev.ku.edu/sites/default/files/Video%20Resources/Flores_Hinton_Schweck_2014.pdf)
- CRA rests on Bruner's enactive, iconic and symbolic stages. Miller, Mercer and Witzel's work is cited as showing that CRA improves conceptual and procedural understanding for students with LD (secondary study guide, search summary, not opened; low authority). — [open-exam-prep study guide](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/praxis-school-psych/academic-interventions/evidence-based-math-writing-interventions)

### Inferences
- The 2025 finding that integrated and non-integrated CRA perform about the same suggests the curriculum can choose either model on practical grounds. In integrated CRA, cube, symbol card and code appear together in one lesson. In non-integrated CRA, the phases are taught in blocks. For many neurodivergent learners, showing all three side by side (cube row, symbol card row, code) may make the mapping explicit. That is a design judgment, not a tested grammar result.
- The math CRA literature transfers as a *design pattern* (explicit modeling, a planned sequence, fading against a mastery criterion), not as evidence that cubes teach grammar. Any claim about "CRA for sentence formulas" should be framed as research-informed rather than research-proven.
- The teacher's UFLI-style phonics routine (explore, connect, see, write, apply, review) already runs from concrete to abstract. A sentence-formula CRA path fits the same daily routine.

### Gaps
- Agrawal & Morin (2016) did not come up in search, so it is unverified and should not be cited until it is located.
- No source gave per-phase mastery criteria, such as how many lessons or what percentage correct before moving from C to R to A. Practitioner lore often cites about three lessons per phase at roughly 80 to 100 percent, but that is background, not verified this session.
- No source covered CRA-I (Strickland & Maccini) in detail.

## 2. CRA and manipulative applications in literacy, grammar and sentence construction (evidence level for each)

### Takeaway
The strongest grammar-specific evidence is for **Shape Coding** (Ebbels; RCT-level for verb argument structure and conjunctions in older students with DLD, mostly from the developer's group). Next is **Colourful Semantics** (small uncontrolled studies, rated "Moderate" by Speech and Language UK). **Elkonin boxes** sit at small-study level for phonemic awareness. **Montessori grammar symbols and boxes** have essentially no direct studies, though Montessori education as a whole has new lottery-study support. Practitioner cube, block and word-tile methods have no research behind them.

### Cited Findings
**Elkonin boxes and sound tiles (phonemic awareness): small-study evidence, consistently positive**
- D.B. Elkonin (1963) had children move markers into boxes to segment the sounds in a word. Letters were added later. The UFLI handout says studies using Elkonin boxes "have consistently found them to be a powerful tool for teaching struggling beginning readers," citing Joseph (2002) and Lane et al. (2009) (search summary, not opened). — [UFLI Using Elkonin Boxes handout](https://ufli.education.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Using-Elkonin-Boxes-Handout.pdf); [UFLI Elkonin Boxes rev](https://education.ufl.edu/ufli/files/2020/03/Elkonin_Boxes_rev.pdf)
- Understood lists a *Remedial and Special Education* study, "Word boxes improve phonemic awareness, letter-sound correspondences, and spelling skills of at-risk kindergartners." McCarthy (2008), "Using sound boxes systematically to develop phonemic awareness," appeared in *The Reading Teacher* (search summary, not opened). — [Understood](https://www.understood.org/en/articles/evidence-based-literacy-strategy-elkonin-sound-boxes)
- Boxes teach students to count phonemes, which is not always the number of letters, and they support phoneme-grapheme mapping. UFLI recommends words with few sounds and regular spellings for beginners (search summary, not opened). — [Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org/classroom/classroom-strategies/elkonin-boxes); [UFLI handout](https://ufli.education.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Using-Elkonin-Boxes-Handout.pdf)
- No meta-analysis of Elkonin boxes specifically was found (search summary). — [Wikipedia: Elkonin boxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elkonin_boxes)

**Montessori grammar symbols and grammar boxes: descriptive and practitioner material, no direct outcome studies found**
- There are nine grammar boxes, covering the parts of speech from article to interjection, and each box builds on the one before (practitioner, search summary, not opened). — [Montessori For Today](https://montessorifortoday.com/?p=4123)
- Each symbol combines shape, colour and size and is tied to a short story that makes the part of speech memorable (AMI, search summary, not opened). — [montessori-ami.org](https://montessori-ami.org/node/9256)
- Lower elementary children usually move through the boxes quickly (practitioner, search summary, not opened). — [Guidepost Montessori](https://www.guidepostmontessori.com/blog/spotlight-grammar-boxes)
- The translator of Montessori's *Elementary Material* described her English grammar material as only approximately experimental and scientific, and said a definitive English version had not yet been built (primary historical text, search summary, not opened). — [Project Gutenberg](https://gutenberg.org/ebooks/42869.epub.noimages)
- Studies of the grammar symbols themselves are scarce. The case for them rests on broader Montessori research (blog, low authority, search summary). — [xiairworld.com](https://xiairworld.com/?p=18726)
- Broader Montessori evidence: a 2025 national lottery study (PNAS, led by Lillard at UVA) followed 588 children in 24 oversubscribed public Montessori schools. At the end of kindergarten, children admitted by lottery showed significantly stronger reading, short-term memory, social understanding and executive function. WWC rated it as meeting standards with reservations because of high attrition (search summary, not opened). — [UVA news](https://as.virginia.edu/news/nationwide-uva-led-study-finds-montessori-preschool-boosts-learning-lower-cost); [WWC study page](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Study/95226)
- Lillard et al. (2017), *Frontiers in Psychology*, was a longitudinal lottery study of two public Montessori magnet schools in a high-poverty city. Later test points favored Montessori children (search summary, not opened). — [Frontiers 10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01783](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01783/pdf)

**Colourful Semantics (colour-coded sentence parts by question word): small, mostly uncontrolled studies; rated "Moderate"**
- Bolderson et al. (2011), *Child Language Teaching and Therapy*: six 5 to 6 year olds had a nine-week no-therapy baseline, then Colourful Semantics twice a week for eight weeks. There was no control group. The authors called the results "encouraging" and said they warrant a larger study (search summary, not opened). — [SAGE 10.1177/0265659011412248](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265659011412248)
- The PenCRU (Exeter) 2018 evidence briefing found no autism-specific Colourful Semantics study and concluded it "may be worth a try" for children with severe language problems (search summary, not opened). — [PenCRU What's the Evidence](https://exeter.ac.uk/media/universityofexeter/medicalschool/subsites/pencru/pdfs/WTE_Colourful_Semantics.pdf)
- Hettiarachchi (2015/2016), Sri Lanka, concluded Colourful Semantics is effective for narrative skills in children with intellectual difficulties. The design and sample size were not confirmed (search summary, not opened). — [Sri Lanka narrative study PDF](https://integratedtreatmentservices.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/Sri-Lanka-Narraitve-Study-2015.pdf)
- The Speech and Language UK "What Works" database rates Colourful Semantics "Evidence Rating: Moderate" (search summary, not opened). — [Speech and Language UK What Works](https://speechandlanguage.org.uk/what-works/)
- An EAL writing practitioner account reported a 27.7% drop in mean grammatical errors over six weeks, with no control group. This is practitioner voice (search summary, not opened). — [NALDIC](https://naldic.org.uk/colourful-semantics/)

**Shape Coding (Ebbels): strongest grammar-specific evidence; physical and visual shapes for phrases, colours for word classes**
- 2007 RCT on verb argument structure: Shape Coding and a semantic therapy both produced significant progress (d > 1.0) that held at follow-up and generalized to untrained verbs. Shape Coding versus controls was d = 1.3. Controls made no progress (search summary, not opened). — [Ebbels Shape Coding (UCL accepted version)](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1516142/1/Ebbels_Ebbels%20Shape%20Coding%20therapy%20(accepted%20version)_segment.pdf); [UCL DLD intervention project](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/pals/projects/research/develang/intervention)
- A 2014 trial with 14 students aged about 11 to 16 found improved comprehension of coordinating conjunctions after eight weekly sessions. A separate adolescent study found that four hours of Shape Coding gave significant gains on the targeted conjunctions in severe receptive language impairment (search summary, not opened). — [Balthazar, Ebbels & Zwitserlood review](https://researchgate.net/profile/Rob-Zwitserlood/publication/340512173_Explicit_Grammatical_Intervention_for_Developmental_Language_Disorder_Three_Approaches/links/63bc0f8203aad5368e78baca/Explicit-Grammatical-Intervention-for-Developmental-Language-Disorder-Three-Approaches.pdf)
- For 6 to 7 year olds, an Australian past-tense study notes "limited evidence" for younger children. Calder et al. (2021) disclose that Ebbels's employer sells Shape Coding courses and resources, so a conflict of interest applies (search summary, not opened). — [Calder et al. 2021](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10128234/1/Calder%20et%20al.%20%282021%29_GREEN.pdf)
- A 2024 dosage study suggests aiming for high teaching-episode rates per session, especially when the number of sessions is limited (search summary, not opened). — [Ebbels et al. 2024 targets and dosage](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10193044/1/2024%20Ebbels%20et%20al%20targets%20and%20dosage%20accepted.pdf)
- Ebbels's visual templates for tense and plural agreement in copula and auxiliary structures also have an effectiveness study (title only, search summary). — [Ebbels visual templates AAM](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10063226/1/Ebbels_Effectiveness%20of%20intervention%20with%20visual%20templates%20targeting%20tense%20and%20plural%20agreement%20in%20copula%20and%20auxiliary%20structures%20in%20school-aged%20children_AAM.pdf)
- The amount of evidence for grammar intervention "varies considerably depending upon language, age, and targets" (review, search summary, not opened). — [Balthazar, Ebbels & Zwitserlood](https://researchgate.net/profile/Rob-Zwitserlood/publication/340512173_Explicit_Grammatical_Intervention_for_Developmental_Language_Disorder_Three_Approaches/links/63bc0f8203aad5368e78baca/Explicit-Grammatical-Intervention-for-Developmental-Language-Disorder-Three-Approaches.pdf)

**Sentence cubes, linking cubes, word bricks, sentence strips and tiles: practitioner and commercial only, no outcome research found**
- Sentence Cubes product: nine dice, each colour-coded by part of speech, rolled to make random sentences for word-order practice (vendor, search summary). — [Rainbow Resource](https://www.rainbowresource.com/020673.html)
- A "Sentence building linking cubes instant center" kit claims to target subject-predicate agreement, parts of speech, punctuation and sentence structure (catalog listing, search summary). — [WorldCat](https://search.worldcat.org/title/Sentence-building-linking-cubes-instant-center/oclc/683256451)
- Roll-a-sentence uses cubes labeled Who? / Did What? / When? / Where?, with paper piles as a cube-free version and the results recorded in a notebook. This is a blog, practitioner voice (search summary). — [This Reading Mama](https://thisreadingmama.com/writing-sentences-activity/)
- The US State Department's "Word Bricks" ESL resource uses brick-shaped word pieces for a longest-sentence game and sentence races. This is a government ESL resource, practitioner level (search summary). — [American English: Word Bricks](https://americanenglish.state.gov/resources/word-bricks-longest-sentence); [About Word Bricks PDF](https://americanenglish.state.gov/files/ae/resource_files/about_word_bricks.pdf)
- Super Duper Handy Handouts suggests sentence-building activities from SLP practice (search summary). — [Handy Handouts #339](https://handyhandouts.com/Handout/339/sentence-building-activities)

### Inferences
- Evidence ranking for the curriculum's purposes: Shape Coding (RCT, older DLD students, developer-led) > Colourful Semantics (small, Moderate) ≈ Elkonin boxes (small, consistent, phonemic level) > Montessori grammar symbols (descriptive) > cube and brick practitioner methods (none).
- Shape Coding is the closest research analogue to Symbolic Grammar. It uses shapes for phrases, colours for word classes, and its strongest results are on argument structure, the "who did what to whom" skeleton. That suggests the first cube formulas should target the core skeleton (noun + verb, noun + verb + noun) before expansions.
- Elkonin boxes are a ready-made literacy CRA in their own right: token, then letter tile, then written letter. The teacher's students may already know this routine from UFLI, so "a box per word" and "a cube per word" can be introduced as the same move one level up (sound → word).
- Montessori's sequence (objects and actions, then symbols placed over words, then sentence analysis) is a CRA path in practice even without outcome data.

### Gaps
- No study was found that tests snap cubes, LEGO or blocks for sentence building specifically.
- No independent (non-Ebbels) RCT of Shape Coding was found in the results.
- The Montessori grammar materials have no isolated outcome data.

## 3. Embodied cognition and gesture in grammar learning

### Takeaway
Glenberg's Moved by Reading gives the clearest evidence that physically acting out sentences, then *imagining* that action, improves comprehension. That is a concrete → imagined fade, a CRA analogue. Gesture research on grammar is small but suggestive. No study tests gestures for parts of speech with school-age children.

### Cited Findings
- Moved by Reading: children read a sentence and then manipulate toys to simulate it. Later the toys are removed and children imagine manipulating them while reading new stories. Glenberg reports that both physical and imagined manipulation produce large gains in memory and comprehension, and that imagined manipulation beat rereading (Glenberg et al., 2004; Glenberg, Goldberg & Zhu, 2009) (search summary, not opened). — [Glenberg, Improving reading (PDF)](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~jerryzhu/pub/Improvingreading.pdf); [PMC article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5249083); [IES grant record](https://ies.ed.gov/funding/grantsearch/details.asp?ID=81)
- Students who acted out sentences had better comprehension than controls and drew better inferences (search summary of an EdWeek report, not opened). — [EdWeek 2011](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/studies-find-students-learn-more-by-acting-out-text/2011/07?view=register)
- Limitation: a 2019 study in Spanish (a transparent orthography) found the method improves comprehension but does not always transfer to new texts read without manipulation (search summary, not opened). — [British Library record: Embodied reading in a transparent orthography (2019)](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100102193669_0x000001)
- A 2025 Malaysian study adapted the method with Playmobil for primary ESL pupils reading short stories (search summary, not opened). — [LALEJ article](https://jml.um.edu.my/index.php/LALEJ/article/download/59287/18356/171197)
- Gesture for grammatical morphemes: a study of 19 children in middle childhood (Janzen Ulbricht, FU Berlin) taught English plural -s and third-person -s with gestures. Response times fell after instruction in the two-gesture condition (p = .039). It is preliminary, with a small sample (search summary, not opened). — [PMC9891517](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9891517/); [FU Berlin dissertation](https://refubium.fu-berlin.de/bitstream/fub188/45224/4/Dissertation_Natasha%20Janzen%20Ulbricht.pdf)
- Aussems & Kita (2020): 3 to 4 year olds who saw iconic *manner* gestures generalized new verbs to same-manner events more than children who saw end-state gestures or no gestures (search summary, not opened). — [WRAP Aussems 2020](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/129055/15/WRAP-Seeing-iconic-gesture-promotes-first-second-order-verb-Aussems-2020.pdf)
- In hearing children, gesture precedes and predicts the acquisition of structures in speech (Goldin-Meadow line of work, review, search summary, not opened). — [PMC4123674](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4123674)
- In adults, a gesture that does not match the action described in speech interferes with learning (search summary, not opened). — [Frontiers in Communication 2024](https://frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2024.1372033/pdf)

### Inferences
- Moved by Reading's physical → imagined fade is a direct model for a sentence-formula CRA: build the cube train, then "build it in your head" (or with a finger-tap per word), then write the code.
- Gestures for each symbol should be *congruent* with the word's job: a pushing or moving hand for a verb, a pointing or holding hand for a noun. The adult incongruence finding warns against arbitrary hand signs.
- Gesture is also a no-materials, sensory-friendly option for students who can't or won't handle cubes.

### Gaps
- No study was found on gestures for parts of speech (noun, verb, adjective) with school-age children.
- Effect sizes for Moved by Reading were not available in the summaries.

## 4. Practical design: cubes per word vs per phrase, phrases as joined units, object → symbol → code transitions, OT considerations

### Takeaway
No research specifies the cube-to-word ratio. The closest research models are Shape Coding (phrases enclosed in shapes, word classes in colour) and Elkonin boxes (one box per unit, then letters replace tokens), and both point to a layered design: one cube per word, coloured by part of speech, with phrases shown as a joined, enclosed unit. For safety, any cube or tile that fits in the CPSC small-parts cylinder is a choking hazard for children under 3 or for students who mouth objects.

### Cited Findings
- Shape Coding encloses phrases in shapes and marks word classes with colour (that is how the method is described in the review literature, search summary, not opened). The exact shape-to-phrase mapping (for example, noun phrase oval, verb phrase hexagon) could not be confirmed this session because the UCL paper was blocked. — [Balthazar, Ebbels & Zwitserlood](https://researchgate.net/profile/Rob-Zwitserlood/publication/340512173_Explicit_Grammatical_Intervention_for_Developmental_Language_Disorder_Three_Approaches/links/63bc0f8203aad5368e78baca/Explicit-Grammatical-Intervention-for-Developmental-Language-Disorder-Three-Approaches.pdf)
- Elkonin progression: markers in boxes first, letters added later, with few-sound regular words for beginners (search summary, not opened). — [UFLI handout](https://ufli.education.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Using-Elkonin-Boxes-Handout.pdf)
- CRA fades manipulatives and drawings "until students work with symbols" (search summary, not opened). — [Flores, Hinton & Schweck 2014](https://simvilledev.ku.edu/sites/default/files/Video%20Resources/Flores_Hinton_Schweck_2014.pdf)
- Integrated and non-integrated CRA showed a negligible difference in the 2025 meta-analysis (search summary, not opened). — [UKY scholars listing](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/a-meta-analytic-review-of-the-concrete-representational-abstract-/)
- Small parts rule: an object that fits completely into the small parts test cylinder (16 CFR 1501.4) is banned for children under 3. The cylinder approximates the fully expanded throat of a child under three. Its diameter is 1.25 in (31.7 mm), and its depth ranges from 1 to 2.25 in (search summary, not opened). — [CPSC Small Parts guidance](https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Business-Education/Business-Guidance/Small-Parts-For-Toys-And-Childrens-Products); [eCFR 16 CFR 1501.4](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-II/subchapter-C/part-1501/section-1501.4); [1995 Federal Register notice](https://govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1995-02-27/pdf/95-4484.pdf)
- Parent-facing rule of thumb: anything that fits through a toilet-roll tube is too small for a child who still mouths objects. Choose the largest safe size and pack materials away after use (parent guide, search summary, not opened). — [Pinnacle Blooms](https://pinnacleblooms.org/ask/how-can-i-work-on-small-object-manipulation-with-my-child-at-home)
- Larger loose parts reduce the risk (practitioner, search summary). Pincer grasp is only one of several fine motor actions children need, so tasks should be graded (search summary, not opened). — [Twinkl blog](https://www.twinkl.com/blog/the-challenges-of-loose-parts-play); [Edutopia: supporting fine motor difficulties](https://www.edutopia.org/article/supporting-students-fine-motor-difficulties)
- No authoritative school OT guideline giving manipulative dimensions by age was found (search summary). — [Edutopia: fine motor in preschool](https://www.edutopia.org/article/promoting-fine-motor-skills-preschool)

### Inferences (design proposals for the curriculum, not research findings)
- **Cubes per word.** Use one cube per word, matching the Symbolic Grammar symbol colour for the part of speech. This mirrors Elkonin's one-token-per-unit rule and keeps the count honest: the cube count equals the word count. Words the curriculum already treats as one unit (compound nouns, word groups that take one symbol) get one cube, which fits the existing word-group decision.
- **Phrases as joined units.** Snap the words of a prepositional phrase together into a rigid "stick" that moves as one piece. Optionally add a sleeve, band or tray (a physical "shape", as in Shape Coding's enclosed phrases) in the preposition's colour. Students can slide the whole stick to a new position ("after the noun" vs "at the start") to show phrase movement without breaking it. The same move works for adjective + noun as a noun-phrase stick.
- **Formula code on the train.** At the concrete stage, a card under the cube train shows the code (for example N-V-P-N). This is the integrated-CRA option and costs nothing, since integrated vs separate phases made no measurable difference.
- **Representational stage.** Symbol cards laid over the cubes, then cards alone, then students drawing the symbols above a written sentence (Montessori-style symbol placement).
- **Abstract stage.** The formula code alone, then a written sentence produced from the code, then the code read off a written sentence. Moved by Reading suggests an "imagined" middle step: tap a finger per word or "build it in your head" before writing.
- **Fading criterion.** Borrow the CRA habit of moving on only after consistent accuracy at a level. The exact criterion is unverified this session, so set it locally (for example, several correct independent builds across separate days) and record it as a curriculum decision, not a research figure.
- **Daily cumulative review.** Rebuild yesterday's formulas with cubes or cards at the start of each lesson, matching her UFLI-style routine.
- **OT and sensory.** Use cubes larger than the small parts cylinder (wider than 1.25 in) for any student who mouths objects. Offer alternatives for limited fine motor control: larger blocks, Velcro or magnetic tiles on a board, a tray with fixed slots (Elkonin-style boxes), digital drag tiles, or gesture. Consider sound (snap cubes click, and some students seek or avoid that), texture and colour vision. Pair colour with shape, as Montessori and Shape Coding both do, so colour-blind students are not disadvantaged. This is a design inference; confirm with the school OT.

### Gaps
- No source compared per-word and per-phrase representations for sentence building.
- No research was found on snap cubes or LEGO for grammar specifically.
- Shape Coding's exact shape inventory (which shape marks which phrase type) was not verified because the source was blocked.
- No school OT or AOTA guidance on manipulative size and sensory features was found. A consult with the school OT is recommended.
